Serving economics — is running your own actually cheaper?

The half of the decision the model-quality debate ignores: even if a small model is good enough, does self-hosting it on our cluster actually beat paying per API token? Decided in the serving stack, not the leaderboard.

Two arrivals, and between them the cheapest thing on this map — the quantization that makes self-hosting affordable at all — acquires a defect class nobody here was measuring, plus a third item that prices harness search. Compression takes proportionally more of what the model knew best. Three models × 11 compression methods (GPTQ, AWQ, OmniQuant, AQLM, magnitude, WANDA, SparseGPT, N:M variants, ShortGPT, layer dropping) on knowledge stratified by fact popularity. In absolute terms head knowledge stays the most accurate band. Normalize each band to its own base-model accuracy and the ordering inverts: tail retention runs +22.7 to +39.5 pp above overall under N:M pruning while head runs −3.6 to −14.4 pp below (compression-costs-head-knowledge-proportionally-more-than-tail). This does not refute quantization-erases-long-tail-knowledge-invisibly, which measured absolute tier accuracy — it corrects how it gets applied. The tail was already the fragile margin, so tail damage is a small absolute number; head damage is proportionally larger and lands on the facts a workflow actually uses. And at settings this map has treated as free, a fifth to a third of the correct answers are different answers. At 4-bit GPTQ, 30% WANDA and 5% ShortGPT — "where standard metrics suggest only minimal degradation" — 22% to 35% of previously correct answers become incorrect (85–99% at the strongest settings) (a-fifth-to-a-third-of-correct-answers-flip-where-metrics-look-clean). Aggregate accuracy barely moves because roughly as many flip back. The identity of the correct set changes; the count does not. Two consequences. Any A/B on this map that quantized between arms compared two different models, so precision joins run 26's condition list. The KV-cache half is genuinely new here and it interlocks with run 7's hardware-fit finding: that one was about squeezing weights into one node's memory, this is the cache competing for the same budget, and halving it roughly doubles resident context — the exact constraint long agentic loops hit. Two independent knobs, both converting precision into batch size. From the measurement side, the same-week result that makes the operator's quality number unsafe to reuse. Across 58 quantizations of Qwen3.6 27B against a 1,400-question probe stratified into 7 obscurity tiers, factual knowledge degrades non-linearly — flat above ~5-bit, dropping at 3-bit, sharp at 2-bit — while the same author's same ladder measured on AIME-120 and Terminal-Bench 2.1 showed no cliff above 4-bit at all (quantization-erases-long-tail-knowledge-invisibly). Reasoning survives compression; long-tail recall does not; and "within 0.8 points across benchmarks" is measured on exactly the probes that cannot see it. These two do not cancel — they compose into the theme's most actionable pairing yet: quantization buys ~30% per token and the bill is charged to factual recall, in a currency your benchmarks do not report. The payoff is a cheap instrument: knowledge loss tracks KL divergence from the BF16 original at r = −0.981, so a candidate quantization can be screened against a sample of our own traffic without running any knowledge benchmark. Status unchanged, for the same reason as the last two runs: this sharpens how cheap and at what hidden cost, and touches neither of the two numbers that would settle the theme — our real utilization (Cloudflare states none) and our marginal engineer cost. Discount both sources as vendor-adjacent: a commercially interested operator blog with an unnamed eval suite on one side, a single self-published author on one model with no replication on the other.

  • 2026-08-03 (run 8) — Stays contested; a fourth cost lever enters, and it sits one layer below the three already here — the software footprint. Every priced analysis in this theme prices GPU-hours, utilization, hardware fit; none prices the deployment stack, which is what actually decides whether a small model runs on constrained or heterogeneous hardware. A custom C++ inference engine reportedly matches vLLM throughput within ~1% at a 66 MiB binary versus a 9.1 GiB Python virtualenv, with companion ports cutting memory ~5× (inference-engine-footprint-is-a-self-hosting-lever). The throughput-parity claim is what makes it load-bearing — it removes the usual objection that a smaller engine costs tokens/sec — and it lands on the cheap-small-model self-hosting case specifically, the same case the compiled-model economics already favor. Discount for source: a vendor blog (LocalAI) with its own benchmarks and an obvious interest, so the exact ratios are illustrative; the throughput-parity-at-tiny-footprint claim is falsifiable and is the one to carry. Doesn't move status — it sharpens how cheap self-hosting a small model can get without touching the two numbers that would settle the theme (our real utilization, our marginal engineer cost).

  • 2026-08-02 (run 7) — Stays contested; a third variable enters, and it interacts with the two already here. The theme had split its dispute by which model you host; this adds which accelerator you host it on. Serving the 2.8T open-weight Kimi K3 in TP8, MI355X measured 952 tok/s/node against a B300 delivering ~1.65× more aggregate throughput — but at $2.50/GPU-hour versus $6.00, the slower part wins the economics: 48 tokens/sec per dollar-of-GPU-hour versus 33, roughly a 45% advantage (accelerator-memory-capacity-swings-self-hosting-economics). The mechanism is capacity, not compute: 288 GB VRAM lets the checkpoint fit in one node, where the comparison part needs multi-node, so the cross-node communication disappears. That makes it a threshold effect rather than a vendor result — a model 20% larger, or a long-context configuration with a bigger KV cache, pushes you back multi-node and plausibly inverts it. Planning consequence: match accelerator memory to checkpoint size and the cross-node overhead becomes margin; carry an implicit NVIDIA assumption into a cost-per-token estimate and it can be wrong by ~45% before utilization is even considered. Discount it accordingly — a single vendor-adjacent blog post with a commercial interest, author-chosen list prices (the TCO term most sensitive to contract and region), and one fixed 1,024-in/400-out shape that flatters aggregate throughput relative to long-context agentic traffic. The ratio is informative, the absolute is marketing. Status unchanged because this sharpens our estimate without touching the two numbers that would actually settle it — our real utilization and our marginal engineer cost.

  • 2026-08-01 (run 5) — Moved emerging → contested, and the dispute turns out to be about which model you host, not about self-hosting. For: compiling a procedure into a 3–8B model cut per-conversation cost 128–462× vs frontier in-context, with the advantage growing with procedure complexity because the compiled prompt is constant-size while an orchestrated one scales with the node graph (compilation-cost-advantage-grows-with-procedure-complexity). Against: a full four-term TCO model puts the break-even for frontier-class open weights at ~600M tok/mo (code) or ~1.2B (chat), and only with a dedicated inference engineer — below that the API wins by up to 30× (self-hosting-savings-erased-below-engineer-scale-thresholds, which rebuts the unqualified form of self-hosted-serving-throughput-cuts-cost). Our own threshold is plausibly lower than the published one — the analysis assumes hiring an engineer, and we already staff a cluster — which makes this the one theme where the decisive number is ours to compute rather than to find.
