The answer splits by axis

The question worth asking isn't "small model or frontier model." It's "an open or small model plus a purpose-built harness, versus a frontier model plus its default SDK, on the same work."

For a year that comparison had one published answer, on one narrow class of work, and it was a clean split: the pairing won on cost by two orders of magnitude and lost on quality by single digits. That answer still holds where it was measured. But the comparison has now been run on open-ended work, with the best scaffold anyone has built, and the result is less flattering. The split isn't between cost and quality any more. It's between two kinds of task.

The original head-to-head

Compiling Agentic Workflows into LLM Weights (arXiv 2605.22502) runs the comparison directly. It takes three task-oriented dialogue procedures of rising complexity — travel booking (14 nodes), Zoom support (14 nodes plus product knowledge), and insurance claims (55 nodes, 2,381 paths) — and races three configurations on each: a small model with the procedure fine-tuned into its weights, the same procedure driven by a LangGraph orchestrator over Claude Sonnet 4.5, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 handed the procedure in context. Two hundred conversations per condition, scored 1–5, replicated under a second judge. The compiled model was Qwen2.5-3B on the simplest procedure and Qwen3-8B on the other two.

Against the orchestrated frontier, the compiled small model won graceful handling 4.81 to 4.38 and naturalness 4.92 to 4.58 on the hardest procedure (both p<0.001), and cut failure rates from 24% to 5.5% on travel and 17% to 9% on insurance. Against frontier in-context it reached 87–98% of quality on the two larger procedures. Per conversation it cost $0.0007 versus $0.327.

The detail that makes this trustworthy is the ablation. The compiled 3B was also raced against the same 3B base model under the same explicit orchestration, and beat it on all five criteria. That isolates the compilation from the model size, a control vendor benchmarks varying model and harness together can't offer.

Read one axis at a time, it said three things. On cost the pairing won, and won harder as work got harder: 128× on the simplest procedure, 462× on the hardest, because a compiled model's prompt is a fixed size while an orchestrated prompt grows with every branch it carries. On quality the frontier still won narrowly, with the residual sitting in information accuracy rather than procedure execution — compiling a procedure doesn't compile world knowledge. And on flexibility the usual objection faded: a full recompile ran 30–50 minutes on 8×H200, which is a CI build rather than a research project.

The obvious caveat was scope. All of it sat on node-graph workflows, dialogue with enumerable paths, the most compilable work there is. You can't compile a procedure you can't enumerate.

What happened when someone ran it on unbounded work

Prime Intellect's Prime Agent is the strongest attempt yet at the other half. It's an open-source coding and research harness with a design that doesn't look like a normal agent loop: an IPython kernel is the only tool interface, context is a variable the model manipulates programmatically, sub-agents are function calls in a persistent REPL, and the agent can create, update and delete its own prompts, skills and memory from its trajectory.

Its authors ran an open-weights model, GLM-5.2, inside that harness against Claude Opus 5 in a stock, unmodified Claude Code, across nine long-context and long-horizon benchmarks. The open pairing won three: many-instruction following (0.209 vs 0.175), long chain-of-thought (0.638 vs 0.558), and building a working emulator from scratch (0.208 vs 0.062). It lost six, several by wide margins: OOLONG at 128k context 0.700 vs 0.900, OBLIQ-Bench 0.669 vs 0.795, LongBenchv2 0.680 vs 0.746, many-instruction coding 0.424 vs 0.522.

The harness was clearly doing real work. The same pairing beat Pi-mono with sub-agents on eight of nine. It was the model that ran out.

Notice how the handicapping ran. The open side got a purpose-built harness; the frontier side got its shipped SDK, untouched. That's the opposite of the usual setup, where a tuned small model is compared against a zero-shot frontier baseline nobody bothered to prompt properly. This is the one comparison where the thumb was on our side of the scale, and the open pairing still lost most of it.

Two things stop this from settling the question. GLM-5.2 is 744B parameters — open weights, not a small model — so nothing here speaks to something we could train ourselves, and the gap it shows isn't a size gap. And it's a vendor blog about the vendor's own product, with no confidence intervals, no repeat runs, and cost claims that live in graphs rather than tables. Its own authors also note they substituted the baselines' official numbers after their in-house runs of those native harnesses scored worse, which is honest of them and still a handling choice.

The pattern in the wins and losses

The three wins and six losses don't scatter. They sort.

The wins are long-running work: hold many instructions at once, sustain a chain of reasoning, build an artifact over hours. That's work where a harness supplies structure, external state and verification, and where the scaffold can carry load the model would otherwise drop.

The losses are long-context work: comprehension over 128k tokens, retrieval across a large document, reasoning about material that has to sit inside the model at once. No harness supplies that. You can hand a model a better filing system; you can't hand it a better ability to read.

That distinction predicts new cases instead of relabelling settled ones, which makes it more useful than the framing it replaces. It also explains why the compilation study came out so differently: a node-graph procedure is exactly long-running work with the context requirement engineered away.

Two careful studies, two different answers

The awkward part is that decontamination doesn't sort this either, and two good studies now disagree.

Databricks built a coding benchmark from its own merged pull requests on a multi-million-line codebase written by more than 3,000 engineers, spanning Scala services, Rust systems code, a React/TypeScript frontend, protobuf/gRPC and Bazel. Changes were filtered to human-authored, test-covered ones and graded by held-out tests rather than a model judge, with git history sealed after agents were caught recovering the true implementation from it. On that, GLM-5.2 was statistically tied with Claude Opus 4.8 on quality at $1.28 per task against $1.94 (Databricks). Their summary: "open models are here for coding." No pass-rate table or task count is published, and Databricks serves open models commercially.

GDPevo (arXiv 2608.03764) is also contamination-resistant, by a different route: it decomposes enterprise workflows into atomic business rules, spreads subsets across training tasks and recombines them in held-out tests, then regenerates the whole 120-task suite automatically when contamination is suspected. It finds a real gap. GLM-5.2 in Claude Code scored 60.09% at $0.53 per task; Opus 4.8 in the same harness scored 67.07% at $1.36; DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Preview scored 48.79% at $0.040.

Both removed contamination. Both graded externally. One found a tie, one found seven points. So contamination isn't the whole story, and the long-running/long-context cut fits the pair better than a general claim in either direction: writing a pull request against a codebase you can grep is long-running work, and executing a multi-rule business workflow correctly leans harder on holding it all at once.

The GDPevo cost column is worth its own moment, because the curve is steeper than per-token pricing suggests. Seven points of accuracy buys a 61% discount. Eighteen points buys 97%. The live decision, at those prices, is between open options rather than between open and frontier. One caution: GDPevo's four agents don't share a harness (GPT-5.5 runs in Codex, the rest in Claude Code), and swapping harnesses alone is known to move cost per task by 2–3.7×, so some of that spread is scaffold rather than model.

The cheapest thing that works

The most immediately usable result this month doesn't involve training anything.

SKILL-KD (arXiv 2607.28048) takes a task where a small model failed and a stronger model succeeded, distils the difference between the two trajectories into a short piece of written guidance, then re-runs the small model to check whether the guidance actually helped, refining it while the model still fails. Weights are never updated.

A frozen Qwen3.5-4B went from 43.5 to 66.8 average across five agent benchmarks, including a 56-point jump on held-out ALFWorld environments. It works across model families: ChatGPT-5.5 successfully taught a Qwen student. And the gain was larger on the 4B (+23.3) than on a 35B model (+16.7).

The counterintuitive detail is the one to take. The winning method produced the smallest library of guidance — 38 rules and 3,010 words — and beat a variant that accumulated 96 rules and 6,821 words by nearly seven points. Validation by re-running the student is what prunes it. More written guidance was worse written guidance.

That rhymes with something visible across several of these results: interventions that check themselves against something outside the model tend to work, and interventions that check themselves against the model tend not to. GDPevo found the same thing from another direction — agents given worked examples with gold answers improved by up to 16.44 points, more than agents left to reflect on their own scores or explore unsupervised.

Nothing prices SKILL-KD, which is the gap in it. Generating the guidance means running teacher trajectories and repeated student re-runs, and the resulting 3,010 words ride along on every subsequent inference. And its baselines are other guidance-generation methods, never a fine-tuned model, so whether text beats weights is still an open question rather than an answered one.

Where the scaffold has a ceiling

OneDayAgent (arXiv 2608.05013) is useful for a reason its authors don't lead with. It pins one harness and sweeps five backend models across three families with no per-model tuning, on 104 open-ended day-long tasks. GLM-5.2 tops it at 0.821, above every shipped commercial agent measured — AutoClaw 0.799, Codex on GPT-5.5 0.664, Manus 0.645, Genspark 0.635, ChatGPT-Agent 0.626 — and a 9B open model floors it at 0.613.

Its internal ablation is small and worth reading closely. Task decomposition alone scores 0.804. Deliverable verification alone scores 0.804. Both together score 0.821, against 0.771 with neither. Each buys about three points; together they buy five, not six. They're evidently patching the same underlying failure, the model losing the plot over a long horizon, from opposite ends.

That five-point spread sits against the tens of points other scaffold results report, and the difference is what the baseline already contains. "Neither module" here isn't a bare model. It's this harness minus two features, and it already outscores four of five shipped commercial agents. Comparing scaffold versus no scaffold against module versus scaffold as if they measured the same quantity inflates how much scaffold design appears to buy. Some of the impression that designed harnesses produce enormous gains comes from that conflation.

One genuine counterweight to the whole cost story, from the same harness family: Prime Agent reports a higher maximum score than each model's native harness at lower total token usage on ARC-AGI-3 (95.5% best@1 with Opus 5, against a 95.4% human-expert baseline). If that holds up, the mechanism matters more than the number. The saving comes from running functions over data rather than spending tokens reading data through tool calls. Removing model calls is a different lever from arranging more of them, and it's the only one on record that moves quality and cost the same direction.

The honest current position

For work you can write down as a procedure, a compiled small model plus a custom harness beats frontier-plus-defaults on cost by roughly 100–460× and trails on quality by single digits, and recompiling to a changed procedure costs under an hour. That's well evidenced and unchanged.

For open-ended work, the pairing now has evidence and it's mixed leaning negative. An open model in the best available harness beat every shipped commercial agent on day-long tasks and tied frontier on real pull requests, and it lost six of nine long-context benchmarks to a frontier model in an untouched SDK. The most defensible reading is that a harness buys you the long-running half of the problem and not the long-context half.

The two things that would settle it are both cheap. Sort your own workloads into long-running and long-context piles before commissioning any model comparison; which pile they fall into may decide the question without a training run at all. And if you do run the comparison, price it per completed task rather than per token, and give the frontier arm the same scaffolding effort you gave yours. Almost nobody has done either.

Open questions

  • No one has run a genuinely small model in a purpose-built harness on open-ended work and published its cost. Prime Agent is the closest, at 744B parameters and with the money shown only in graphs.
  • Whether written guidance can substitute for fine-tuning is unasked. SKILL-KD's baselines are other guidance methods, never a tuned model.
  • End-to-end cost including data curation and training remains unpriced by anyone, on either side.
