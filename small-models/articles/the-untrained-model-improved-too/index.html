The untrained model improved too

Take a language model, train it on its own filtered outputs for three rounds, and check which individual problems it can now solve that it could not before. That per-problem ledger — gains here, losses there — has largely replaced mean accuracy as the way people report self-improvement, because an average can hide a model that learned five things and forgot five others.

Now run the identical pipeline on a copy of the model that you did not train at all.

Phantom Gains (arXiv 2608.20290) did that, with rank-32 LoRA on Qwen3-8B and a frozen control pushed through every stage of the same evaluation. The untrained copy posted a corruption-to-learning ratio of 1.5 on MATH-500 — it appeared to have both learned and forgotten things, in quantity. On AIME, the statistic that separates genuinely new capability from sharpening what was already there credited the untrained model with expanding into 28% of the problems it had never solved.

Neither number is a bug in anyone's code. The first is mostly inference batching: the same prompt, decoded once, does not always produce the same tokens depending on what else is in the batch. The second is the shape of a one-success threshold applied to noisy estimates. Both are what standard practice produces when it is pointed at a model that did not change.

Seven of them

The paper catalogues seven such failures and shows that each one inverts a reported finding when its control is absent:

  • A single greedy decode per problem manufactures capability changes out of batching nondeterminism.
  • The expansion statistic assigns an untrained model 0.280. Measured across 110 frozen comparisons, its true null is 0.058, with a confidence interval of [0.038, 0.078] — small, but not zero, which is why the obvious repair of raising the threshold does not survive.
  • A fixed output token cap pushed a distillation student's truncation rate from 16.5% to 65.3%, purely because that student writes longer answers. Capability loss and stylistic change become the same measurement.
  • Transition-level metrics need roughly ten times the data that accuracy does. A 112-problem pilot cannot produce one.
  • Change only the training seed and the same method's corruption ratio lands anywhere between 0.55 and 1.53, with accuracy shifts of +3.3, −1.5 and +0.7 points. One method, three seeds, three conclusions.
  • A ten-prompt refusal probe showed a ten-point safety drop that fifty prompts dissolved.
  • A null estimated from one frozen comparison is a point estimate being treated as a parameter.

The repair is not expensive, which is the part worth taking seriously. Replace the threshold with a per-problem exact test against a pooled baseline under false-discovery-rate control, and it detects nothing on any of eleven held-out replicates — and stays at nothing when you change the multiple-testing rule, the error rate and the pool size. The baseline draws come from replicates a multi-arm study already has lying around. No new training runs required. The authors do note the catch: you need more replicates than most studies actually possess.

Once the instrument reads zero on a model that did not change, the substantive result is legible. Distillation from an external teacher (gpt-oss-120b) registered 8–11 detections on problems the base model rarely reached. Three flavours of self-training — majority-vote filtering, STaR, and a policy-gradient arm — registered 0–2. A regression rules out the boring explanation that distillation simply gained more overall, at p < 10⁻⁸. And self-training was not neutral on what already worked: STaR broke 106 previously-solved problems and majority-vote broke 88, against a design-matched frozen control that broke 8.

The authors are careful about how far that reaches. Three rounds of LoRA at about 270 optimizer steps is a small schedule compared to the ones the finding is positioned against, the expansion comparison rests on only 22 problems, and one backbone family carries every claim. The finding is that this regime does not expand — not that self-training cannot.

The same problem, one level down

Two other papers from the same week found the same structure in different places, and the coincidence is what makes this worth writing down.

Credit Without Ground Truth (arXiv 2608.19760) asked what the step-level credit signals used to train LLM agents actually track. The signals are the standard ones: outcome-conditioned log-probability ratios, LLM-judge scores, and the policy's own confidence. The ground truth is built by executed replay — at each decision point, resample four alternative actions from the policy, roll each one forward to completion at least three times, and see what changes.

Against that, in ALFWorld with Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, none of the signals beat chance. Median within-trajectory rank correlations of 0.0193, 0.1142 and −0.043, every one of them overlapping a shuffled control. Per-step sign agreement of 50.7% and 44.9%.

The mechanism is identifiable, which is better than a bare null. Implicit credit puts about 2.33 times more weight on how confident the model was than on how much the step mattered — a ratio that replicates at 2.37 and 2.35 across configurations. Conditioning on the outcome, the operation that is supposed to make the signal causal, contributes a partial correlation of −0.004 with a p-value of 0.87. The signal is a fluency meter with extra steps.

That has a practical edge. A router that saves money by skipping the expensive LLM judge on high-confidence steps cuts judge calls by 13.1% per turn, and recovers pivotal steps at 11.9% — chance. The steps it is most comfortable skipping are the ones that decided the outcome.

The paper's seven-arm training experiment includes an arm built from the replay ground truth itself. No arm reliably beat the untrained policy at 0.422 accuracy, and the design's minimum detectable effect was about 11.8 points, so it could not have seen a realistic improvement had one existed. What did separate the checkpoints was training dose: sparser credit rules drop more turns from batches, moving optimizer steps from 112 to 88 per round. Any comparison of credit rules that does not match effective sample size is measuring how much data each rule happened to keep.

Then QuoteBench (arXiv 2608.13547) found the same composite-number problem in agent evaluation. A coding agent does not run Bash; it emits text that a harness serializes, sometimes wraps in another shell, sometimes ships over SSH, and then reparses. Insert one deliberately unescaped parser into that path, replay the identical model reply through it, and every one of eight configurations loses between 55.4 and 73.2 points on exact final-state validation.

Tell the model in advance that its output will be reparsed and six of the eight recover 30.4 to 60.7 points. GPT-5.6-sol scores 94.6% on the raw path and 91.1% when both the disclosure and the parser are present. A matched comparison would report that as a gap of −3.6 points. It is a −64.3 point hole and a +60.7 point patch. The number is not noisy — it is a sum, and the summands were the finding.

Swapping the execution path also reorders the models: Kendall's τ between the raw-path and nested-path rankings is 0.57, with one reversal the authors call unambiguous (GPT-5.6-sol trails Gemini-3.5-Flash by one task on one path and leads by eighteen on the other) and four more pairs sitting on single-task margins. QuoteBench is deliberately narrow — one mechanism, one-shot Bash generation, single stored generation per task, and multi-turn recovery excluded, which is exactly how a real agent would claw some of those points back. The authors say plainly that the results characterise the benchmark rather than deployment prevalence.

What to actually do

The three papers disagree about almost everything except the shape of the problem: an aggregate that looks fine, a control that was never measured, and a conclusion that flips when it is.

Four things follow, and none of them requires new hardware.

Run the pipeline on something that did not change. Not as a sanity check at the end — as an arm, matched in data stream, volume and evaluation, present from the start. If your metric reads non-zero on it, that reading is your floor, and every result gets compared against the floor rather than against zero.

Measure the null more than once. Phantom Gains needed 110 frozen comparisons to put its own null at 0.058 [0.038, 0.078]. One frozen comparison gives you a number, not a distribution, and a threshold fitted to it is fitted to noise.

Match effective sample size across arms. Any component that filters, prunes, gates or routes changes how much data reaches the model as a side effect. If arm A trains on 112 optimizer steps and arm B on 88 because its credit rule was sparser, the difference between them is dose.

Name the path. A benchmark score is a property of a model and the pipe it was measured through. Say which serialization layer, which output ceiling, which effort setting, which chat template version. Credit Without Ground Truth reports a chat-template defect that moved its own measurability rate by about twelve points mid-analysis — inside a paper whose entire subject is measurement error.

And budget for it. Transition-level reporting costs roughly ten times the evaluation data that a mean does. That is the honest price of knowing which tasks your change won and which it lost, and it is inference rather than training — cheaper than the GPU time it protects, and considerably cheaper than shipping on a number the untrained model would also have posted.
