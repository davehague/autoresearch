The 95% that isn't

Nobody serves a large open-weight model at full precision. The checkpoints are too big for affordable hardware, so everyone compresses — and the compression vendors all report roughly the same reassuring number: you keep about 95% of the model. That number is real, and it is measured on the wrong things.

Syzygy Research published a compression of Alibaba's Qwen3.6-35B-A3B down to 1.7 bits per weight, small enough to run on a laptop at up to 120 tokens per second. Their model card claims 95.0% mean retention across twelve benchmarks, and — unusually, and to their credit — publishes the per-benchmark table behind the mean. Read down it:

Retention
AIME26 (competition math)99.5%
MATH-50099.4%
GSM8K98.4%
MBPP+ (code)98.3%
HumanEval+ (code)97.4%
MMLU-Redux96.2%
IFEval (instruction-following)94.0%
MuSR (multi-step reasoning)92.7%
BFCL-v3 (function calling)92.0%
τ²-bench (tool-use agents)90.0%
IFBench (hard instruction-following)83.2%

The spread from top to bottom is 16 points. And the ordering is not arbitrary: it sorts by how far the task is from a problem with one checkable answer. Competition math survives almost untouched. What degrades is following instructions, calling functions, and completing multi-turn tool-use tasks — which is to say, everything a model does when it is acting as an agent rather than answering a question.

So "95% retention" is a mean over a set of benchmarks in which math and code happen to outnumber agentic ones. A model that keeps 99.5% of its AIME score and 83.2% of its IFBench score has not lost 5% of itself. It has lost a sixth of the part you were planning to deploy.

This is not one vendor's quirk

An independent test run the week before found the same asymmetry from the other direction. A case study on Qwen3.6 27B built 58 quantizations of a single model and probed each with 1,400 questions stratified into seven tiers of obscurity. Factual knowledge held flat above about 5 bits, dropped at 3-bit, and fell off sharply at 2-bit — non-linear, with the rare facts going first. The same author ran the same quantization ladder against AIME-120 and Terminal-Bench 2.1 and found no cliff at all above 4 bits.

Two independent measurements, different models, different methods, same finding: reasoning benchmarks are insensitive to exactly the damage compression does. One study shows it costs obscure facts. The other shows it costs instruction-following and tool use. Both show that if you compress a model, run a reasoning eval, and see nothing, that is what you should have expected — whether or not anything broke.

Which makes the savings hard to price

The savings are not in doubt. Cloudflare, serving Kimi and GLM in production, reported that an FP8 key-value cache halved cache memory — lifting Kimi K2.6's resident context from roughly 686,000 to 1.37 million tokens at 41% higher peak throughput — while INT4 weights shrank GLM 5.2 from 705 GB to 421 GB and cut per-GPU memory from 88 GB to 52 GB, with throughput up 16–55% depending on concurrency. Net effect: about 30% lower cost per token.

Their quality claim for all of that is "within 0.8 points across benchmarks." Which benchmarks is not stated. Given the two results above, that sentence is compatible with losing very little and compatible with losing a sixth of your agentic capability, and there is no way to tell which from the outside.

Note also that these are two separate knobs, not one. Weight precision decides whether the checkpoint fits your hardware; cache precision decides how much context you can hold while it runs. Halving the cache roughly doubles resident context, which is the wall long agent loops actually hit. Anyone planning a deployment is turning both, usually without describing either as a quality decision.

What to measure instead

Three things follow, and none of them require new tooling.

Evaluate the capability you are shipping, not the one that is easy to score. If the workload is agentic, the before-and-after has to be agentic: tool calls, instruction adherence, multi-turn completion, on your own tasks. A benchmark average is now demonstrably the wrong instrument — the Syzygy table is the cleanest proof, because the answer is visible inside a single vendor's own numbers only because they declined to report just the mean.

There is one cheap screen. The 58-quantization study found that knowledge loss tracks KL divergence from the full-precision original at r = −0.981. That correlation is strong enough to rank candidate quantizations against a sample of real traffic without running any benchmark at all. It grades output distribution shift rather than task success, so it is a filter, not a verdict — but it is a filter you can run in an afternoon.

Price the work, not the tokens. Databricks built a coding benchmark from its own merged pull requests on a multi-million-line codebase, graded by held-out tests, and found Sonnet 5 costing $2.09 per task against Opus 4.8's $1.94 — despite a cheaper per-token rate, because it burned 1.9× the tokens. In the same study, holding the model fixed and changing only the surrounding software moved cost per task by more than 2×. Per-token pricing ranked both of those backwards.

The honest state of it

Every source here is vendor-adjacent. Syzygy is selling a compression method and published no independent evaluation, no error bars, and no ablation showing the losses come from the bit width rather than from its retraining step. Cloudflare is selling inference. Databricks commercially serves open models. The 58-quantization study is one self-published author testing one model without replication.

What makes the picture credible is not any single source but that they disagree about everything except this: compression is cheap, the savings are real, and the bill is charged to capabilities that standard evaluations do not report. If you are going to serve an open model — and at these sizes you will compress it to do so — the compression is a quality decision wearing a cost decision's clothes. Price it that way.
