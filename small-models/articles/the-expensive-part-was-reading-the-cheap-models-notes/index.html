The expensive part was reading the cheap model's notes

The standard advice for running coding agents on a budget is to start with a cheap model and switch to an expensive one when the cheap one gets stuck. A study published this week measured that plan on 500 software-engineering tasks and found it costs more than never using the cheap model at all, and scores ten points lower.

The numbers come from The Handoff Tax: Continuing Non-Native Trajectories in LLM Agents (arXiv 2608.24358), which ran 58,000 agent runs and 2 million API calls across two model pairs. Take the Claude pair on SWE-bench Verified. Claude Haiku 4.5 working alone resolves 60.7% of tasks at $0.40 per task. Claude Opus 4.7 working alone resolves 79.2% at $0.72. Start with Haiku, hand the run to Opus partway through, and you get 69.2% at $1.61 — a bit over twice the price of just using Opus from the start, for ten fewer points.

The GPT pair behaves the same way on quality. GPT-5.6 Luna alone resolves 58.7%, Sol alone 83.7%, and the handoff 67.5% — 36% of the gap closed.

The reason is mundane. When the second model picks up the run, it has to read everything the first model did: every tool call, every intermediate reasoning step, every abandoned attempt. It pays for those tokens at its own rate. A cheap model that flails for twenty steps has not saved money; it has written an expensive prompt.

Deleting the notes helps

The interesting result is what happens when you hand over less.

The study tested three interfaces. Pass the whole trajectory. Pass a compacted summary of it. Or delete the trajectory entirely and hand over only the repository — the files the first model actually edited — plus the original task. In every condition the code changes survive; only the narrative varies.

Deleting the narrative is the best option, in both families, by a lot. The Claude pair goes from 69.2% to 72.4%. The GPT pair goes from 67.5% to 79.7%, which lifts gap recovery from 36% to 84%. The compacted summary lands in between.

So the weak model's edits are worth keeping and its account of why it made them is worth negative points — up to twelve of them.

This is not an isolated oddity. SWE-bench Science (arXiv 2608.19799) found the same sign reversal from the other direction: adding supplied context to 91 production repair tasks moved the strong model from 36.26% down to 31.87% while moving a weaker model from 16.48% up to 23.08%. The same text helps one model and hurts the other. What the handoff study adds is that this happens inside a single run, where the unhelpful text was produced by the other arm of the same experiment.

There is a practical version of this. If your agent setup can switch models mid-task, the switch should hand over a diff and a task statement, not a transcript. That is a small change and, on one of the two pairs measured, it is worth twelve points.

The other direction works

Run the switch the other way — start with the expensive model, finish with the cheap one — and the economics reverse. The GPT pair holds 81.0% against Sol's own 83.7%, while moving the long tail of the run onto a model priced roughly eight times lower. The Claude pair is weaker at 65.6%, so the effect is not uniform, but neither pair shows the penalty that escalation showed.

And the preference about what to hand over flips too. Downshifting, deleting the strong model's trajectory hurts: 65.6% drops to 60.9%. The strong model's reasoning is something the weak model uses. The weak model's reasoning is something the strong model has to work around.

Two other recent results point the same way about which part of the work is actually expensive. GameXpert-Bench (arXiv 2608.21833) measured what it costs an agent to find a bug rather than be told about it: Claude Opus 5 lost 7.6 points, while open-weight models lost between 19.6 and 30.5. Being handed a named defect is cheap; noticing it is not. And What is Missing from AI Post-Training AI (arXiv 2608.19072) found that a better scaffold moved execution quality by about forty points and strategy quality by roughly zero.

Put together, the shape is fairly clear: the capability you are paying frontier prices for is figuring out what is wrong. Once that is settled, a much cheaper model can carry out the fix at close to the same success rate. Most cost-saving setups do this backwards.

Cheaper models are getting good enough for this to matter

This would be a narrow finding if the cheap end of the market were far behind. It is not, and it moved again this week.

JIT-Agent (arXiv 2608.25593) trained a 27-billion-parameter open model, built on Qwen3.6-27B, to write a custom agent scaffold on demand for whatever task it is handed. Given one of those generated scaffolds, DeepSeek-V4-Flash scored 9.1 points above GPT-5.6 on DeepSearchQA and 4.3 above it on OdysseyBench. Against Claude Code as the scaffold, on the same underlying model, the generated one scored 85.1 to 79.6 while using 400,000 tokens instead of 625,000. It lost one of the three head-to-head comparisons, on an instruction-following benchmark, 63.8 to 66.9.

Two of nine benchmarks is a narrow win and the paper does not say what harness the GPT-5.6 comparison used, which matters. But it is the first study to assemble the whole configuration — open model, purpose-built scaffold, frontier model as the control — and have the open side come out ahead on anything.

The uplift also varies enormously by task type: planning tasks gained 20.2 and 24.8 points from a better scaffold, while workspace tasks gained 3.4 and 5.4. A seven-fold spread from the same generator and the same four-module design. Averaging across task types produces a number that describes none of them.

Meanwhile the open models themselves keep closing. GLM-5.3-Flash, released 26 August under an MIT licence, scores 84.3 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 against 87.4 for the best frontier score in its own comparison table, and beats Claude Opus 4.8 on three of five rows. Those are vendor-published numbers with vendor-chosen settings, and independent measurement runs lower — Artificial Analysis puts its composite at 57, and a community run of 113 DeepSWE tasks resolved 58.4% against the card's claimed 63.4. Still, the gap on agentic coding work is now small enough that mixing models is a real design decision rather than a last resort.

There is a catch worth naming, because it is easy to miss. GLM-5.3-Flash activates only 18 billion parameters per token, but it has 320 billion in total, and all of them have to be in memory. That is somewhere around 160 to 200 GB at four-bit precision — a server, not a workstation. The open model that closed the gap is one you rent. The open model you can actually own is smaller and measurably weaker.

What to be careful about

The handoff study uses two model pairs and both are closed, same-family pairs: Haiku to Opus, Luna to Sol. Nobody has measured what happens when a Qwen model hands off to a Claude model, which is exactly the case where the two would disagree about tool formats and message structure — plausibly the case where the tax is highest.

It also runs one episode per task and the authors say plainly that they do not estimate run-to-run variance. That is a real limitation for this kind of work. Thinkingbox (arXiv 2608.19741), a benchmark of 507 stateful business workflows, found that the best agent tested could find a working solution on 91% of tasks but succeeded on only 25% of them every single time out of twenty attempts. A comparison decided on one run per condition has partly selected on luck, and the honest reading is that the direction of these results is more trustworthy than their magnitudes.

The scaffold-generation result has the opposite problem: the effects are large but the paper publishes no training cost for the 27B model that produces them, and reports no confidence intervals. And the quantization question sitting underneath all of this got its first well-funded measurement this week — an independent sweep of Qwen3.8-27B found four-bit indistinguishable from full precision on three benchmarks, two-bit costing about seven points, and one-bit at random-guessing level. It cost roughly $3,000 in rented GPU time, $2,308 of which went to the single agentic benchmark that was the only one able to detect the two-bit loss at all. The cheaper benchmarks reported that two-bit was free.

That last detail is the one to carry around. Across all of these results, building the thing is cheap and finding out whether it worked is not.
