The cluster was never the constraint

If you have GPUs, training a small specialist model for a narrow task is easy. That was true in early August, and three weeks of results have made it more true — while quietly moving the bottleneck twice. First to the data. Now, for the kind of training people actually want to do in 2026, to something nobody was measuring at all: the sandbox.

The easy part keeps getting easier

The mechanics of fine-tuning stopped being a research project a while ago. PyTorch-native post-training with first-class multi-node support ships in torchtune, and Unsloth puts a run at roughly twice the speed and a third of the VRAM of a naive setup, on AMD as well as NVIDIA.

The memory floor has since fallen through. Agentic ESOpt (arXiv 2608.17310) replaces reinforcement learning with evolution strategies — perturb the parameters, run inference, score the whole trajectory, never run a backward pass — and measures peak GPU memory on a 4B model at 8.41 GB, against 58.88 GB for agentic GRPO and 89.40 GB for agentic PPO. That is full-parameter optimisation, not adapters, at roughly inference memory. It was also faster on the paper's long-horizon task: 9.4 hours against 19.0.

At the small end the numbers are almost silly. A LoRA-tuned 1.5B that runs real shell-command work on a laptop CPU cost one A100-hour to train. A skill library that lifted a 9B across five benchmarks cost $8.95 in API tokens (arXiv 2608.10538). A verified reinforcement-learning environment costs under 30,000 synthesis tokens (arXiv 2608.14312).

The part that bites, still

The catch is what you train on, and nothing since has softened it. Sensitivity of Small Language Models to Fine-tuning Data Contamination (arXiv 2511.06763) fine-tuned 23 models from 270M to 4B with training data deliberately corrupted at 25, 50, 75 and 100 percent:

  • Syntactic corruption is fatal. Character-level scrambling produced near-total failure in every model tested.
  • Semantic corruption has a cliff, not a slope. False or irrelevant answers did almost nothing to measured quality, right up until they did everything. You can carry a serious defect and watch a healthy loss curve the whole way.
  • Bigger students are more exposed. The paper calls it a capability curse: more capable base models absorb corrupted semantics more readily, so the reflexive fix for a weak fine-tune points the wrong way.
  • Alignment training is not a safety net. In places it lowered resilience rather than raising it.

Understanding Knowledge Distillation in Post-Training (arXiv 2606.22942) narrows the other common shortcut: distillation only pays when the teacher genuinely beats the student on the target task, and fails when the capability gap is too small, when the task is beyond what demonstrations can teach, or when the teacher's synthetic data is poor.

The bottleneck moved again, and it isn't a GPU

The most interesting training result of the last month is not about training at all.

Through 2026 the technique people converged on is training a model through a shipped agent harness — run Claude Code or OpenHands or OpenCode unmodified, put a proxy at the model's API boundary, and reconstruct training samples from the captured traffic. Agent Lightning v1.0 (arXiv 2608.17528) named the pattern and reported that four other frameworks had already adopted the same design; it lifted a 9B from 41.8% to 56.4% on SWE-bench Verified from 6,000 examples. ClawGym II (arXiv 2608.16798) did it to an open 30B model through harnesses it never read.

LEGO-RL (arXiv 2608.17393) built the same thing at production scale and then, unusually, measured its own pipeline. Across 3,699 trials, the breakdown of where wall-clock time goes:

stageshare of wall-clock
agent execution91.3%
verification3.9%
sandbox setup2.3%

Mean trial duration is 920 seconds, of which 840 is the coding agent working in a container. The optimiser is a rounding error on the clock.

This upends the standard way of budgeting for it. Nobody rations containers. Everybody rations GPUs — and GPU prices have been the loudest cost signal of the year, with Nvidia doubling the RTX PRO 6000's MSRP to $16,000 and DDR5 up as much as 500% year over year. Those numbers are real and they price the wrong resource for this workload.

The corroboration is in what actually made LEGO-RL faster. Asynchronous scheduling completed seven training steps where synchronous completed three in the same 7.5 hours. Fetching container image data on demand instead of pulling whole images improved worst-case cold-start latency 23× and cut network traffic from 21.6 GB to 1.59 GB. Both wins are attacks on the 91.3%. Neither touches the GPU.

One caveat worth stating: LEGO-RL is the authors' own system, measured by its authors, on one harness against one benchmark family — and, like every paper in this area, it publishes no dollar cost, GPU count or total wall-clock for the training runs themselves.

Automating the judgment is a different problem from automating the work

The obvious next thought is to hand the whole loop to an agent. What is Missing from AI Post-Training AI (arXiv 2608.19072) checked, across a corpus spanning seven benchmarks, four base models and 20 agent configurations, and the answer splits cleanly.

Agents are good executors. Given a base model and a benchmark, they write the code, launch the run, evaluate checkpoints and improve the number. An experience scaffold — a persistent experiment journal, a distilled skill library, a dedicated evaluator agent — lifted execution by 12.6 points on GSM8K and 40.8 on HumanEval.

They are not strategists. The training strategy is fixed before the agent writes a line of code, and the entire remaining budget goes to local adjustments inside it. The choice does not respond to the task — it responds to which tool you opened. 80.7% of Claude Code trajectories converge on full-parameter supervised fine-tuning; 89.6% of Codex CLI trajectories converge on parameter-efficient tuning. Those two paths differ by roughly 50 GB of GPU memory and, at the extremes, by orders of magnitude in cost.

Three escalating interventions failed to unstick it. Experience improved execution and left the strategy static. A human reviewer redirected the plan successfully before training, and the agent fell back into local-adjustment loops once training started. Two to eight times the reasoning tokens helped on the easy tasks and did essentially nothing on the hardest. The authors' conclusion is that what's missing is not capability but a trigger — a mechanism for spontaneously re-evaluating the plan mid-flight.

The interventions ran on a single agent-and-model pair, three times each, on a 1.7B base and three well-trodden benchmarks, so this is a sharp finding on a narrow setup rather than a law. It comes with a useful price tag: 10 hours on four A800 GPUs per attempt. Cheap. And, on this evidence, cheap for an attempt whose most consequential decision was made in the first few minutes.

What this changes

Three things, in order of how quickly they'd change a plan.

Budget the sandbox, not the accelerator. If you're training through a real agent harness, the question to answer before requesting GPU time is how many concurrent containers you can run and what your image registry does under a hundred simultaneous cold pulls. That measurement takes an afternoon and almost nobody has made it.

Record which agent drove the run. A comparison between full-parameter and parameter-efficient tuning is not interpretable if the method was selected by the coding tool rather than by evidence. It belongs in the methods section next to the base model and the seed.

The data question is still the whole thing. Cheap compute makes the run routine. It says nothing about assembling a corpus worth training on, and getting that wrong fails quietly, suddenly, and invisibly to the metrics you'd naturally watch. There's one early instrument: Reasoning Quality Emerges Early (arXiv 2606.26797) shows trace quality can be read from roughly the first 100 tokens — cheap, but only for reasoning traces, and not a general detector for the corruption modes above.

What it does not say

It does not say don't train your own. The ceiling is high: Sub-Billion, Super-Frontier (arXiv 2606.22606) reports fine-tuned models between 360M and 3B beating GPT-5.4 and Claude Sonnet 4.6 across nine relation-extraction benchmarks in two domains, by margins that aren't close.

The results are complements. One shows the prize is real. The others show what has to be true to claim it — narrow scope, a genuinely stronger teacher, clean data, enough sandbox throughput to keep the GPUs fed, and a human making the method call — and what happens when any of them is missing.

Open questions

  • What adequate curation and validation looks like at production scale — still unmeasured.
  • A detection method for threshold (semantic) failures, since loss curves don't show them.
  • Whether the capability curse holds at deployment sizes; the contamination study topped out at 4B.
  • Whether the 91.3% execution share holds for harnesses other than OpenHands and task families other than SWE-bench.
  • What the total bill actually is. Not one paper in this group publishes a dollar figure for its own training runs.
