In a production coding-agent bill, moving to open-source and lower-cost models is the biggest lever at 40–60% — ahead of routing, token trimming and governance

Databricks published a ranked ledger of its agentic-coding cost levers, described as directional, based on an informal survey: open-source / lower-cost models 40–60%, dynamic request and task routing 30%+, reducing token overhead 20–40%, developer visibility, tripwires and budgets 10–20%. Its own gateway router is reported to cut average task cost by more than 30% while roughly matching the quality of the most expensive model. GLM models showed "highly competitive price/performance" and were rolled out internally. The framing is a split between an intelligence frontier (best capability at any price) and an efficiency frontier ("the set of models that have the best price point for a given level of intelligence"), with the claim that most daily coding does not need the former.

For this vault the ordering is the finding, and it raises the bar on our own case rather than supporting it. The 40–60% row prices buying a cheaper model's API — GLM through a vendor endpoint — not running one on our hardware. So the first half of an agentic coding bill is available with no infrastructure at all, which means any self-hosting proposal has to beat the open-model API price, not the frontier one. That is a much harder target than the comparison self-hosting-savings-erased-below-engineer-scale-thresholds was built against, and it belongs to thread (b) of the economics map.

Run 12's finding cuts the other way in the same breath, and the two together are the actual position: the cheaper API is a rate someone else sets, and one of those rates just acquired a stated 2–10× of headroom overnight (open-model-api-prices-are-not-a-stable-planning-input). So the honest reading is that switching models captures the largest immediate saving and carries the only unhedged price risk on the board.

One small item with a sharp implication: Databricks reports cost regressions from Opus 5.0 versus 4.8, and Stripe found Opus 4.7 did not meaningfully improve quality over 4.6. This vault has been modelling frontier capability as a monotonically rising bar a small model must chase. Two independent production reports say a newer frontier release can cost more for no measured quality gain — so the bar is not only lower than leaderboards imply (frontier-agents-top-out-at-25-percent-on-realistic-oncall-rca) but not reliably rising in the dimension we would be paying for.

Limits, and they are large. The four percentages come from an informal survey with no methodology, no n, no confidence intervals and no definition of "task" — practitioner consensus written down, not measurement. The router figure is unaudited and self-interested: Databricks sells the gateway that implements the lever. "Roughly matching the quality of the most expensive model" is asserted with no evaluation described, which is exactly the claim grader-model-is-a-fourth-variable-that-has-to-be-pinned says not to accept unexamined.
