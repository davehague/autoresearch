Reproducing the tokens is not enough for a sparse model — replaying rollout-time expert routing is what closes the train–inference gap

Run 23 recorded that a trainer which sees only request–response pairs inherits five named failure surfaces: retokenization, sample merging, advantage calculation, loss normalization and backend scheduling (the-trainer-sees-only-request-response-pairs-and-that-is-five-failure-surfaces). It was an enumeration without measurements. LEGO-RL measures two of them on the model class we would actually host.

The first is history alignment. A shipped harness constructs prompts, compacts context and rewrites history on its own schedule, so the trajectory the trainer reconstructs need not be the token sequence that was sampled. LEGO-RL captures raw generation streams at the serving boundary through an in-process proxy, and — the part that matters more — excludes from optimization any trajectory whose history was genuinely rewritten or truncated, training only on tokens whose rollout-time identity and probability can be preserved exactly. Faithfulness is bought by discarding data, not by approximating it.

The second is specific to sparse mixture-of-experts policies and is not on run 23's list. Even with an identical token sequence, rollout and training can route to different experts. Replaying rollout-time routing decisions moves the rollout–training log-probability correlation from 0.9946 to 0.9993 and cuts the mean per-token discrepancy |Δlog p| from 0.0062 to 0.0025 — on the same single-node workload, so nothing else varies. Across the three production runs the correlation is held above 0.99, with medians reported per harness.

This is a checklist item rather than a discovery, and that is its value. Every open checkpoint this vault has considered training at the sizes that fit our hardware is sparse MoE — the trained model here is Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, ~3B active. A run that trains on the right tokens with the wrong experts will not crash; it will train slightly off-policy and report plausible curves. The instrument that catches it costs one correlation statistic.
