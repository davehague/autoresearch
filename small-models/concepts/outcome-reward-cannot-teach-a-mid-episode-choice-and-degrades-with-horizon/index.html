Sequence-level reward cannot teach a decision made in a handful of tokens, and the longer the episode the more actively it teaches the wrong thing

The pairing's training half has one default answer to every question: run outcome-rewarded RL over the whole trajectory and let credit find its way. SkillGate names a structural reason that fails for decisions taken in a few tokens, and audits a completed run's own training artifacts to confirm it.

Under a broadcast sequence-level advantage, one number updates both the handful of tokens naming a chosen skill and the thousands executing the task. Three properties follow, each measured over 12,800 training trajectories and each worsening monotonically with horizon:

  • Share. The choice's portion of the loss dilutes 7× with trajectory length.
  • Sign. The credit it inherits is increasingly wrong-signed — a correct choice is punished whenever the execution after it fails.
  • Value. Matched prompt groups show a +11.2 pp success gap for making the correct read. The decision is among the most valuable in the trajectory and receives the least reliable signal.

The authors call it selector credit starvation, and the fix is to stop broadcasting: partition the token support into two disjoint credit channels, outcome credit reaching only execution tokens, and a separate action-local advantage reaching exactly the skill-naming tokens, positive only when the trajectory's single read is the correct one. The action-weighted sum of the selector advantage over a group is exactly zero, so it exerts no pressure toward reading more or less — only over which name gets written.

Why this matters past skills: the argument is about token share and horizon, not about skills. Run 15 established that outcome-only supervision decays with horizon across 1,547 papers, and run 12 found turn-level credit helps a 3B more than a 7B. This supplies the mechanism in a form precise enough to predict where else it bites — any short, high-leverage decision embedded in a long trajectory. Choosing a tool. Choosing a sub-agent. Choosing whether to verify. Every one of those is a few tokens inside thousands, and this vault's harness literature is largely a catalogue of exactly such decisions.

The uncomfortable corollary for the pairing: the longer the horizon, the worse plain agentic RL is at the choices the harness exists to structure. Training and harness engineering are supposed to be substitutable levers; on this argument the training lever's grip on harness-shaped decisions weakens as the work gets longer, which is the direction our workloads run.
