Reporting which tasks a change won and lost, rather than a mean, costs about ten times the evaluation data — and a hundred-problem pilot cannot do it

Phantom Gains' fourth failure mode is a sample-size claim rather than a bias: transition metrics require roughly 10× the data that accuracy does, and the paper's own 112-problem pilot is called insufficient for the ledger it was meant to produce. The reason is arithmetic — a transition is the difference of two noisy per-problem solve-rate estimates, so its variance is the sum of theirs, and the quantity being estimated (did this problem flip) is per-problem rather than pooled. The paper's working design is k = 128 samples per problem per checkpoint with a hysteresis band of [0.41, 0.59] on the estimator.

Two other numbers set the same budget from different directions. The seventh failure says a null estimated from one frozen comparison is a point estimate treated as a parameter; the paper's own null needed 110 frozen comparisons to land at 0.058 [0.038, 0.078]. And this run's credit-assignment audit found its 128-task training design had a minimum detectable effect of ~11.8 points — larger than any effect anyone was hoping to see (credit-rule-comparisons-measure-training-dose-not-credit).

This prices this vault's standing experiment. The plan of record — run the candidate checkpoint on our tasks as shipped and record where it fails, two harnesses, one prompt set, with repeats — is a transition ledger. Sized for a mean it is a week; sized for the ledger it is an order of magnitude more evaluation, plus frozen replicates budgeted purely to measure the null. That is still cheap relative to training, and it is not what anyone writing the plan would have costed.

The useful corollary is that the extra cost is evaluation, not training. Nulls come from baseline replicates a multi-arm study already owns; what has to grow is the number of problems and the samples per problem, both of which are inference.
