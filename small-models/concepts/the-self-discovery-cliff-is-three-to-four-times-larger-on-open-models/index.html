Self-discovery costs the open tier roughly 2.6–4× what it costs the leader.

This is the capability half of a gap the harness excuse does not reach, and it lands on top of two prior results that say the same thing in different vocabulary. Run 27's ThinkingBox found three quarters of agent failures are agents continuing as though a failed action had succeeded (three-quarters-of-agent-failures-are-failures-to-recover-from-a-tool-error); run 21 found the open–frontier gap is mostly a consistency gap (the-open-frontier-gap-is-mostly-a-consistency-gap). Noticing that something is wrong, unprompted, is where the open tier degrades fastest.

It is directly actionable and it is a routing rule, not a research programme: a workflow whose harness can name the defect narrows the open-model gap roughly fourfold; a workflow that requires the model to notice on its own is the one to keep on frontier. That also means any harness we build should be spending its complexity budget on detection and verification rather than on execution scaffolding — consistent with run 24's finding that a scaffold moved execution 40 points and strategy zero (the-scaffold-moved-execution-forty-points-and-strategy-zero).

Discounts: unrefereed preprint, single run per configuration, no seeds or variance anywhere; effort settings differ by row (max / xhigh / high / "on"), so part of the cliff spread may be a price difference rather than a capability one; bugs are synthetically injected (19–27 per game) and may not resemble real defect distributions; game development is not our workload class; and the paper never labels weight availability, so the open/closed split is ours.
