Escalating 7% of calls to a frontier model cut 74% of the bill for 6% of the accuracy — the deployment shape, stated as a fraction rather than a benchmark delta

NVIDIA released NeMo Switchyard, an open-source library routing each request across a heterogeneous model pool, alongside the open-weights Nemotron 3.5 Lightning (30B total / 3B active). Three deployment figures are named: LangChain reports 74% lower cost by routing only 7% of calls to a frontier model, at a 6% accuracy tradeoff; Ramp reports matched performance at 58% lower cost and 33% lower runtime; and NVIDIA claims a mixed stack reaching frontier-level accuracy at nearly one-third the task-completion cost of Opus 4.8 alone.

This is routing-across-models-is-learnable-routing-a-model-across-itself-is-not arriving as shipped infrastructure four days after the vault corrected itself on it. The mechanism is the same external-referent argument: a heterogeneous pool lets a strong model's successes label a weak model's failures from outside the weak model, which is exactly what routing-supervision-is-scarcest-where-routing-would-help-most showed is impossible when a model routes across its own configurations.

The 7% is the number to steal. Every cost argument in this vault has asked which model to run; this asks what fraction of calls need the expensive one. Seventy-four percent of a bill for 7% escalation and 6% accuracy is a better trade than any single-model swap on the ledger — better than open-model-trades-seven-points-for-61-percent-of-the-bill, and in the same direction as switching-to-cheaper-models-is-the-largest-named-coding-cost-lever. It also reframes the threshold test: if 7% of calls need frontier, "does the small model match" was never the question. Which 7%, and can we identify them cheaply, is.

What the architecture concedes: the winning configuration contains a frontier model, on someone else's API, at a price they set (open-model-api-prices-are-not-a-stable-planning-input). Routing lowers exposure to that price; it does not remove it.
