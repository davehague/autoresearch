Harness benefit may be non-monotonic in model size, peaking at middle-tier models — which would break the vault's "harness upside is largest on small models" extrapolation

Weng relays two findings from a cited 2026 study that cut against a rule this vault adopted last run: harness-updating capability is roughly flat across models from Qwen2-32B to Opus 4.6, and harness benefit is non-monotonic in model size, with middle-tier models benefiting most.

The vault's run 7 synthesis concluded that harness effects — both the accuracy that good layout buys and the accuracy that self-inspection loops cost — are largest at the small end, from harness-design-is-a-large-quality-lever-on-small-models (a 4B ablation, 22 points), graph-action-space-harness-lifts-small-model-retrieval (gain shrinking as the model grew) and self-judgement-penalty-is-largest-on-the-smallest-models (8.0–11.3 points at 1.5B against 2.0–1.3 at 7B). Every one of those lives at 1.5B–7B. A curve that rises to a middle-tier peak and falls off at both ends is consistent with all three — they only ever observed the rising limb — while implying the opposite practical conclusion. If benefit peaks in the middle, "pick the smallest model that fits and invest in the harness" is the wrong shape, and our harness spend pays best on a mid-size open model.

The flat harness-updating result is the cheaper and more comfortable half: proposing a better harness does not appear to require a frontier model, so a mid-size open model can plausibly do its own harness-design work.

Grade this down hard. It is a secondhand relay — the study arrives as a sentence in a survey essay on self-improvement, without its sample, task set, or the size grid that would let us see where the peak sits. It does not overturn three direct measurements; it identifies that all three were taken on one side of a curve nobody has plotted. What it earns is a design change to our own experiment: put a mid-size arm in the size ladder rather than assuming monotonicity.
