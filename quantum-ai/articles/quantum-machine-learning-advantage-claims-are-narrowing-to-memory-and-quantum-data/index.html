Quantum machine learning advantage claims are narrowing to memory and quantum data

The version of quantum machine learning that most people picture, a variational circuit trained like a neural network on a small quantum computer, has been losing ground to classical simulation since 2023. The advantage claims that are still being made have moved elsewhere: to memory use when processing streamed classical data, and to learning from data that is itself quantum. Both are newer, both are theory or simulation, and neither has been run on hardware.

What the push-back shows

Cerezo and eleven co-authors argue in Does provable absence of barren plateaus imply classical simulability? (arXiv 2312.09121, Nature Communications 16, 7907, 2025) that the structure that lets a variational circuit avoid barren plateaus, the flat loss landscapes that make training impossible, usually confines it to a small subspace that a classical computer can also handle. The evidence is collected case by case rather than proved in general, and the authors list their own caveats: average-case arguments, smart initializations, and models outside their assumptions.

A concrete case is the quantum convolutional neural network, a flagship model. Bermejo and colleagues (Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks are Effectively Classically Simulable, arXiv 2408.12739, PRX Quantum 7, 020304, 2026) show that a randomly initialized QCNN can only use low-weight information from its input, that the usual phase-of-matter benchmarks are solvable from exactly that information, and that a classical surrogate built from Pauli shadows reproduces QCNN classification on up to 1,024 qubits. They also note that hard datasets can be built, for example by embedding Shor's algorithm, so the result limits today's benchmarks rather than the whole model class.

Sahebi and colleagues (On Dequantization of Supervised Quantum Machine Learning via Random Fourier Features, arXiv 2505.15902, a preprint) extend a classical-matching technique to quantum neural networks and quantum kernels for regression and classification, with sufficient conditions and numerical support for existing kernel methods. It says when a classical model can match; it does not say a quantum model never wins.

The empirical base rate is not kind either. Bowles, Ahmed and Schuld (Better than classical? The subtle art of benchmarking quantum machine learning models, arXiv 2403.07059, a preprint) ran 12 popular models on 6 classification tasks, 160 datasets in all, in noise-free simulation. Out-of-the-box classical models won overall, and removing entanglement often made no significant difference. Their literature sample, a keyword search of arXiv through December 2023, found about 40% of the 55 papers claiming that a quantum model outperforms a classical one and three papers reporting that it does not. These are small problems and mainstream near-term models, so the result says little about later constructions.

Three of those four papers come from one research network (Cerezo, Holmes, Cincio and collaborators), and every result is conditional. That is a reason to read the cluster as one strong argument rather than four independent confirmations.

What the remaining claims are

Zhao, Zlokapa, Neven, Babbush, Preskill, McClean and Huang (Exponential quantum advantage in processing massive classical data, arXiv 2604.07639, a 169-page preprint from Google Quantum AI and Caltech) prove that a quantum computer of polylogarithmic size, using a technique they call quantum oracle sketching with classical shadows, can classify and reduce the dimension of streamed classical data where a classical machine with the same accuracy needs exponentially more memory. On movie-review sentiment and single-cell RNA data they report size reductions of four to six orders of magnitude with fewer than 60 logical qubits.

The limits sit in the paper itself. The real-data numbers are classical simulations that predict quantum performance from convergence guarantees; deep circuits on 60 logical qubits were not simulated, and no hardware ran. The advantage is in space, not time: given enough memory, classical algorithms have asymptotically comparable runtime and may be faster in practice, while the quantum algorithm pays a data-loading cost that the extrapolation ignores. A critique of the resource accounting has circulated outside peer review, but we have only seen it described in a search result and cannot assess it. The proofs have not been independently checked as far as we could find.

The second claim concerns quantum data. Danaci and co-authors (Evidence of Quantum Machine Learning Advantage with Tens of Noisy Qubits, arXiv 2605.21346, a preprint) simulate a learning task with a known asymptotic advantage under realistic noise and device models. They report a clear gap between coherent processing and the best measure-first classical strategies at 30 to 40 noisy qubits, and say that matching the quantum protocol classically would take months or years of measurements. The simulations reach at most 22 qubits and are extrapolated beyond that.

Where that leaves things

Nothing in the push-back touches either surviving claim, because simulability arguments target trainable variational models and these constructions are different: one is a fault-tolerant streaming algorithm, the other a quantum-data protocol. The honest summary is that the old hope has weakened, the new hopes are narrower and unproven on hardware, and the test that would matter, a circuit-level run or an independent check of the oracle-sketching resource count, has not been reported.
