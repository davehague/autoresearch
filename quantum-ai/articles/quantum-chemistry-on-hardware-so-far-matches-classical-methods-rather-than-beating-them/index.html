Quantum chemistry on real hardware so far matches classical methods rather than beating them

Chemistry is the application most often promised to quantum computers. The experiments run so far on real hardware compute molecules that classical computers can already handle, and the papers that compare them to classical methods find parity at best. At the same time, the classical side keeps improving on the targets quantum computers were meant to own.

What the hardware has done

IBM, RIKEN and collaborators used a Heron processor together with the Fugaku supercomputer to run sample-based quantum diagonalization on the nitrogen molecule and two iron-sulfur clusters, with circuits of up to 77 qubits and 10,570 gates (Chemistry Beyond the Scale of Exact Diagonalization on a Quantum-Centric Supercomputer, Science Advances 2025, arXiv 2405.05068). The quantum chip only supplies samples of electron configurations; the classical computer does the diagonalisation. The authors say the results suggest this approach can tackle problems beyond the size exact diagonalization can handle. A follow-up used all 152,064 nodes of Fugaku and reports accuracy "comparable to some all-classical approximation methods", with no head-to-head numbers in its abstract (Closed-loop calculations of electronic structure on a quantum processor and a classical supercomputer at full scale, arXiv 2511.00224).

Exact diagonalization is a modest bar, though. A numerical study by Reinholdt and colleagues, published in the Journal of Chemical Theory and Computation in 2025, tested the same family of methods on N2 and 2Fe-2S. It found that the method keeps drawing configurations it already has, and that where that problem is absent its expansions are less compact than those from classical selected configuration interaction, making it more expensive (Critical Limitations in Quantum-Selected Configuration Interaction Methods, arXiv 2501.07231). That is one group and two molecules, and it uses no quantum hardware, so it is a strong prompt for a like-for-like comparison rather than the last word.

The error-corrected route is earlier still. Quantinuum ran quantum phase estimation for the hydrogen molecule on logical qubits encoded in the [7,1,3] color code, with up to 1,585 fixed and 7,202 conditional two-qubit gates. The energy came out within 0.001(13) hartree of the exact answer, which reads as 0.001 plus or minus 0.013 hartree, about eight times the 0.0016 hartree usually taken as chemical accuracy (Quantum Error-Corrected Computation of Molecular Energies, arXiv 2505.09133, a preprint). It shows a pipeline running, on a molecule a laptop can solve.

Google's Quantum Echoes experiment is the one result in the field that its authors place beyond known classical algorithms, but it measures an abstract quantity. The company's own blog says its NMR molecular-geometry demonstration "is not yet beyond classical" (A verifiable quantum advantage, research.google/blog/a-verifiable-quantum-advantage).

What the classical side has done

A 2023 Nature Communications paper by 18 authors, among them quantum-algorithm researchers at Google and Caltech, concluded that evidence of exponential quantum advantage across chemical space for ground-state energies "has yet to be found", while leaving room for useful or polynomial speedups (Evaluating the evidence for exponential quantum advantage in ground-state quantum chemistry, arXiv 2208.02199).

In January 2026 Zhai, Chan and colleagues posted classical calculations, combining high-order coupled cluster, density matrix renormalization group and an extrapolation protocol, that estimate the ground-state energy of a model of the nitrogenase FeMo-cofactor to chemical accuracy (Classical computational simulation of the FeMo-cofactor model to chemical accuracy and its implications, arXiv 2601.04621). FeMoco is the molecule most often named as the reason to build a fault-tolerant machine. The result is a preprint, covers a model and not the whole enzyme, and has not been reproduced.

Machine learning is attacking the everyday end of the problem. Microsoft's Skala, a learned exchange-correlation functional for density functional theory, reaches 2.8 kcal/mol error on the GMTKN55 main-group benchmark at roughly the cost of semi-local DFT (Accurate and scalable exchange-correlation with deep learning, arXiv 2506.14665, preprint). That is still above the 1 kcal/mol bar and does not address strongly correlated molecules such as FeMoco.

The quantum side is also moving

Low and colleagues at Google Quantum AI report that spectrum amplification and a new factorization cut the cost of fault-tolerant ground-state energy estimation by a factor of 4 to 195 on iron-sulfur and CO2-fixation catalyst models (Fast quantum simulation of electronic structure by spectrum amplification, Physical Review X 2025, arXiv 2502.15882). That is an algorithm result with no hardware behind it.

Gundlach and colleagues at MIT modelled both sides together and expect classical methods to stay ahead in many cases for at least a couple of decades, with quantum phase estimation passing full configuration interaction for tens to hundreds of atoms within the next decade (Quantum Advantage in Computational Chemistry?, arXiv 2508.20972). It is a projection that depends on assumed hardware progress.

Where that leaves things

No chemistry computation on hardware has been shown to beat the best classical method, and the one numerical comparison of the leading hybrid method with classical selected configuration interaction favours the classical side. The case for quantum chemistry now rests on strongly correlated systems and on fault-tolerant machines that do not exist yet. Both of those are targets where the classical estimates are also falling, and where the key preprints are unreplicated.
