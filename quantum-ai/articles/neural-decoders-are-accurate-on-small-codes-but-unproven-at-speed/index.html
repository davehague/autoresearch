Neural decoders for quantum error correction are accurate on small codes and unproven at speed

A quantum computer that corrects its own errors needs a classical program, the decoder, that reads the error signals and decides what went wrong, once per cycle, roughly every microsecond on superconducting chips. Machine-learning decoders have beaten conventional ones in accuracy since 2023. What nobody has shown yet is a learned decoder that is also fast enough, on real hardware, at the code sizes that matter.

What the accuracy results show

Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI's Learning to Decode the Surface Code with a Recurrent, Transformer-Based Neural Network (AlphaQubit; arXiv 2310.05900, published in Nature 635, 2024) trained a transformer on simulated data and fine-tuned it on measurements from the Sycamore chip. In the preprint it made 2.748% logical errors per round at distance 5, against 2.915% for a tensor-network decoder, a gap of about 6%. The advantage over matching decoders was described as larger. On simulated data it kept a smaller edge up to distance 11. The same paper reports the costs: the amount of training data grew roughly a hundredfold between distance 3 and 11, and throughput was one to two orders of magnitude short of a 1 microsecond cycle.

A December 2025 successor, A scalable and real-time neural decoder for topological quantum codes (AlphaQubit 2; arXiv 2512.07737), reports about 1 microsecond per cycle on Google TPUs for the surface code to distance 11 and the color code to distance 9. Its distance-11 logical error is 3.73e-6 for the real-time model against 1.89e-6 for the full one, so the speed costs about half the accuracy. Those numbers come from simulated noise; the only experimental data is recorded Willow data at distances 3, 5 and 7, decoded offline. It is a preprint, and it does not close the loop with a quantum processor.

For qLDPC codes, which promise far fewer physical qubits per logical qubit, a Harvard group's Scalable Neural Decoders for Practical Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computation (Cascade; arXiv 2604.08358) reports logical error about 17 times below IBM's Relay-BP decoder on the [144,12,12] Gross code at 0.1% physical error, and about 4,000 times below BP+OSD, reaching roughly 1e-10. That is simulation only, trained at a single noise level.

The classical side is not standing still

IBM's Improved belief propagation is sufficient for real-time decoding of quantum memory (Relay-BP; arXiv 2506.01779) is a non-learned decoder. In simulation it reaches between one and two orders of magnitude lower logical error than BP+OSD on the Gross code at p = 3e-3 and is comparable to matching on surface codes. Its real-time argument rested on an assumed 20 ns belief-propagation iteration; an October 2025 IBM prototype, Real-time decoding of the gross code memory with FPGAs (arXiv 2510.21600), reports 24 ns per iteration and under 1 microsecond average decoding time per cycle for error rates below 3e-3 (abstract read; we did not check the full paper). Google's Tesseract (arXiv 2503.10988) is another classical baseline, a search-based most-likely-error decoder. Cascade's comparison is the one direct test of a neural decoder against Relay-BP, and it comes from the neural decoder's authors.

Speed is where the disagreement sits

Cascade's own body text reports about 40 microseconds per cycle for a single unbatched syndrome on an NVIDIA H200 GPU. That meets the roughly 1 ms budgets of trapped-ion and neutral-atom machines but not the 1 microsecond budget of superconducting ones; its FPGA figures are projections. An independent study, Rethink the Role of Neural Decoders in Quantum Error Correction (arXiv 2605.12046, ICML 2026), tested five neural architectures to distance 9 in simulation and found accuracy depended more on training-data scale (convergence beyond about 1e7 samples) than on architecture, and that 4-bit quantization was needed to approach microsecond FPGA latency. In its synthesis results only one architecture, a temporal convolutional network, fit the budget at distance 9, and its analytical estimate (about 1.05 microseconds) is slightly over budget while the synthesis estimate (0.77 microseconds) is under. The authors did not run full place-and-route.

Live hardware is thin. A 12-author preprint, Real-time Surface-Code Error Correction Using an FPGA-based Neural-Network Decoder (arXiv 2605.04892), ran a distance-3 surface code on a superconducting processor with 550 ns total closed-loop latency, 124 ns of it the network, inside a 1.25 microsecond cycle. NVIDIA's CUDA-Q QEC 0.6 post reports a small CNN pre-decoder in front of PyMatching that cut decoding time 2.5 times on a distance-13 code but improved logical error only 1.1 to 1.5 times; it is a vendor benchmark on an NVIDIA B300 GPU and the post does not say whether it used QPU data. A three-author Mamba decoder preprint (arXiv 2510.22724) argues transformer attention costs O(d^4) in code distance and reports a state-space alternative matching it on Sycamore data.

Where this leaves things

Learned decoders are measurably more accurate than the best conventional decoders on the small hardware codes where they have been tested, and the most recent simulation results suggest the gap may widen on qLDPC codes. The unresolved questions are practical ones: whether the data and compute bill stays tolerable as codes grow, whether any learned decoder runs at about 1 microsecond in a live loop beyond distance 3, and whether classical decoders such as Relay-BP, which already have an FPGA path, close most of the accuracy gap first. No independent group has yet compared the two families at equal latency and equal noise.
