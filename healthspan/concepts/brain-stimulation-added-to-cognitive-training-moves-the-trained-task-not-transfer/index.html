The cleanest test this literature has of "does brain training transfer" — and it lands on the same answer as all the messy ones

Twenty-three randomised trials gave healthy adults cognitive training and split them by one variable only: whether the weak electrical current running through their scalp was real or a sham. Both arms trained identically. On the task they drilled, the current helped: Hedges' g = 0.424 (95% CI 0.180 to 0.667). On working-memory tests they had not drilled, it did not: g = 0.130 (−0.040 to 0.301), not significant. Overall, across everything, g = 0.191 (0.062 to 0.320) — small, but real.

⚑ Why this design is worth more than the numbers. The recurring objection to every cognitive-training result in this vault is the comparator: trials compare training against a wait-list, or against nothing, in an intervention nobody can be blinded to, on outcomes scored by assessors who know which arm they are watching. Here both arms did the same training, the sham device produced the same scalp sensation, participants could not tell which they got, and the only difference was the current. So the estimate genuinely answers what does the stimulation add — and the transfer failure cannot be blamed on a weak control group.

⚑ That is what makes this a confirmation rather than a new claim. cognitive-training was built on the finding that the trained task moves and transfer is where every study gets thin, assembled from sources that each had a comparator problem. This source has no comparator problem and produces the same shape.

⚠ The split is a pattern, not a demonstrated dissociation, and the authors say so first. The point estimates differ threefold, but the direct statistical comparison between trained and untrained outcomes was not significant (p = 0.076). The authors write that "the current evidence does not establish a reliable difference between outcome types." The vault records the pattern at that strength and no higher.

⚑ What a g of 0.19 buys. About a fifth of a standard deviation. Pick one person from each arm at random and the stimulated one scores higher roughly 55% of the time — a hair better than chance. No outcome in this review is functional: every endpoint is a laboratory memory score, and whether the gain corresponds to anything a person notices in daily life is neither addressed nor addressable here.

⚠ What it does not establish. Only 1 of 40 study-outcome entries was at low risk of bias (34 raised some concerns, 5 were high risk); the review was not prospectively registered and screening was not fully duplicated. Residual heterogeneity was large (Q(134) = 572.37, p < 0.001) and no moderator explained it. The "intervention" spans 1–20 sessions at 1–2 mA under a single label, with no dose-response fitted. Nothing null here is sold as absence — the transfer interval reaches 0.301, so a small real transfer effect remains entirely possible. Capped suggestive.
