Resistance training's gait/functional edge over other exercise is small, and mostly not statistically distinguishable from it

Restricted to the 28 trials that compared resistance training against an active alternative (stretching, walking, cycling, agility, multicomponent exercise) rather than a passive control, the pooled RT-specific effect on gait/functional outcomes drops from moderate (ES 0.59) to small (ES 0.22, moderate GRADE certainty). Walking distance, gait speed and sit-to-stand all lose statistical significance entirely. Timed-up-and-go is the exception — it keeps a significant edge, but it halves, from large (ES 1.20 vs passive) to moderate (ES 0.42 vs active).

This pushes back on any reading of resistance-training-improves-gait-and-functional-outcomes-vs-passive-control as evidence that resistance training is uniquely effective for gait and falls-relevant function — most of the apparent advantage is the advantage of being active at all, not something specific to lifting. It is the first source in this vault to build the active-vs-passive comparator distinction directly into its method rather than requiring inference — closing, for this one intervention-outcome pair, the comparator-strength gap docs/open-gaps-log.md logged on 2026-07-29 and 2026-07-31 as missing from shared/study-appraisal.md's lens set.

Filed suggestive, not stronger: the authors' own limitations section reports moderate-to-considerable risk of bias across the underlying trials and no pooled confidence intervals. stance: pushes-back because it directly qualifies the strength of a claim resistance-training would otherwise be read as supporting without reservation.
