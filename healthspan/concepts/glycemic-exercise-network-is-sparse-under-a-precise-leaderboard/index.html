A ranking reported to two decimal places, resting on five to nine trials per option

SUCRA — the surface under the cumulative ranking curve — is the number network meta-analyses put on a leaderboard. It expresses the probability that an option ranks best within this particular network, given this particular set of trials. It is not an effect size, it carries no confidence interval in the way an effect estimate does, and crucially it does not know how much evidence sits behind each option — a node built from five small trials and a node built from fifty large ones are ranked on the same scale, and the output looks equally precise either way.

Here is what sits underneath this leaderboard:

And the quality underneath that: of all 34 trials, 6 are at low risk of bias, 24 carry "some concerns", and 4 are at high risk. The authors' own CINeMA certainty ratings across network comparisons run from very low to moderate — a fact they put in the abstract rather than a supplement, which is to their credit and is the reason the vault can state it.

What the review did right, and it is more than most. Consistency — whether the direct and indirect evidence inside the network agree — was checked three ways: by deviance information criterion against an unrelated-mean-effects model, by node-splitting on every closed loop, and by a sensitivity analysis. All HbA₁c node-splits returned p > 0.05. One fasting-glucose loop disagreed (aerobic versus combined, p = 0.0335) and the disagreement vanished when high-risk-of-bias trials were excluded — which is exactly the diagnosis you want, and exactly the diagnosis that tells you the high-risk trials were distorting something. Publication bias was tested per outcome, and the positive result — Egger's p = 0.0337 on fasting glucose — was reported and left uncorrected.

Why this note exists. The vault has now refused four SUCRA leaderboards on this reasoning: supplement-outcome-specific-ranking-too-tidy-for-a-sparse-network (a 78-trial supplement network whose per-outcome ordering was too tidy for its sparsity), collagen-ranking-rests-on-indirect-evidence (a first-place finish assembled from indirect comparisons), exercise-type-cognition-ranking-not-supported, and now this one. A pattern of four is a rule.

The rule, stated for reuse: take the direction, refuse the ordering. What this network supports is combined training beats its components — which is corroborated from a completely different literature and outcome (aerobic-plus-resistance-lowest-mortality) and is therefore believable on grounds outside the ranking. What it does not support is the relative placement of the other four nodes, which rests on five to nine trials each, mostly not at low risk of bias, against comparators the paper never describes (traditional-chinese-sports-ranking-is-unseparable-from-its-comparator).

Where this connects to the run's other lesson. Tonight the vault also recorded that an umbrella review's top credibility grade, convincing, is earned on p-values and case counts and contains no effect-size criterion at any tier (umbrella-credibility-grades-measure-decisiveness-not-magnitude). SUCRA and a credibility grade fail the same way: both are confident-looking summary numbers that answer a narrower question than the one a reader brings to them. The defence is identical — read the underlying quantity and the amount of evidence behind it before reading the label.
