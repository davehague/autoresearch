"It works, but only in the right people" — the subgroup rescue, and how to price it

Amiri 2026's two primary outcomes both failed: insulin p = 0.09, HOMA-IR p = 0.08. The conclusion then pivots:

"However, its effects may depend on baseline insulin status, highlighting the need for targeted interventions in individuals with higher insulin resistance."

The support for that sentence is a meta-regression in which baseline insulin and baseline HOMA-IR significantly modified the treatment effect (p = 0.01). What the same meta-regression also tested, and found null: baseline HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, serum magnesium level, age, and BMI.

That is one significant modifier out of six tested, uncorrected for multiplicity, deployed to reframe a study that did not find what it went looking for. Per shared/study-appraisal.md's p-hacking lens — many comparisons, no correction, the winner promoted — this is a hypothesis-generating observation being read as a finding, and the vault grades it single-study / open-question accordingly.

Why the vault is careful rather than dismissive here. The moderator is not implausible. "The supplement works if you're deficient / dysregulated to begin with" is a real phenomenon, and the vault has recorded a close cousin of it approvingly: nad-precursors's benefit is genuinely confined to disease-specific populations (nad-precursors-benefit-confined-to-deficiency-populations), and walking-sleep-benefit-scales-with-baseline-sleep-problem — landed the same night — is exactly this shape and is graded suggestive.

So what separates a credible moderator from a rescue? Two things, and Amiri has neither:

  1. Pre-specification. Kong's baseline-PSQI moderator was one of a planned moderator set on a pre-registered protocol, and the primary effect was significant on its own. Amiri's moderator is reached for after the primaries fail.
  2. Multiplicity discipline. Six moderators, one hit, no correction. Kong's β = −0.302 sat under a significant main effect; Amiri's p = 0.01 is the only thing holding the paper's forward-looking claim up.

A moderator that appears only after the main effect dies is a different kind of object from one that explains a main effect that lived. That distinction is the note.

Practical consequence: none. The population is diabetic and pre-diabetic adults; David is neither; and even taking the moderator at face value, it says magnesium might help people with high baseline insulin resistance — which is a claim about a clinical population and a matter for a clinician, not for this document. magnesium-glycinate stays on the Watch list, on its sleep signal, unchanged.

Rebutted by: magnesium-lowers-homa-ir-where-a-smaller-pool-could-not — a 78-RCT pool (Mohammadi 2026, 2026-08-13) reports the primary HOMA-IR effect this note's paper was trying to rescue by subgroup, significant on the whole sample at −0.48 (−0.79, −0.18) — so the post-hoc moderator was not needed to find an effect. ⚠ It does not vindicate the moderator either: the rebutting synthesis states that baseline magnesium status could not be examined at all, because "the majority of included RCTs were not designed to recruit participants according to Mg status." The responder hypothesis is untested after 78 more trials, not confirmed — see magnesium-dose-response-analysis-found-no-dose-response-for-most-outcomes.
