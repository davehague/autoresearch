The company that makes the jelly paid for the trial, declared no competing interests, and published the null

The funding statement is explicit: "This study was funded by Shinryo Corporation (Fukuoka, Japan). The experimental jellies were provided by the company." The competing-interests statement, two paragraphs later, reads: "The authors declare no competing interests."

Both facts are recorded, and the tension between them is the point. A manufacturer funding a trial of its own consumer product and supplying the product is a commercial interest by any ordinary reading, whatever the authors' personal position. Per shared/evidence-model.md bias watch, this is flagged and not laundered into higher apparent certainty.

And yet the direction of the finding cuts the other way, which is why this does not fire a fatal flag. shared/study-appraisal.md is explicit that industry funding is not fatal on its own — it contributes to a mixed verdict when it compounds a real design weakness. Here the design is unusually strong for a supplement trial: pre-registered before enrolment, powered a priori at 90% with a Holm-adjusted alpha, double-blind with a placebo matched on appearance, texture, taste and smell, blinding formally verified as intact, 95% adherence, zero dropouts. And the pre-specified primary endpoint came back null — a result directly against the sponsor's commercial interest, published anyway (hydrogen-supplementation-null-on-sleep-quality-in-powered-rct).

⚑ That combination makes the null stronger, not weaker, and it is worth being explicit about why. The usual worry about sponsor funding is that it biases results toward the product. When a sponsor-funded, sponsor-supplied, adequately-powered, properly-blinded trial reports no effect, the funding is evidence for the null rather than against it: the party with every incentive to find an effect, and the resources to design a trial that could, did not find one. A negative result from a motivated sponsor is among the more credible things in nutrition research.

Where the funding does show, and it shows in the framing rather than the numbers. The paper's title, abstract and conclusion are about the microbiome responder analysis, not about the failed primary endpoint — an exploratory split defined by a post-randomisation outcome, in cells of roughly eleven people, without family-wise correction (hydrogen-responder-subgroup-is-defined-by-a-post-randomisation-outcome). That is where a commercially inconvenient null gets converted into a commercially useful story: not by altering a number, but by choosing which number the paper is about. The authors' own hedging — "exploratory," "warrants confirmation in larger studies" — is honest and will not survive the trip to a product page.
