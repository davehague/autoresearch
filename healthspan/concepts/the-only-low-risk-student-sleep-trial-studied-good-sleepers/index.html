The quality gradient runs backwards: the best-measured trial studied people who slept fine

One trial of seven earned a low overall risk-of-bias rating — Li's 12-week supervised Baduanjin trial. It is also the largest in the review (n = 206) and the only one with blinded outcome assessors. Its participants started at PSQI 3.7 and 4.1, below the ≥5 threshold conventionally used to define poor sleep. They did not have a sleep problem.

Meanwhile the trials enrolling genuinely poor sleepers are the ones nobody blinded. Iyer's yoga trial began at PSQI 9.2/9.3; Ezati's and Amzajerdi's at 6.3–6.5. All three sit at some concerns on outcome measurement.

That arrangement is the reverse of the one you would design, and it complicates a claim this vault already holds. exercise-sleep-benefit-concentrated-in-poor-sleepers states that exercise's sleep benefit concentrates in poor sleepers. This source is consistent with that — and equally consistent with the benefit concentrating wherever the outcome assessment is weakest. The two explanations are not separated by any analysis in the paper.

⚠ The review's one interesting subgroup inherits the same confound. Shorter interventions appeared to help more — and the shortest trials (Iyer and Fu, both 4 weeks) are also those with the worst baseline sleep, while the longest (Li, 12 weeks) has the best. Duration and baseline severity are entangled. The authors correctly decline to interpret the subgroup, but do not name the entanglement; it is named here.

Graded single-study and pushes-back: this is one review's trial table, not a meta-regression, and it complicates rather than overturns the poor-sleeper claim.
