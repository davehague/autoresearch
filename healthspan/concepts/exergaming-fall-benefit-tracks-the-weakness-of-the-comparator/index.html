An intervention's effect size is a statement about its control group — demonstrated here by the authors themselves

Nine trials of video-game-based exercise for fall prevention, stratified three ways by what the comparison group was actually asked to do. The gradient is the finding:

ComparatorFall rateCertainty
Non-evidence-based active (a leaflet, low-intensity exercise)IRR 0.58 (0.44–0.77), I² = 41%MODERATE — the only one in the paper
Usual care / no controlIRR 0.60 (0.35–1.01) — crosses onelow
Evidence-based active (strength and balance training)IRR 0.41 (0.27–0.62)low — and number of fallers RR 1.38 (0.62–3.06)

Read the table twice. The only moderate-certainty result in the entire review is the comparison against a control the authors themselves classify as having no fall-prevention evidence base. Against usual care, the interval crosses one. Against real strength-and-balance training — the thing this vault already recommends — every estimate is low certainty and the one count-of-fallers estimate points the wrong way.

The pooled headline of IRR 0.53 averages across all three, so its impressiveness is partly borrowed from the weakest contrast. The authors say so, and they say it having deviated from their own registered protocol to make the point: the PROSPERO protocol planned to pool all active comparators together, and they split them after identifying "important conceptual and clinical differences," before final synthesis. A protocol deviation that costs the authors their headline is the rare kind worth trusting.

⚠ What this does not license. The evidence-based-comparator estimates are low certainty on one to three small trials, so "exergaming is not better than strength and balance training" is no evidence of superiority, not evidence of equivalence — the distinction this vault has now enforced twelve times. What can be said is narrower and sufficient: strength and balance training is the comparator everything has to beat, and nothing here beats it.
