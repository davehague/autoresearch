A single 200 mg dose of L-theanine improves choice reaction time (31-RCT meta-analysis)

In a Molecular Psychiatry meta-analysis of 31 randomized placebo-controlled trials, a single 200 mg oral dose taken 30–60 minutes before cognitive testing improved choice reaction time — SMD 0.51 (95% CI 0.25–0.77), which the authors read as enhanced attention. It is the paper's most precisely-estimated result: the interval sits well clear of null, and the finding is an acute, single-dose effect, not a product of chronic supplementation.

Why this matters for the vault. l-theanine was opened on subjective sleep quality (l-theanine-subjective-sleep-quality) and has been arguing ever since about whether the objective sleep evidence exists (l-theanine-objective-sleep-signal). The highest-tier L-theanine synthesis now available never pools a sleep outcome at all — and the effect it does find clean is an alertness effect. That does not contradict the sleep claim, but it reorients the map: the best-evidenced thing L-theanine does is make a rested person quicker on a reaction task, in the middle of the day.

Bounded honestly (why suggestive). The source's reliability verdict is mixed (shared/study-appraisal.md), which caps strength here. The 31 pooled trials share only 1,168 participants — a mean of roughly 38 people per trial — and the senior author founded a company that sells dietary supplements, the same seller-conflict pattern already recorded on l-theanine-sleep-sr-cotter-2025. Choice reaction time is a laboratory surrogate, not a healthspan outcome: nothing here says a faster reaction test extends anything.

The practical shape of the claim. This is a daytime finding with a daytime dose and a daytime timing window, which is exactly where the vault already files the well-known L-theanine + caffeine pairing (caffeine-timing). It supplies no reason to take L-theanine at bedtime, and per GOAL.md an attention-sharpening compound is not the obvious member of a wind-down routine.
