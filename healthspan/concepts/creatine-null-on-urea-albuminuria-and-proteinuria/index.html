Four kidney markers that ignore creatinine are all flat on creatine

Alongside the estimator disagreement that makes this study interesting (creatine-lowers-estimated-gfr-without-lowering-measured-gfr), the same 26 randomised trials pooled four other kidney measures — serum urea, albuminuria, proteinuria and urinary creatinine — and found no significant difference versus placebo on any of them.

This is the load-bearing part of the safety case, and it is easy to skip past. Taken alone, the tracer comparison that fails to reproduce the filtration drop is an underpowered null (p = .06) and proves nothing (creatine-reference-method-gfr-comparison-is-underpowered). What turns "one estimator disagrees" into "the estimator is what is wrong" is that four independent markers, none of which uses creatinine as its signal, agree with it.

What each marker would have shown if the kidney were actually being damaged. Serum urea is a second nitrogen waste product cleared by the kidney; genuine loss of filtration raises it. Albuminuria and proteinuria measure protein leaking into the urine, which happens when the filtering membrane is injured — they are the standard early markers of kidney damage, and they are the ones a clinician looks for when an estimated filtration rate drops. Urinary creatinine checks the output side of the creatinine story. A process capable of cutting true filtration by 10.75 mL/min would be expected to disturb at least one of these. None moves.

Graded suggestive, with an explicit reporting caveat. The source is appraised mixed, capping strength. But there is a second, specific limit here: the retrieved structured abstract reports these four outcomes only as "no significant differences" — it gives no point estimates, no confidence intervals and no study counts. So the vault cannot say how tightly any of these nulls is bounded, and cannot rule out that some rest on very few trials. That is a real gap in what this note can support: it is four nulls pointing the same way, which is a coherence argument, and it is not four precisely-bounded demonstrations of no effect.

What it does and does not license. It supports reading the creatinine rise and the estimated-filtration drop as a measurement artifact rather than injury — which is the source's own conclusion. It does not establish that creatine is renally inert over long horizons: every endpoint in this study is a laboratory marker, and no hard renal outcome — incident chronic kidney disease, progression to dialysis, kidney failure — appears anywhere in the source.
