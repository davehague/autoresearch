Bolting an unsupervised home exercise plan onto a working diet intervention added nothing detectable — because 54% of participants didn't do it (RCT arm, n=34)

Within the same 105-person trial, participants randomized to diet counseling plus a home-based exercise prescription (PROMED-EX) showed no larger improvement than diet counseling alone (PROMED) on nutritional status (MNA +2.9 vs +2.7), diet quality (+3.9 vs +4.0 points), or global cognition (+0.2 vs +0.3) — and its physical health-related quality-of-life gain was smaller and non-significant where the diet-only arm's was significant (+4.0, 95% CI ‒0.8–8.8 vs +6.0, 1.2–10.9).

The exercise simply wasn't done. Only 46% of the exercise arm reached even half their prescribed sessions, 13% reached 80%, and self-reported physical activity energy expenditure did not differ from the control group at all. The program was unsupervised, home-based, and delivered via printed guides and prerecorded videos — the authors' own discussion names the missing ingredients directly: no instructor supervision, no social-support scaffolding, both previously shown to drive exercise adherence in older adults. Participants with compromised nutritional status at baseline may also have had less exercise tolerance from fatigue.

Why this is a no-effect finding on the exercise increment, not on exercise generally. This trial cannot speak to whether a supervised or group-based exercise program would add benefit over diet alone — it tested one specific unsupervised home format and that format was not followed. It is direct, randomized-trial evidence for exercise-adherence's core standing question: format and supervision, not motivation alone, gate whether a prescribed exercise dose is actually delivered.
