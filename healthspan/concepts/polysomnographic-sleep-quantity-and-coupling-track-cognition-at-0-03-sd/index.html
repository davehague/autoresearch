Two of nine sleep dimensions tracked cognition — at three hundredths of a standard deviation

Of nine latent composites derived from 44 polysomnography-based sleep metrics, exactly two were associated with concurrent global cognition in 5,958 adults: sleep quantity and efficiency (longer, more consolidated sleep), β = 0.03 (95% CI 0.004–0.06), p = 0.033; and slow oscillation–spindle coupling — how precisely the fast bursts of sleep-spindle activity lock to the slow electrical waves of deep sleep — β = 0.04 (0.003–0.07), p = 0.039. The remaining seven, including fragmentation, N3 predominance, REM bouts and respiratory disturbances, were not significant.

What β = 0.03 means, in the only terms that matter. The outcome is a composite cognitive score standardised so the population spread is 1.0. An association of 0.03 means one unit of change in the sleep composite goes with three hundredths of that spread. For scale, the difference between an average performer and someone at the 60th percentile is about 0.25 — roughly eight times larger. These are effects no individual could experience and no test could detect in one person.

Three reasons this is filed as directionally interesting and practically inert.

It is cross-sectional. Sleep and cognitive testing happened at the same visit. "Better-preserved brains sleep more efficiently" fits the data exactly as well as "efficient sleep preserves the brain," and this design cannot separate them.

The multiplicity landscape produces hits like these by construction. Nine composites against two outcome families is at least eighteen primary tests with no correction described in anything retrievable. Two survivors at p = 0.033 and p = 0.039 sit precisely where uncorrected noise lands — a Bonferroni-type threshold across nine tests (α ≈ 0.0056) would erase both. The vault records them as hypothesis-generating.

It replicates a prior null-shape rather than a prior effect. sleep-cognition-associations-small-and-cross-sectional is the MESA analysis reaching the same result independently — five of thirteen composites significant, all at β ≈ 0.05, all cross-sectional — including, notably, spindle–slow oscillation coupling at β = 0.050, the same dimension surviving here at 0.04. Two consortia, different cohorts, different clustering methods, and the same dimension turns up at the same trivial magnitude. That consistency is real and worth recording; it is also consistent with a small true effect and with a shared methodological artefact, and this evidence cannot distinguish those.

Held suggestive, not strong, by the source's mixed appraisal — a paywalled full text (methods unauditable), an observational ceiling, uncorrected multiplicity, and effect sizes below any stated minimal important difference. The companion finding is the one with weight: the same nine composites were null for incident dementia across 1,134 cases (nine-polysomnographic-sleep-composites-null-for-incident-dementia).

Nothing here supports chasing a sleep-architecture number. Slow oscillation–spindle coupling is not a modifiable target with any established intervention, and 0.03 SD would not be worth targeting if it were.
