The biomarker moved a long way and the deaths did not follow it — within three years, in people over 75

The measurement is unusually clean. Because this trial randomised withdrawal of an established drug rather than initiation of a new one, it produced something rare: a randomised, sustained, large change in a single biomarker, with the pre-change value observed in the same people. LDL cholesterol rose about 56 mg/dL (≈1.45 mmol/L) within three months of stopping and then sat there for three years. The continuation arm did not budge. Adherence, in other words, was demonstrated biochemically rather than asserted.

The size of that change, in the field's own currency. Large statin meta-analyses express benefit per mmol/L of LDL reduction — roughly a fifth fewer major vascular events per mmol/L. A 1.45 mmol/L rise is therefore a large exposure change by the standards of the literature that established the drug class. Over three years, all-cause mortality did not diverge.

⚠ What this does not license. "LDL doesn't matter" is the over-reading and this trial cannot support it. Four reasons, all of them the trial's own:

  • Three years is short. Statin mortality benefits in the trials that established them accrue over five to ten years.
  • These people had been treated for a decade (61.7% for more than ten years). If statins work partly by stabilising plaque, plaque already stabilised may take years to change, and the authors write that this "remains unclear."
  • The trial was not powered to see a small difference — it enrolled 49% of its target, and its interval reaches +2.60 percentage points.
  • Every cardiovascular secondary ran numerically the wrong way — heart attack 2.1% vs 1.4%, stroke 2.7% vs 1.9% — with intervals wide enough to hold real harm.

What it does show. ⚑ Within a three-year horizon, in over-75s with no cardiovascular disease, a large randomised change in LDL cholesterol was not accompanied by a change in the rate at which they died. The authors' own framing is that in this age group "lowering LDL cholesterol has less effect on ASCVD outcomes and mortality than other major risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, smoking, cancer, and ageing" — and they note the observational literature reporting higher all-cause mortality at low cholesterol in people over 80, which is itself confounded by illness lowering cholesterol.

The vault's pattern. This is another surrogate-versus-outcome dissociation, and it has an unusual property: unlike creatine's brain loading or nicotinamide riboside's doubled NAD⁺, the surrogate here is not a mechanistic curiosity but the single best-validated biomarker in cardiovascular medicine, with a causal case built on randomised trials and Mendelian randomisation. ⚑ A dissociation between an established causal surrogate and an outcome, inside one randomised comparison, is the strongest form of the pattern this vault collects — and the most important one not to over-read.

Capped suggestive. Not a claim about cholesterol in general, in younger people, or in anyone with cardiovascular disease.
