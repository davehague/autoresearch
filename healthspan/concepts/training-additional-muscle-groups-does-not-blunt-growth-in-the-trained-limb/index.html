Adding leg work to the session did not cost the arm anything

A three-arm randomised trial testing whether muscles compete for a limited pool of growth resources — a hypothesis this same laboratory published two years earlier. 105 untrained adults, randomised to:

  • arm only — low-load unilateral elbow flexion, 2 sets at 30% of 1RM to failure (n = 36)
  • arm plus legs — the identical arm work, plus 4 sets of knee extension per leg at 20–30 RM (n = 33)
  • non-exercise control, time-matched (n = 36)

18 supervised sessions over 6 weeks. Outcomes: ultrasound muscle thickness at the 60% and 70% anterior upper-arm sites, and elbow-flexor 1RM. Analysed with Bayesian ANCOVA comparing Bayes factors and posterior probabilities across competing models — a framework that can express evidence for equivalence, not merely a failure to reject a difference.

Result: the added leg work cost the arm nothing. Anterior upper-arm muscle thickness rose 0.19 cm with the legs versus 0.18 cm without. Elbow-flexor 1RM rose 0.32 kg without the legs versus 0.25 kg with. The two differences point in opposite directions, which is the signature of noise rather than of a real cost. The authors' conclusion on their own hypothesis: "no evidence for competition of adaptations in muscle size and strength under uncontrolled nutritional conditions."

The scope conditions are the whole story, and the authors write them themselves. They close by saying "whether greater training volume or limited nutrient intake induces a competition for resources warrants further investigation." Those are exactly the two conditions under which competition is predicted, and this trial imposed neither: the volume added was moderate and the participants ate freely. So the finding is "adding a moderate second exercise does not blunt growth in a fed, low-volume, untrained context" — not "training volume is free."

Two limits on how far to trust the numbers. First, no measure of uncertainty around any estimate was retrievable — the structured abstract publishes four point estimates and not one interval, Bayes factor or posterior probability, and the article is behind a subscription wall. The entire inferential claim lives in numbers this vault has not seen. Second, untrained people, six weeks, low load to failure, one muscle measured. Capped at suggestive on that basis.

This is the third independent design in this vault to look for a downside of more training work and fail to find one, which is why it is recorded as extends rather than as a new claim.
