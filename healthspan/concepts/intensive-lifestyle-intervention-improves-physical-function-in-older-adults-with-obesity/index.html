Six months of supervised diet plus combined training moved every functional measure in frail, obese 72-year-olds — and the arm it beat was given the same drug

DEMFOS was designed to test a drug, and its most useful output for this vault is the comparison the drug was tested against. Two of its three arms did a supervised weight-management and exercise programme; the third got monthly one-hour group education about healthy eating. All three took metformin or placebo, and the two arms compared here were both on metformin — so the difference below is entirely the diet-and-training programme, with the pill held constant.

What the programme was. A 500–750 kcal/day energy deficit with about 1 g/kg bodyweight per day of protein and weekly dietitian review, aiming at 8–10% weight loss in six months; plus three supervised 90-minute sessions a week, each built as 15 min flexibility, 30 min aerobic, 30 min resistance training, 15 min balance. Aerobic work began near 65% of peak heart rate and progressed to 70–85% over four to six weeks; the machine was chosen per person as whatever caused the least joint discomfort. Resistance work was nine upper- and lower-body movements, starting at 1–2 sets of 8–12 repetitions at 65% of one-rep max and progressing to 2–3 sets at about 85%.

What it produced, in a cohort with a mean age of 72.8 and a mean of 2.7 chronic conditions.

  • Physical function — the modified physical performance test, nine scored everyday tasks out of 36: +2.5 points (1.2 to 3.8), p = 0.0002. About +12% against +4%.
  • Aerobic fitness — VO₂peak, the peak rate of oxygen consumption during a graded treadmill test: +3.4 mL/kg/min (2.0 to 4.9), roughly +19%, against essentially no change in the education arm.
  • Walking capacity — 6-minute walk distance +44.5 m (14.2 to 74.7).
  • Leg strength and power — isokinetic knee extensor peak torque +12.2 N·m (5.8 to 18.5), peak power +13.5 W (3.9 to 23.1).
  • Gait speed — +0.11 m/s (0.03 to 0.19), on a measure where roughly 0.05 m/s is usually treated as the smallest change people notice.
  • Weight — −7.8 kg (−10.2 to −5.2); fat mass −5.7 kg; MRI thigh fat volume −275 cm³.
  • Quality of life — SF-36 physical component +3.6 points (0.5 to 6.7); obesity-specific quality of life +7.0 points (1.3 to 12.7).

Adherence, which is the part that usually breaks in this population. Median 92% attendance at diet sessions and 85% at the three-times-weekly supervised exercise sessions, over six months, in sedentary 72-year-olds who mostly had hypertension and arthritis. This was heavily supported — weekly dietitian contact, qualified trainers, a Veterans Affairs facility — and the number should be read as what is achievable with that scaffolding, not without it.

Why the vault records this as extends rather than as news. That combined aerobic-plus-resistance training is the strongest exercise configuration is already the vault's position from a different literature and a different endpoint — aerobic-plus-resistance-lowest-mortality and mixed-and-strength-training-outrank-endurance-for-preserving-lean-mass. What DEMFOS adds is a randomised, six-month, hard-instrument demonstration in exactly the population where the argument for exercise is usually hedged: old, obese, frail, multimorbid. It sits alongside multidomain-intervention-improves-frailty-and-function-frail-elders on multidomain-lifestyle as a second randomised programme moving function in this group.

Two honest limits. The comparator was monthly education, not an active alternative programme — so the effect size is measured against near-nothing, which the vault has repeatedly found inflates estimates (a weak comparator makes any intervention look strong). And the population is 89% men at a single Veterans Affairs centre. The direction is not in doubt; the magnitude belongs to this comparison.

And it was not free. The same arms lost lean mass, thigh muscle volume and hip bone density — weight-loss-with-supervised-training-still-costs-lean-mass-and-hip-bone.
