The trustworthy read of the network: protein supplements give small gains, and nothing reliably beats whey

Strip the indirect-evidence collagen headline (collagen-ranking-rests-on-indirect-evidence) and Drummond 2026's 78-RCT network says what the vault already believes. Whey — the most-studied, best-anchored node — beats placebo by a small margin: fat-free mass SMD 0.16 (0.05–0.28), p = 0.005; strength SMD 0.15 (0.03–0.27), p = 0.015. Bovine colostrum, beef protein, and the plant proteins (pea, soy, rice) did not significantly beat placebo at all.

This confirms protein-target's thesis from a new angle. The map holds, from Morton's 49 RCTs, that protein added to resistance training gives small, real gains that plateau around ~1.6 g/kg/day (protein-supplementation-augments-resistance-training). Drummond, across a much wider supplement network, finds the credible per-supplement effects are exactly that size — small — and finds no protein source with trustworthy (direct-evidence) superiority over whey. The exotic option topping the SUCRA chart is an indirect-evidence artifact, not a reason to switch.

The decision-relevant compression: hit your daily total; the specific supplement you use to fill the gap is near-noise. The whole point of the daily-target lever is that the amount is what matters, not the brand, the amino-acid marketing, or the leaderboard position. A person hitting ~1.6 g/kg/day from food plus a third-party-tested whey to close the gap is doing the thing the evidence supports; a novel powder promising a larger effect on indirect evidence is not an upgrade.

Bias/quality note: short trials, body-composition surrogates, and the heterogeneity of a 13-node network; graded suggestive. The finding is used to reinforce the existing target, not to add a new number to it.
