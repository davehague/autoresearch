Long naps tracked with dying — over a two-year window, in people over 60

In 11,054 Chinese adults aged ≥60, a self-reported post-lunch nap of 60 minutes or longer carried all-cause mortality HR 1.289 (95% CI 1.055–1.576, p = 0.013) against no nap, adjusted for age, sex, education, marital status, smoking, alcohol and chronic-disease count. Unlike nighttime sleep duration, the nap association was linear — the restricted cubic spline found no non-linearity at all (p = 0.973) — so more nap tracked with more risk across the whole range, with no protective short-nap zone.

This is the vault's first quantitative claim about daytime sleep, which is why it is recorded despite being untrustworthy.

Graded open-question and capped single-study, and the reason is the follow-up length. The window is two years. Long daytime napping in adults over 60 is one of the best-established downstream markers of undiagnosed disease — occult cancer, heart failure, frailty, depression, systemic inflammation — all of which independently cause death. Over two years, the population most likely to be napping an hour a day at baseline overlaps heavily with the population already dying of something. The source is appraised unreliable on exactly this ground (shared/study-appraisal.md fatal flag #5: reverse causation is the more parsimonious explanation and the design cannot exclude it). The authors excluded deaths within six months as a sensitivity check; the conventional lag for this exposure is two to five years, which would exhaust the entire follow-up.

Three further limits, each of which points the same direction. The confounder set omits physical activity, body-mass index, depression, functional dependence and sleep-disordered breathing — every one of which causes both long napping and mortality. Chronic disease enters as a count, so well-controlled hypertension and metastatic cancer weigh the same. And the exposure is a single self-report of post-lunch napping only — naps at other times were not captured by the CHARLS item.

Read the direction, not the magnitude, and read even the direction as unresolved. A 29% relative hazard is well inside the range that unmeasured confounding and reverse causation can generate on their own; it is not the kind of large, dose-responsive effect that survives the observational discount (contrast daily-steps-mortality-dose-response, where HR 0.53 is far out of confounding range). The vault refused to coin a napping intervention map on this evidence — logged in docs/open-gaps-log.md with the standard for reconsidering: ≥5 years of follow-up, an explicit lag analysis, and adjustment for activity, depression and function.

What it does not license, plainly: this is not a reason to stop napping. Nothing here tests whether taking a nap changes anything. It reports that people who napped a lot were more likely to have died two years later.
