A null against yoga is not a null against sitting still

The eight trials pooled in this meta-analysis did not share a control arm. Alongside genuine wait-list and seated controls, walking was compared against yoga, educational lectures and health education, conventional treadmill training, resistance exercise, unsupervised individual practice, and — inside the interval-walking trials — a slower walk. Several of those are themselves plausible cognitive interventions.

What this changes about the reading. The paper's pooled conclusion is that walking training did not improve cognition (walking-training-null-on-cognition-in-older-adults). Across a mixed comparator set, the claim that estimate actually supports is closer to "walking is not measurably better than the other reasonable things an older adult might do" than to "walking does nothing." Those are different statements with different practical consequences, and the paper does not report an inactive-control-only subgroup that would let them be separated.

Which direction the confound runs. An active comparator makes an intervention harder to distinguish, so a mixed comparator set biases toward a null. That means this design feature is a reason the effect might be understated — the opposite direction from the usual worry about exercise trials, where an unblindable intervention against a do-nothing arm inflates the effect.

The vault holds both halves of this problem, from the same week. daily-steps records that walking ranked among the top modalities for sleep specifically against active controls (walking-among-top-sleep-modalities-against-active-controls) — treated as the harder and therefore more credible test. And exercise-cognition-mci-nma-liu-2026, ingested the same night, derives every one of its positive cognitive estimates from comparisons against a wait-list (exercise-improves-cognition-in-mci-against-wait-list-controls). Two exercise-and-cognition syntheses, opposite comparator choices, opposite answers. The comparator is not a footnote in this literature; it is a large part of what determines the result.

The reading habit this generates: before believing an exercise trial's effect size, find out what the control group was asked to do. An unblindable behaviour measured against people who were told to wait produces a different number from the same behaviour measured against people who were given something else to do.
