Nine of fourteen expert-consensus aging biomarkers predicted nothing

Of the 14 biomarkers of aging consented by an expert panel specifically for use as outcome measures in intervention studies, nine showed no significant association with all-cause mortality in BASE-II (n=1,083, 316 deaths, 7.4-year mean follow-up), in models adjusted for age, sex, lifestyle, and genetic ancestry:

CRP · gait speed · IGF-1 · blood pressure · muscle mass · DNAmGDF15 · frailty phenotype · Timed-Up-and-Go (with muscle strength distinct from the handgrip measure that did predict).

Only handgrip strength, IL-6, standing balance, cognitive health, and DunedinPACE reached significance.

Why this is a push-back on the field's measurement layer, not on any intervention. These are not obscure markers. Gait speed, the frailty phenotype, and Timed-Up-and-Go are staple primary and secondary endpoints in geriatric and geroscience trial design; blood pressure and CRP are among the most-measured variables in medicine. A trial in this cohort powered on gait speed or the frailty phenotype would have been powered on something that does not track the outcome anyone actually cares about. That is a serious problem for a panel nominated for exactly that purpose, and it is the most consequential thing in the source.

Two failures the vault should hold onto specifically. Muscle mass did not predict mortality — and in the same run, sarcopenic-obesity-exercise-nutrition-nma-yu-2026 found that no intervention moved skeletal muscle index either (sarcopenic-obesity-muscle-index-unmoved-by-every-intervention). Muscle mass is thus both the thing that would not move and the thing that would not predict, while grip strength is what moved and what predicted. And blood pressure's null here should be read narrowly: this is a 1,083-person cohort of 60–80-year-olds, not evidence against a vast hypertension literature — it is evidence that BP adds little within this panel, in this cohort, adjusted for age.

Bounded honestly (why suggestive, capped). Source reliability is mixed. These are nulls in a single convenience-recruited Berlin cohort of 1,083 people with restricted range (better-than-average health and socioeconomic status), which attenuates marker–mortality associations by construction. Fourteen simultaneous comparisons without stated correction cut both ways: they inflate false positives and leave true small effects undetected. This is an absence of demonstrated prediction in one cohort, not a demonstration that these markers are useless. No external replication is offered.
