The first deficiency-selected vitamin D trial here, and its pre-specified endpoint is flat

Young people aged 10–24 who had both major depression and low vitamin D received 4,800 IU of vitamin D3 daily for eight weeks or placebo. On the trial's pre-specified primary outcome — the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, administered by a trained clinician through a structured interview — the randomised vitamin D group was indistinguishable from placebo: B = −0.30 (95% CI −1.81 to 1.21), p = 0.698.

Why this specific null is worth recording rather than filing. vitamin-d sits at contested because four randomised outcome families have come back flat — cancer, cardiovascular disease, fractures, androgens — while the observational literature keeps insisting vitamin D matters. Every one of those trials tested people who were already replete, which left one live thread holding the map above fading: maybe supplementation works when the person is genuinely deficient (vitamin-d-deficiency-mortality-signal). This is the first source in the vault that selected for deficiency, and its primary endpoint is null. That makes it a fifth flat primary, and the first one inside the thread the map was relying on.

How far it goes, stated exactly. The deficiency thread in this vault is about all-cause mortality in adults, estimated from an observational target-trial emulation. This trial is about depressive symptoms in 10-to-24-year-olds over eight weeks. The exposure matches; the outcome family, the age band and the horizon do not. A null on adolescent mood is not a null on adult mortality. The gap narrows by a fraction and stays open — the same restraint the vault applied when it refused to close this gap on an androgen null eleven days earlier.

Read the instruments in the right order. The null is on the clinician-rated scale; the significant result is on a self-reported anxiety questionnaire (GAD-7, B = −1.78, −3.35 to −0.32). In a supplement trial, an effect that appears on self-report and not on assessor rating is the ordering that most invites an expectancy explanation, and it is the reverse of the ordering that would be reassuring. The paper's conclusion is drawn from an uncorrected severity subgroup of roughly eighteen versus sixteen people (vitamin-d-depression-conclusion-rests-on-an-uncorrected-subgroup-of-a-null-trial).

Graded suggestive, and read as a null that is not an absence. The source is appraised mixed, capping strength. The randomised comparison is 41 versus 39 people with no recoverable power analysis, so under this vault's standing rule — an underpowered null is not a demonstration of equivalence — this cannot show that vitamin D does nothing for depression in deficient young people. It shows that the pre-specified, properly randomised, clinician-rated comparison found nothing, which is a real result and a modest one. No baseline or achieved vitamin D levels are recoverable, so the trial cannot even confirm the deficiency was corrected.

Safety, recorded because it is what the trial establishes most cleanly. No excess hypercalcaemia or hyperphosphataemia at that dose over eight weeks. A dose used by a trial, not a dose recommended here (shared/safety.md).
