The most robust finding in this zinc review is a measurement of its own confounding

Of eighteen randomised trials and roughly twenty pooled outcomes, exactly two reached GRADE high certainty. One was a 5.70 mg/dL fall in total cholesterol. The other was that serum magnesium rose by 0.15 mg/dL (95% CI 0.08 to 0.22), I² = 0%, p < 0.00001.

⚑ Zinc does not raise serum magnesium. Magnesium does. The finding is high-certainty and perfectly consistent because the trials that contributed it gave both minerals, and giving someone magnesium reliably raises their magnesium. The review's single most internally consistent result is a direct measurement of the confounding that undermines every other result in it.

The authors state the problem plainly in their limitations:

"Several studies administered zinc alongside other micronutrients or bioactive compounds, including magnesium, vitamin D, calcium, curcumin and vitamin A. Because these agents may independently affect glycemic control, inflammation, oxidative stress and lipid metabolism, the isolated contribution of zinc could not be definitively determined."

The narrative confirms it trial by trial: Jamilian gave zinc + magnesium + calcium + vitamin D to women with gestational diabetes; Karandish gave zinc + curcumin for 90 days in type 2 diabetes; Shidfar gave zinc + vitamin A in type 1 diabetes. For each, the review notes that zinc's contribution cannot be separated.

⚑ Why this matters beyond one paper. Any pooled estimate in this review labelled "the effect of zinc" is really the effect of whatever those trials gave, averaged. The insulin-resistance findings that carry the abstract's conclusion — serum insulin and HOMA-IR — are exactly the outcomes on which this vault already holds a magnesium claim (magnesium-lowers-homa-ir-where-a-smaller-pool-could-not). ⚠ This is not a claim that magnesium produced zinc's HOMA-IR result. It is a statement that this review cannot rule it out, and that the review's own magnesium finding shows the co-administration was real and detectable.

⚠ A second, independent attribution problem: baseline zinc status. The authors record that it "was inconsistently assessed and reported across studies, precluding stratified analyses according to zinc deficiency or sufficiency," and note that responses "are likely to differ between zinc-deficient and zinc-replete individuals." ⚑ Repleting a deficiency and supplementing someone who is already replete are different interventions with different expected effects, and this review pooled them under one word. For a mineral, that is close to the whole question.

⚑ Why this note is filed on multivitamins and not on a zinc map. No zinc map was coined — the candidate scored 1 of 4 on this vault's coining bar, failing the isolating contrast, the risk-of-bias and the pre-specified-dose legs. This map is the correct home rather than a drawer, because its own founding caveat is the same defect: multivitamins was coined recording that "MVM is inconsistently defined" across trials, different formulations blurred into one label. This is that failure, one micronutrient down — and a useful demonstration that the problem is not solved by naming a single ingredient in the title.
