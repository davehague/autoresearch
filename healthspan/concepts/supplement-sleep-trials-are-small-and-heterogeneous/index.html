Twenty-eight trials of thirty people each is a small evidence base counted many times — and it biases the estimate upward

The trials pooled here enrolled 7 to 50 participants each. Heterogeneity was substantial on the headline outcome (PSQI, I² = 67%) and very high in the amino-acid subgroup (I² = 85%) containing L-theanine and GABA; the mineral subgroup ran I² = 52%. Sleep latency, WASO and awakenings showed I² = 0% — clean, but on the outcomes with the smallest effects. The paper's own publication-bias assessment fires: contour-enhanced funnel plots and Egger tests "reveal varying degrees of asymmetry" across outcomes, which the authors attribute to either publication bias or heterogeneity.

The reason this matters is directional, not decorative: this configuration inflates pooled estimates. Small trials are where small-study effects live. A 20-person supplement trial that finds nothing is substantially less likely to be written up than one that finds something — and here the meta-analysis tested for exactly that and found asymmetry. So the pooled PSQI −0.70 (supplement-sleep-effect-small-and-below-clinical-threshold) should be read as an upper estimate: the true category effect is more likely smaller than 0.70 than larger. Note the arithmetic consequence — the upper bound of that interval is already −0.03, so shrinking the estimate pushes the whole thing to the edge of nothing.

The authors' framing of the asymmetry deserves a sentence. Attributing it to "publication bias or heterogeneity" is technically correct and practically permissive: heterogeneity is the more comfortable explanation and it is the one that does not require discounting your own result. The vault records the asymmetry as detected, and does not adopt the reading that explains it away.

This has direct precedent in the vault, and it was decisive there. Kong 2026's walking-sleep meta-analysis ran the same tests and they fired at p < 0.001 (Egger z = −6.53, Begg p < 0.001), in a corpus with the same profile of small, open-label, self-reported trials (walking-subjective-sleep-quality-large-effect). Twice now, when this literature has been tested for publication bias, it has failed.

The heterogeneity lands hardest exactly where the vault would want to look. I² = 85% in the amino-acid subgroup means the trials of L-theanine and GABA disagree with each other more than they agree — so even if a per-substance estimate were extractable (supplement-sleep-benefit-not-attributable-to-any-supplement), it would not be stable. The subgroup structure does not rescue the attribution problem; it compounds it.

One thing the paper does right, and the vault should not flatten it: the low-heterogeneity outcomes (SL, WASO, NASO at I² = 0%) are genuinely consistent across trials. That consistency is real. It is also attached to the smallest effects in the paper and, in the case of awakenings, to a non-significant one — so what the corpus agrees on most cleanly is that not much happens.

Net effect on the vault: none of the wind-down maps move. A base of tiny, heterogeneous, publication-bias- untested trials cannot promote magnesium-glycinate, l-theanine, melatonin or chamomile, and it is not a well-powered null that could demote them either. It caps the category and leaves the members where they were.
