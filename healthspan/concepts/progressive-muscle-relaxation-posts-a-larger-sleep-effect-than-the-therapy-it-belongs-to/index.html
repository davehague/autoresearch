A component of the treatment outperformed the whole treatment, in a fourteenth of the evidence

The number. Across 14 randomised trials and 957 people, progressive muscle relaxation improved self-reported sleep quality by Hedges' g = −1.24 (95% CI −1.71 to −0.77). A standardised effect size divides the between-group difference by the pooled spread; by convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large. This is well past large.

⚑ Which is the problem, and the comparison that shows why is inside this vault. cbt-insomnia was coined on a PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis of 53 randomised trials and 13,608 participants — cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, the most intensively studied non-pharmacological sleep treatment there is, delivered over weeks by trained clinicians. Progressive muscle relaxation is one component of that programme, typically one module among sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring and sleep hygiene. ⚑ A single component, tested in 957 people, reporting a larger standardised effect than the complete therapy tested in 13,608, is a statement about the two literatures rather than about the two treatments.

⚠ What the smaller literature most plausibly measured. Twelve of the fourteen trials compared relaxation against routine care or no intervention at all (the-relaxation-sleep-effect-was-measured-almost-entirely-against-doing-nothing). Nobody can be blinded to whether they spent thirty minutes tensing and releasing their muscles. The outcome is a 19-item questionnaire asking people how they think they slept — no actigraphy, no polysomnography, no objective measure anywhere in the synthesis. Attention, ritual and expectancy against a no-treatment control, recorded on a self-report instrument, is a known machine for producing effect sizes of this size.

⚠ And the base has a hole in it that the paper itself measured. Egger's test for publication bias came back at p = 0.004; trim-and-fill imputed three missing studies and pulled the estimate to −0.92 (the-relaxation-sleep-literature-fails-its-own-publication-bias-test). I² was 85.5% and neither pre-specified subgroup split explained any of it — the authors say so: "despite our subgroup analysis, we have not yet identified the source of this heterogeneity."

⚑ One thing the paper gets right that its own abstract gets wrong. The abstract states relaxation "has no effect on older adults aged ≥55." The results section reports the formal subgroup-difference test as χ² = 0.17, df = 1, p = 0.679 — no difference between age groups. The ≥55 estimate (−1.17, −2.67 to +0.32) is not a smaller effect; it is four trials with a wider interval. The results are right and the abstract is wrong, and it is the abstract that gets quoted.

Where this lands. Appraised unreliable on fatal flag #2 (selective reporting in the pooled base), so this is capped single-study and moves nothing on any map. ⚑ A candidate progressive-muscle-relaxation map was scored and refused — filed here instead, because relaxation training is already a component of the intervention this map covers.

⚠ What this note is not saying. Not that relaxation does nothing. Even the bias-corrected −0.92 is a large number, and this is a free, harmless, equipment-free technique with no interaction surface. The claim is that this evidence base cannot establish the size of the effect — and size is what a decision layer needs.
