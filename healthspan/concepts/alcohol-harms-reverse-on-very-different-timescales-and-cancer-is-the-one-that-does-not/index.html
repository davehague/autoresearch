What you get back by drinking less, and how fast — the answers run from "immediately" to "you don't"

Most of what this vault records about alcohol is about risk accumulating. This note is about the other direction, and it is the part that decides whether a change is worth making. The timescales differ by orders of magnitude across outcomes.

HarmReversesEvidence
Injury, intoxication-mediated harm⚑ ImmediatelyRisk tracks blood alcohol concentration at the time, not history. Nothing carries over.
Acute immune suppressionShortly after abstainingBlocked granulocyte differentiation and suppressed myeloid proliferative response are transient.
Blood pressure⚑ Days to weeksDose-dependent rise; reduction lowers it. Alcohol reduction has been used as first-line treatment for incident hypertension in heavy drinkers.
Liver cirrhosis risk⚑ Immediately on reductionShown at population scale: sharp declines in cirrhosis mortality after the Gorbachev reforms and during the German occupation of Paris, when alcohol was confiscated. Also after tax increases.
Brain atrophyPartially, with sustained abstinenceCT studies show partial recovery of brain volume.
Chronic immune impairmentPartially, depending on volume and durationNatural killer T-cell populations are reduced by chronic use.
Established cirrhosis⚠ Often notReduction may slow progression and lower death risk without reversing the disease.
Cancer⚠ Largely not"Alcohol-related damage may already be too advanced for reductions in consumption to yield meaningful benefits." Alcohol may additionally promote progression via acetate, angiogenesis, invasion and impaired immune response.

⚑ The practical shape of this: reduction pays off fastest on the axes a healthy person can actually notice, and not at all on the one that would kill them. Blood pressure, sleep quality, injury risk and liver enzymes respond on a timescale of days to weeks. Cancer risk does not respond at all in any timeframe that helps.

Two things the natural experiments add that ordinary epidemiology cannot. The Gorbachev-era and wartime supply shocks are population-level interventions with abrupt onset — closer to a randomised design than anything else available in alcohol research, and they moved cirrhosis mortality sharply. And tax increases, a much milder intervention, are also associated with reduced cirrhosis mortality. Both are external shocks to exposure rather than individual choices, so healthy-user bias cannot explain them. ⚑ In a literature whose central weakness is that drinkers choose their drinking, these are the cleanest causal evidence in the paper.

Even for cancer, the population picture is not entirely static: reductions in population-level consumption and increases in abstention rates are associated with improved cancer outcomes, and hospital cohorts show reduced risk at several sites after alcohol treatment interventions. That is a population signal, not a promise to an individual.

A note on what is not here. This source says nothing about the reversibility of alcohol's effects on sleep — the axis GOAL.md weights most heavily — because it says nothing about sleep at all. Logged as an open gap rather than filled from general knowledge.
