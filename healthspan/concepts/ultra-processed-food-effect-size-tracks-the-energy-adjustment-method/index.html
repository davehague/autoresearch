The one thing that explains why UPF studies disagree is how they adjusted for calories

Mekonnen 2026 ran meta-regression on the pooled ultra-processed-food → cardiovascular association across sex, region, dietary assessment tool (FFQ versus 24-hour recall), UPF unit (calories versus weight), outcome type, event type (morbidity versus mortality) and follow-up length (<10 versus ≥10 years). None of them explained anything.

One moderator did: the statistical technique used to adjust for total energy intake (QM = 8.23, df = 3, p = 0.042). Together the tested moderators account for R² = 99.98% of between-study heterogeneity, collapsing residual I² from 45.2% to 0.01% — that is, once you know how a study handled calories, you know essentially everything about why its answer differs from the others'.

The direction is the uncomfortable part. Studies using the standard model — total energy simply entered as another covariate — report estimates RR 1.04 (95% CI 1.002–1.07, p = 0.038) higher than studies using other approaches. And the pooled estimate is significantly more attenuated among studies applying both the residual and the standard method (p = 0.009), which is the more careful of the two practices.

Set that against the headline. The total pooled effect is RR 1.09. The difference attributable to a choice made inside a statistics package is RR 1.04 — roughly half the effect being explained.

Why energy adjustment is not a technicality here. UPF intake is usually expressed as a share of what someone eats. People who eat a lot of ultra-processed food also, on average, eat more calories. If you adjust for total energy one way, you are asking "does swapping processed calories for unprocessed ones matter?"; adjust another way and you are asking "does eating more processed food matter, holding total food constant?" Those are different questions with different answers, and the literature has been answering whichever one its authors' model implied — mostly without saying which.

This does not show the association is an artefact. It shows that the size of the association is not a stable property of the exposure, and that the studies making the weakest analytic choice report the largest effect.
