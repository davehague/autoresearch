Swap polysomnography for a questionnaire and the insomnia mortality signal disappears

Running the identical analysis with self-reported short sleep (<7 h) in place of polysomnography-measured short sleep (<6 h), in the same 767 people over the same follow-up, insomnia symptoms with self-reported short sleep were not associated with all-cause mortality. The objectively-measured version of the same contrast gave HR 2.57 (1.12–5.89).

Why this matters for the vault. This is the most extreme instance yet of a pattern the vault has now seen in five separate literatures: change the measuring instrument and the answer changes. In the earlier four cases the effect shrank — fat-free mass predicts mortality nearly twice as strongly by tape measure as by bioimpedance (fat-free-mass-measurement-method-gradient); wrist step counters read 15–30% above hip-worn ones in the same person on the same day (wrist-worn-step-counts-run-15-to-30-percent-above-hip-worn); self-reported sleep duration keeps the shape of the mortality curve and loses its magnitude against an accelerometer (self-reported-sleep-duration-preserves-shape-loses-magnitude); creatine appears to lower kidney filtration by one measure and does not move it at all by the tracer method (creatine-lowers-estimated-gfr-without-lowering-measured-gfr). Here the effect does not shrink — the instrument determines whether there is an effect to see.

The consequence for reading the literature is concrete: every cohort study that measured sleep duration by questionnaire was structurally unable to identify the subgroup this analysis found at risk. That includes the great majority of the evidence behind short-sleep-under-7h-mortality-risk.

Bounded honestly (why suggestive). The two arms of the comparison use different thresholds — <6 h objective versus <7 h self-reported. Those are the conventional cut-points in each literature, chosen because people over-report sleep relative to polysomnography, but they were not derived in this paper, so part of the self-report null could be threshold choice rather than instrument error and nothing retrievable excludes that. The sample is 767 with four cells, the full text is paywalled, and a null in a small sample is never a demonstration of absence. What makes this the more credible half of the paper is that a null arising from an instrument swap inside the same people is much harder to explain away than a positive result in one cell of four.

The practical shape of the claim. It is a reading rule, not an action: when a sleep finding is built on "how many hours do you usually sleep?", treat the exposure as a different variable from measured sleep, not a noisier version of it.
