The model predicts the benefit shrinks to nothing — and then reverses — as blood sugar gets closer to normal

Fourteen meta-regressions were run: seven continuous moderators across two outcomes, each fitted three ways with the best form chosen by cross-validation. Eleven found nothing. Three produced credible intervals excluding zero, and the only one that replicated across both outcomes was baseline glycaemia.

⚑ As baseline HbA1c rises from 7% to 10%, the predicted effect of yoga moves monotonically from about +0.4% to about −0.5%. As baseline fasting glucose rises from 7.5 to 11 mmol/L, the predicted reduction strengthens from roughly zero to roughly 2.5 mmol/L.

Read the left end of that curve. At a baseline HbA1c of 7% — which is well-controlled diabetes, and far above what a person without diabetes would show — the model predicts yoga makes the number slightly worse than the control condition. The entire benefit lives at the poorly-controlled end.

This shape is believable, and that is the point. Two ordinary mechanisms produce it. Regression to the mean: people recruited with high values drift downward on remeasurement whatever you do to them. Headroom: you cannot lower a number that is already near its floor. ⚑ Both operate in the control arm too, which is why a randomised comparison should in principle cancel them — but with waiting-list controls, no blinding, and behavioural spillover into the treatment arm (twenty-eight-yoga-trials-and-not-one-active-control), the cancellation is not guaranteed.

⚠ It also matters that these are study-level associations, not individual ones. The curve is fitted across trial averages, so reading it as "a person with HbA1c of 9% will benefit more than a person at 7%" is an ecological inference — the study-level pattern need not hold within individuals. The authors are careful about this elsewhere and the caution applies here too.

⚑ The two dose results are the reason this note is filed as pushing back rather than as a scope condition. A physical practice should show more benefit the more of it you do. It does not. Split at the World Health Organization threshold of 600 MET-minutes per week — the standard activity-guideline cut-point — the HbA1c effect was −0.63% above the threshold and −0.63% below it. Identical. And the one dose gradient the analysis found runs the wrong way: shorter sessions predicted larger effects (standardised β = +0.95), driven by a trial of ten participants prescribing ten-minute sessions, which was the only fasting-glucose trial rated at high overall risk of bias. Excluding it attenuated the coefficient to 0.67 but did not remove it.

⚑ This vault has seen this exact shape before and drew the same conclusion. The coenzyme-q10 candidate was refused on 2026-08-26 because its benefits got smaller as the dose got larger across five outcomes; ginger was refused on 2026-08-15 on a nonlinear coefficient opposing its own pooled effect. An intervention whose measured effect is flat in dose and larger in shorter sessions is displaying the signature of small-study effects and bias, not a physiological response — which is consistent with the finding that the HbA1c estimate crosses zero once small-study bias is corrected for (the-yoga-hba1c-effect-cannot-be-distinguished-from-zero-after-adjusting-for-small-study-effects).

⚠ To be fair to the paper: the authors say all of this themselves. They call the session-duration result exploratory, robustness-check it three ways, attribute it partly to the one small trial, and explicitly decline to use it to recommend a duration. They also correctly note that the absent subgroup effects are absence of evidence for effect modification, not evidence of equivalence — the strata were small and underpowered.

In one sentence: whatever this literature is measuring gets bigger as baseline blood sugar gets worse and does not get bigger as you do more yoga, which is the profile of a statistical artefact rather than a training effect.
