Training during a calorie deficit preserves about half the fat-free mass you would otherwise lose — and does not cost you the weight loss

A PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis of 34 head-to-head RCTs (n=1,455) in overweight/obese adults found that adding exercise to calorie restriction preserved +0.87 kg of fat-free mass (95% CI +0.59, +1.16; p ≤ 0.001) versus dieting alone — roughly 45.7% of the FFM loss prevented (the abstract's number; the paper's discussion says 54%, and the vault records both rather than choosing). Body mass fell slightly more with exercise (−0.88 kg), so the preservation is not bought by dieting less hard.

The obvious confound was tested and rejected, which is the finding's best feature. If exercise preserved lean mass merely by blunting weight loss, the effect should scale with weight-loss magnitude. Three separate meta-regression models — weight change in the control arm, in the intervention arm, and the between-group difference — found no moderation, with R² = 0% in each. Number of training sessions did not explain it either. Whatever training is doing here, it is not arithmetic.

Hold the number loosely, because the authors say so. They detected publication bias on this very outcome (funnel asymmetry, significant Egger's test) and state the pooled effect may be overestimated — without offering a corrected estimate. Trial quality is fair (PEDro mean 5; RoB 2: 59% some concerns, 41% high risk), heterogeneity is moderate (I² = 62.1%), and FFM was measured by five different instruments including BIA in 9 of 34 trials — the method fat-free-mass-measurement-method-gradient already identified as the weakest, and the one most disturbed by the body-water shifts that active weight loss causes. Capped suggestive.

And it is fat-free mass, not muscle. Only 2 of 34 trials reported skeletal muscle mass. FFM includes water, bone, organs and connective tissue; during a deficit those move independently of muscle. The muscle claim is inference, not measurement.

What it changes on this map, and what it does not. fat-free-mass exists as a marker literature and states that nothing on it is actionable. This is the first source on it that is: FFM during weight loss is modifiable. It does not close the standing GLP-1 gap — that gap asks for muscle function during pharmacologic weight loss, and this trial set has no functional outcome and no GLP-1 population. The pre-refusal on that transfer stands. Status held emerging.
