"Plant protein ranked lower" is what the table says; "plant protein doesn't work" is not what the evidence says

What the network found. Soy (+0.55 kg), pea (+0.48 kg), blends (+0.62 kg) and rice (+0.96 kg) all pointed positive against placebo and none of their credible intervals cleared zero, while whey (+1.24), milk (+1.18) and casein (+0.92) did. A reader stopping at the SUCRA column gets "dairy works, plants don't."

⚠ Four facts that make that reading unavailable.

  1. Node size. Soy 5 trials, pea 3, blend 3, rice 2. Whey vs placebo alone had 14.
  2. Certainty. CINeMA graded soy low and pea, rice and blend very low against placebo — the two lowest rungs the framework has.
  3. Fragility. The paper's own threshold analysis says one or two additional null trials would displace rice, blend, pea or soy from their positions. Whey would need eight.
  4. Indirectness. For comparisons like rice-versus-blend, more than 95% of the pooled estimate came from indirect evidence routed through placebo — the authors flag it as "a point that warrants interpretive caution."

⚑ The authors go further and offer a methodological explanation rather than a biological one. They note the mechanistic case — soy has 7–8% leucine against whey's 13%, a protein-digestibility-corrected amino acid score of ~0.91 against 1.00, and trypsin inhibitors — and then immediately undercut how much of the observed gap it explains: one trial found comparable biceps thickness gains between pea and whey over 12 weeks, and another found no difference in lean mass or strength between a high-protein plant-based diet and a protein-matched omnivorous diet over 12 weeks of resistance training. Their diagnosis, verbatim: "The discrepancy may be partly methodological: trials matching total protein intake closely (rather than supplementing a fixed dose on top of habitual diet) tend to narrow the gap between animal and plant sources." ⚑ The gap may be a dosing artefact — matched-total trials shrink it, fixed-supplement trials widen it — which is the same baseline-intake confound that runs under the whole paper (protein-dose-response-cannot-separate-supplementing-from-replacing-without-baseline-intake).

⚠ And the search itself may have selected against the plant nodes. Database searching had no language restriction but only English-language full texts were eligible, which the authors note "may have introduced language bias by excluding relevant trials published in other languages, particularly studies from East Asian populations where plant-based protein supplementation is more common." The thinnest nodes in the network are the ones the eligibility rule was most likely to thin.

Where the authors land, and the vault agrees. Their practice guidance for vegetarians and vegans is a refusal to give guidance: "based on the current evidence (rated low to very low certainty), no firm recommendation can be made regarding specific plant-based protein types or doses." ⚑ This is an absence of evidence, and the paper labels it as one.

Capped suggestive. stance: open-question — the map does not move on plant protein in either direction.
