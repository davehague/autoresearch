Where fitness has been tested as a classifier, it lands at "acceptable" — not at what a five-fold risk gradient suggests

In the most favourable case available — predicting severe cardiac events in people who already have systolic heart failure — cardiorespiratory fitness reaches diagnostic odds ratio 4.1–8.1 and area under the curve 0.73–0.77, at moderate certainty. An AUC of 0.75 means that if you pick one person who has an event and one who doesn't, the fitness measure ranks them correctly about three times in four. That is conventionally called acceptable. Coin-flip is 0.5.

Detecting coronary artery disease is weaker and much less certain: dOR 2.3–7.6, AUC 0.66–0.79, at very low certainty — downgraded for risk of bias, heterogeneity, inconsistency and indirectness. Detecting mitochondrial disorders is the standout at dOR 18.1–26.6 and high certainty, but that is a rare disease question with no bearing on healthspan.

⚑ One quieter finding. Among adults without known disease, exercise testing was better at confirming coronary artery disease than at ruling it out — a bad result is informative, a good result is not very reassuring. That is the opposite of how a screening test is usually wanted to behave, and it carries very low certainty.

Two limits on all of it. The quoted ranges are spans across different fitness measures, not pooled estimates with intervals — only qualitative synthesis was possible. And indirectness was named in three of four GRADE downgrades: accuracy in heart-failure patients says little about a healthy adult, whose pre-test probability of disease is entirely different (crf-diagnostic-evidence-is-absent-for-healthy-adults).
