Falls happened less often; the number of people falling mostly did not change

These are two different claims and the review separates them cleanly. Fall rate — events per person-year — drops across most comparisons (IRR 0.41 to 0.60). The number of people who fall at least once mostly does not: null at 3 months, null at 6 months, RR 1.38 (0.62–3.06) against real strength-and-balance training, and significant at 12 months in exactly one study (RR 0.75, 0.61–0.92) — which the paper's own GRADE table rates low certainty.

⚠ And the outcome that carries the healthspan consequence is essentially unmeasured. People experiencing one or more injurious falls: one trial, n = 507, RR 0.81 (0.61–1.08) — not significant. Neither active-comparator comparison reported it at all.

Why the distinction is not pedantry. A rate reduction driven by frequent fallers falling somewhat less often is a real but modest good. "Fewer people fell" and "fewer people were injured falling" are the claims that change a healthspan, because it is the fracture — not the fall count — that ends independence. Exactly one low-certainty estimate in this paper supports the second claim, and none supports the third.

The parallel already on this map is close enough to be worth stating. unstable-rt-reduces-fear-of-falling-not-falls recorded that unstable-surface resistance training moved fear of falling without moving falls — a substitution of the measurable proxy for the outcome. This is the same substitution one rung further along: fall rate standing in for people-who-fall, standing in for people-who-get-hurt. The vault now holds three tiers of this ladder in one literature, and concerns about falling were null here too (SMD −0.28, −1.18 to 0.61 at 12 weeks; −0.10, −0.23 to 0.02 at 12 months).

⚠ How the falls were counted matters here more than usual. No trial could blind participants, and in several the ascertainment is a self-reported fall diary — an unblinded participant classifying and recording their own events. The vault has measured what that costs elsewhere: loneliness's health association halved on objective measurement (loneliness-health-association-halves-on-objective-measures), and cold-water immersion was objectively null while its subjective benefits failed the authors' own bias tests (cold-water-immersion-damage-marker-benefit-fails-bias-adjustment). A fall is more objective than a mood score and less objective than a fracture, and the one outcome here that could not be misremembered — injurious falls — is the one that came back non-significant.

Graded open-question — it qualifies the review's own headline framing of exergaming as fall prevention rather than contradicting any existing vault claim, which is this vault's convention for a reporting critique. shared/safety.md says an over-cautious flag beats a missed one; no safety flag is raised, because no serious adverse events occurred in any trial and the finding is about efficacy, not harm.
