In a review about the Mediterranean diet, the largest number belongs to moving

A hazard ratio compares the rate at which something happens in two groups over time. HR 0.64 means one group accumulated deaths at 64% of the other group's rate — a 36% lower rate.

Across at least 260,354 people who already had coronary heart disease, the same reviewers measured diet and movement side by side, with the same methods, in the same patients:

⚑ Movement wins on three axes at once: effect size, number of contributing studies, and agreement between them. The activity estimate for all-cause mortality has the lowest heterogeneity in the entire paper (I² = 29%, meaning most of the variation between studies is chance rather than real disagreement) and rests on 11 studies. The diet estimate rests on four.

⚠ Both are observational, and the same bias threatens both — but it threatens the activity estimate harder. Healthy-adherer bias is the tendency for people who stick to any regimen to do better regardless of the regimen. In a post-heart-attack population, "maintained or continued physical activity versus persistent inactivity" is uncomfortably close to a measure of who stayed well enough to keep moving. Reverse causation runs the same way: worsening disease reduces exercise, and then kills. The 0.42 cardiovascular-mortality estimate is the single most exposed number here, and it should not be read as "exercise halves cardiac death in heart patients."

The certainty grade says as much. All observational evidence in this review was rated GRADE very low, and ROBINS-E rated 25 of the 30 observational studies at high risk of bias, with none at low (twenty-five-of-thirty-observational-studies-in-this-synthesis-are-high-risk-of-bias).

And the pooled magnitudes are unstable even where the direction is not. Leave-one-out analysis — removing each study in turn — preserved direction and significance on every estimate, but the numbers moved: the activity all-cause estimate ranged 0.59 to 0.66, the cardiovascular one 0.33 to 0.53. ⚑ More tellingly, on three of four activity pools, removing a single study collapsed heterogeneity from 80–90% to zero — one study was carrying nearly all the disagreement in each pool.

⚑ Why this is worth keeping despite all of that. The comparison is internal. The usual objection to ranking diet against exercise is that the estimates come from different literatures, different populations and different reviewers. Here they come from the same review, the same patients and the same methods, so the shared biases largely cancel in the comparison even though they inflate both absolute numbers. This vault's GOAL.md already orders behavioural levers above dietary ones; this is a third independent literature landing in the same place.

⚠ What it does not say. It says nothing about primary prevention — nobody here was healthy. It says nothing about a specific exercise prescription: the authors explicitly warn that each pooled estimate is "an average association within a specific exposure contrast" and should not be generalised to any particular activity intervention. And the diet estimate being smaller does not mean the diet does less; it means four high-risk-of-bias cohorts measured it less precisely.

In one sentence: when eating well and continuing to move are measured in the same patients by the same reviewers, moving produces the bigger, tighter and better-replicated association — and both estimates sit on evidence graded very low.
