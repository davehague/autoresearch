"More bioavailable" creatine has never been tested against monohydrate at an equal dose

Across 343 distinct randomised controlled trials of oral creatine published between 1994 and 2023:

  • Bioavailability is mentioned in 38 trials (11.1%), most often in passing in the Discussion (28 trials, 8.2%).
  • Five trials (1.5%) ever compared two creatine formulations head to head.
  • Three (0.9%) did so while discussing bioavailability. They pitted creatine monohydrate against creatyl-L-leucine, creatine nitrate, and polyethylene-glycosylated (PEG) creatine hydrochloride.
  • In all three, the significant increase in muscle creatine stores or power occurred only in the monohydrate arm.

⚑ And in all three, the monohydrate arm was given more creatine. Not more product — more creatine, by moles, after correcting for the fact that different salts and conjugates carry different fractions of actual creatine per gram:

comparisonmonohydrate's molar advantage
vs creatyl-L-leucine1.6×
vs creatine nitrate1.3–4.3×
vs PEG-creatine HCl2× or 4×

The worked example: 3 g/day of creatine monohydrate is 20.11 mmol of creatine. The comparator arms received 7.73 mmol (low-dose creatine nitrate) or 15.45 mmol (high-dose) — so participants in the monohydrate group got 2.6× and 1.3× more of the actual molecule. The review's explanation is that these trials were designed to compare supplementation regimens as sold, not to compare bioavailability, and nobody equalised the dose.

What follows, and what does not.

  • The marketing claim that premium formulations are better absorbed than monohydrate has no supporting evidence. The review's closing line is that these claims "are not adequately supported by evidence from clinical trials."
  • Nor has monohydrate been shown superior. It won three comparisons in which it was handed 1.3–4.3× the dose. That is not a demonstration; it is an uncontrolled contrast.
  • The practical answer is unchanged and now better grounded: buy the cheap, well-characterised form. Not because it beat the alternatives, but because it is the form 80.2% of the evidence base actually used, and nothing has beaten it.

One further gap: only four trials in 343 reported any strategy to improve uptake at all (dissolving in warm water, or co-ingesting with food or maltodextrin), and only two ever aimed at raising creatine in the brain.
