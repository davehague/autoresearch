The trial's positive finding is self-reported, and its own machine-read check did not confirm it

DELISH missed its primary weight endpoint and reports a secondary instead: diet quality improved substantially more in the Mediterranean arm. By food-frequency questionnaire at 24 months, the Alternative Healthy Eating Index-2010 was 3.62 points higher (1.11 to 6.12, P = .005), the DASH score 1.91 higher and the alternate-Mediterranean index 1.15 higher, with more monounsaturated fat (+2.3% of energy), more polyunsaturated fat (+0.9%) and less carbohydrate (−1.9%). ⚠ Saturated fat did not differ (0.1%, −0.6 to 0.8).

⚑ The trial also measured diet quality with an instrument that does not care what participants say. A Veggie Meter reads carotenoid pigment accumulated in the skin by reflection spectroscopy — an objective proxy for weeks of fruit and vegetable intake, and a rare inclusion in a behavioural weight-loss trial. It did not favour the Mediterranean arm at any time point:

The authors' explanation for the 4-month gap is reasonable and does not cover the rest. The WeightWatchers programme "had a major focus on increasing nonstarchy vegetables and fruits, while the Mediterranean-style intervention focused on all dietary components" — so a carotenoid-specific advantage early is unsurprising. ⚑ But at 24 months, the arm carrying a 3.62-point AHEI advantage and a 1.15-point Mediterranean-index advantage has a skin carotenoid difference of −0.94 points. On the one dietary measure a participant cannot talk their way into, the two arms are identical.

Why this matters more than the usual self-report caveat. The trial was open-label, and the Mediterranean arm spent its first four months being counselled specifically on what a high-quality diet looks like — which is simultaneously the intervention and a four-month training course in how to fill out a food-frequency questionnaire favourably. Social desirability bias is not a generic footnote here; it is the mechanism most likely to produce exactly this pattern of results. The authors name recall and social-desirability bias in their limitations.

⚑ The reading rule this licenses, stated generally. When a trial reports a self-reported outcome and a machine-measured outcome of the same construct and they disagree, the machine is the finding. This vault has the mirror-image rule already: diet-adherence-biomarkers-validate-exposure-not-causal-inference — a nutrient biomarker proves the food was eaten and says nothing about who eats it. DELISH completes the pair: when the biomarker is silent and the questionnaire is loud, the questionnaire is measuring something other than the food.

⚠ Read the disagreement fairly in both directions. Skin carotenoids track carotenoid-rich produce, not the Mediterranean pattern as a whole — olive oil, nuts, fish and whole grains carry no carotenoid signal, and the self-reported changes that were largest (monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fat) are precisely the components this instrument cannot see. So this is a failed corroboration, not a refutation. What it removes is the ability to claim the diet-quality result was objectively confirmed, which is what a reader would otherwise assume from a trial that carried an objective biomarker. Neither the diet-quality result nor the carotenoid result carries multiplicity correction; Table 3 holds roughly fifteen outcomes at three time points with none applied.

Capped suggestive on a mixed appraisal.
