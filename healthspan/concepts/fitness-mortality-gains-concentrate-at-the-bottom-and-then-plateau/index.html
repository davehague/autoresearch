Most of the survival difference is between the least-fit fifth and everyone else — and three of the tests flatten out entirely

The quintile gradients in Wu 2026 are monotonic, but they are not evenly spaced. The authors state where the difference lives: "Most of the mortality differential was concentrated between the lowest quintile and the upper quintiles… suggesting that the greatest health gains may occur when individuals move out of the lowest-performing stratum. This pattern supports prioritizing intervention for the least-fit older adults."

⚑ Restricted cubic splines make the same point with numbers. Modelling each test as a continuous raw value rather than in fifths, four assessments showed statistically significant nonlinearity, and in each the curve fell steeply at low performance and then flattened:

  • 30-second chair stand (men): plateau at roughly 15 repetitions.
  • Back scratch (both sexes): plateau at roughly −10 cm.
  • 8-foot up-and-go (women): plateau at roughly 7–8 seconds.

Past those points, better performance was not associated with further reductions in mortality risk.

⚑ This is the same shape the vault already recorded from an entirely different exposure. aerobic-volume-mortality-dose-response-nonlinear and moderate-activity-75min-week-mortality-minimum describe a front-loaded curve for aerobic activity volume — most of the benefit arriving early, with a floor around 75 minutes per week and the bulk of the return banked by roughly 150. One of those literatures measures self-reported behaviour and the other measures administered performance tests; they produce the same curve. Two independent measurement approaches converging on "the return is in getting off the floor" is worth more than either finding alone.

⚠ What this does not license. A plateau in an observational curve is not evidence that training past the plateau is useless — it is evidence that, in this cohort, people above it did not die at measurably different rates. Performance above the plateau also carries less statistical power (fewer deaths, sparser data at the extremes), and the endpoint here is death, not capability, injury resistance, or the ability to do a thing you want to do. A person training for pickleball is not training for the mortality curve.

⚠ And reverse causation bites hardest exactly where the curve is steepest. The steep part of the curve is the low-performance end, which is also where undiagnosed illness clusters. Some of that slope is the disease that is about to kill people showing up in their legs first.

The reading that survives: the biggest measurable difference in this cohort is between doing badly and doing adequately, not between doing adequately and doing exceptionally — which, under GOAL.md's least-work-for-maximum-benefit rule, is the more useful half of the finding anyway.
