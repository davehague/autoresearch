The sleep window predicted mortality better than time actually asleep

Of the three exposure metrics compared in the same participants, the strongest association with all-cause mortality came from sleep period time — the sleep window, which includes wakefulness after sleep onset — not from total sleep time, which counts only minutes actually asleep. At 7 hours versus a 5-hour reference, sleep period time gave HR 0.70 (0.61–0.79) against total sleep time's HR 0.83 (0.78–0.89). Sleep period time also produced a much sharper optimum (~7.7 h) than total sleep time, whose risk-minimum sprawled across 6.8–9.3 h. The authors suggest sleep period time "may be more relevant than total sleep time for future device-based recommendations."

The tempting reading is wrong, and the vault should say so plainly. "Time in bed beats time asleep" would license the conclusion that lying awake is as good as sleeping, which nothing here supports and which is biologically implausible.

The disciplined reading points somewhere else. Sleep period time is largely a measure of when you go to bed and when you get up — a sleep-opportunity and timing variable — whereas total sleep time is contaminated by sleep fragmentation, which is itself a downstream marker of illness. So the metric that won is the one closest to a behavioural regularity measure, and the metric that lost is the one most polluted by health status. If that interpretation is right, this result quietly argues for sleep-regularity's primacy rather than for a duration target — the actionable variable is the schedule you set, not the minutes you achieve.

Recorded as an open question, not a finding. This analysis does not test that interpretation: it reports which metric predicted best, not why. It also does not compare sleep period time against the Sleep Regularity Index directly, which is the comparison that would settle it. Logged as a gap.
