Two years of a dietitian-delivered Mediterranean programme lost the same weight as two years of WeightWatchers

The comparison, precisely. DELISH randomised 360 primary-care patients with obesity to Med-South — a three-phase, 24-month, registered-dietitian-delivered programme promoting an adapted Mediterranean-style eating pattern — or to two years of free access to the WW (WeightWatchers) workshop and digital programme. Both arms received the intervention at no cost and both were advised to do regular moderate-intensity physical activity. At 24 months, intention-to-treat with 200 imputations for missing weight:

Read the interval, not the P value. A 95% confidence interval running −1.79 to +1.57 percentage points is not a shrug. It says the data are incompatible with either programme beating the other by more than about 1.8% of body weight — for a 165 lb adult, under three pounds at the most extreme edge the data allow. ⚑ This is a bounded null rather than a failure to detect, and it stayed bounded across sixteen analysis cells: unadjusted, adjusted, excluding participants who started weight-loss medication or had bariatric surgery, and with missing weights recovered from the electronic health record.

Why it counts as a null and not a failure. The trial was powered to detect a 4-percentage-point difference at an assumed standard deviation of 7.3% — a requirement of roughly 50 people per arm. It randomised 180 per arm. ⚑ The result is a trial that overshot its own power requirement by more than threefold and therefore produced an interval narrow enough to exclude anything worth acting on (the-weight-loss-control-arm-outperformed-its-own-design-assumption-threefold).

⚠ The scope of the claim, stated tightly. This is not evidence that the Mediterranean pattern does nothing. mediterranean-diet's load-bearing claim is all-cause mortality across 54 cohorts and 1,833,267 adults, supported by PREDIMED in the wider randomised literature (mediterranean-diet-lowers-all-cause-mortality). This trial tested a different and adjacent proposition — that a Mediterranean-style programme is a better weight-loss tool than a good commercial one — and found it is not. The map never claimed otherwise, and stance: open-question rather than pushes-back reflects that.

⚠ Two limits on how far to lean. The trial was open-label; and delivery was cut by the pandemic from a planned 28 in-person counselling visits to 5, with per-protocol adherence falling to 38% (Mediterranean) and 34% (control) in the final phase. The 24-month comparison is between two largely lapsed programmes, which is realistic and is also a ceiling. There was no usual-care arm, so neither arm's loss can be separated from secular trends over a period when GLP-1 receptor agonists were diffusing through primary care.

⚑ The trajectory is worth keeping. The Mediterranean arm spent its first four months on diet quality with no weight-loss goal and was 3.44 percentage points behind at 4 months (P < .001). It had caught up completely by 12 months and stayed level to 24. Changing how one eats before trying to lose weight cost nothing at two years in this trial.

Capped suggestive on a mixed appraisal.
