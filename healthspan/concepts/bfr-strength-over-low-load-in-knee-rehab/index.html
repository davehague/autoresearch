Adding the cuff to light loading builds strength — but not, here, pain relief

Against low-load training without occlusion, the cuff earns its keep on strength: SMD 1.11 (95% CI 0.67 to 1.55), p < 0.00001 (5 trials, n=324), in middle-aged/elderly OA and post-arthroplasty knees. Preoperative BFR also improved postoperative strength (SMD 0.97, 0.07 to 1.87, p = 0.03). Pain, though, did not move vs low-load control (SMD −0.10, −0.36 to 0.16, p = 0.46).

The strength result is the sturdiest thing on the BFR map, now replicated three ways across three populations: Xu (bfr-adds-lower-limb-strength-in-athletes, athletes, SMD 1.09), Hou (bfr-reduces-patellofemoral-pain-during-loaded-tasks carries a strength gain SMD 0.28 in knee-pain patients), and Chen (OA/TKA, SMD 1.11 vs low-load control). Adding occlusion to light training reliably adds strength — that thread is real.

The pain null needs reading precisely, because it looks like it contradicts Hou and it does not. Hou found BFR a clinically meaningful analgesic — but during loaded tasks in patellofemoral pain, and against comparators that were not themselves potent analgesics. Chen's comparator is low-load exercise, which is itself an established pain-reliever for knee OA, and the population is OA/TKA, not PFPS. So "no pain difference vs low-load control" means the cuff adds strength on top of the exercise's own analgesia, without adding further pain relief — a null against an active analgesic, not a failure of BFR to relieve pain. The two findings are compatible: BFR's reliable contribution is strength; its analgesic contribution is context-dependent.

For David: confirms that if a physio ever prescribes low-load BFR for the healing knee, the strength benefit over plain light loading is well-replicated — while the pain benefit is not something to bank on in an OA-type presentation.
