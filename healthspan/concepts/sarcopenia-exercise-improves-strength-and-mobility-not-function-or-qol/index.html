In diagnosed sarcopenia, exercise reliably moves strength, gait speed and balance tests, but not functional ability or quality of life

A 12-RCT meta-analysis (n=652) of resistance, balance, and aerobic exercise in older adults diagnosed with sarcopenia found significant improvement in handgrip strength (SMD 0.39), knee extension strength (SMD 0.62), gait speed (SMD 0.66), and timed-up-and-go (SMD −0.73) — but no significant difference in functional ability or quality of life between exercise and control groups.

Why this matters for the vault. This is another instance of the surrogate-moved-patient-centered- outcome-didn't dissociation fat-free-mass and protein-target already carry from Roschel 2021 (lean mass rose; strength, function and frailty status did not, in frail elders given protein+leucine+creatine on top of resistance training). Here the direction is partly reversed — strength did move — but the same gap appears one level up: physical-performance tests improved while the patient-reported outcomes that are supposed to follow from them did not. Filed on resistance-training-programming as a population boundary (diagnosed sarcopenia), parallel to Jin-Hao 2026's existing sarcopenia-specific load-axis finding there; cross-linked to balance-training for the gait-speed/TUG overlap.

Bounded honestly. The review does not decompose resistance-alone versus combined (resistance+aerobic+ balance) arms in its reported results, so which modality drove which specific gain is not established. No stated risk-of-bias tool or GRADE certainty grading is given in the retrievable abstract.

The practical shape of the claim. Structured exercise in diagnosed sarcopenia reliably builds measurable strength and mobility; whether that translates into a person's reported functional ability or quality of life is not yet shown by this literature. No prescription or dose implied.
