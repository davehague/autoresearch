Doing the pooling honestly is what made the bias check impossible

Two problems in meta-analysis pull in opposite directions, and this review is a clean illustration of the tension.

Problem one: pooling things that are not the same thing. Studies of "physical activity and mortality" measure wildly different contrasts — some compare people who kept exercising against people who never did, some compare people who met a guideline threshold against inactive people, some just split their own sample into higher and lower. Those are different questions with different answers, and averaging them produces a number that means nothing. The technical name for what is being compared is the estimand. This review did the right thing: it pooled each contrast separately, and reported Mediterranean-score estimates expressed per-1-standard-deviation or per-1-point outside the highest-versus-lowest pool rather than forcing them in.

Problem two: publication bias. Studies with positive results get published more readily than studies with null ones, so a pool of published studies is systematically optimistic. The standard detection tools — funnel plots (which plot each study's effect against its precision and look for a lopsided scatter) and Egger's regression (which tests that lopsidedness statistically) — need at least about ten independent studies to have any power at all.

⚑ After stratifying properly, no single pool in this review contained ten independent studies. So no funnel plot and no Egger's test could be run anywhere in the paper, and the authors say so outright: publication bias and small-study effects "could therefore not be formally assessed."

⚑ The two good practices are in direct conflict, and there is no way to have both with this many studies. Pool loosely and you can test for bias in a number that means nothing. Pool honestly and you get meaningful numbers you cannot audit. The authors chose meaning over auditability, which is the right call — and it leaves a total blind spot that the review's certainty ratings do not fully price in.

⚠ Why it matters here specifically. This vault has repeatedly found that small-study effects are where lifestyle and supplement literatures break. In the same week: the yoga glycaemic literature's headline HbA1c effect could not be distinguished from zero once PET-PEESE adjusted for small-study effects (the-yoga-hba1c-effect-cannot-be-distinguished-from-zero-after-adjusting-for-small-study-effects); the CoQ10 analysis showed benefits shrinking as dose rose, the classic small-study signature; and publication bias was formally detected on two CoQ10 outcomes. ⚑ The one check that has most reliably deflated this vault's dietary and supplement findings is the one check this review structurally could not perform. Its estimates should be read as un-audited in the direction that has historically mattered most.

A practical rule this note exists to record. When a review reports that publication bias was not assessable, that is not a neutral procedural footnote and it is not the same as "no publication bias was found." It means the finding carries an untested exposure in the direction where these literatures usually fail. Look for the sentence; most reviews bury it, and this one did not.

In one sentence: the review pooled its studies correctly and thereby made every pool too small to check for the bias that most often inflates results of this kind, and it said so plainly rather than running the test badly.
