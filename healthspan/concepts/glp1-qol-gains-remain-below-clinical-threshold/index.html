Even taken at face value, the numbers stay under the bar — this confirms the QoL null rather than overturning it

The largest quality-of-life figure anywhere in SURMOUNT-5's HRQoL analysis is 5.45 points (General Health, tirzepatide); semaglutide's is 4.20; the between-drug difference is 1.25. The SF-36v2 norm-based scales have a population standard deviation of 10.

Set that against the standard the vault already applied. Nong 2026's BMJ network meta-analysis concluded that no weight-loss drug convincingly improved quality of life on the grounds that every mean difference fell below 5 points against a minimally important difference of 10 (glp1-no-meaningful-quality-of-life-benefit). Shukla's numbers sit inside that same range — and they are uncontrolled pre-post changes, not differences against placebo (glp1-qol-improvement-uncontrolled-no-placebo-arm), which makes them upper bounds on whatever the drug contributed.

So a paper titled "Improved health-related quality of life" produces results consistent with the conclusion that quality of life did not meaningfully improve. That is not a contradiction the vault has to resolve — it is the ordinary gap between statistical and clinical significance that shared/study-appraisal.md asks every study to be read through. With ~750 participants, a 1.25-point difference clears p = 0.003 comfortably. It does not clear a threshold anyone would notice in their life.

A note on the MID, kept honest. The vault carries the MID of 10 because that is the benchmark its existing strong, reliable source used, and applying a consistent bar across sources is the point of having one. Different MIDs are defensible for different SF-36 scorings, and the vault does not have the full text methods here to adjudicate. The claim being made is comparative and does not depend on the exact bar: whatever threshold Nong used to declare 19 drugs' worth of change unconvincing, Shukla's changes fall in the same territory. And the between-drug difference — 1.25 points, about an eighth of a standard deviation — is small under any threshold anyone has proposed.

What this changes in the clinician conversation: the honest framing is that tirzepatide edges semaglutide on a general-health questionnaire by a bit more than a point, and neither has been shown to move quality of life by an amount a person would feel. For someone weighing ~$100/month against a stated goal of living a happy life rather than maxxing biomarkers, that is the number that matters, and it is small.
