Choosing the load from today's performance beats reading it off a percentage table — on surrogates, in a small network

A PROSPERO-registered network meta-analysis (27 RCTs, n=694, healthy/active adults) compared five ways of deciding session load. Against percentage-based training — a fixed % of a 1RM tested weeks ago — three alternatives improved back-squat 1RM: variable resistance (bands/chains) SMD 0.62 (0.29–0.95), autoregulatory progressive resistance exercise 0.59 (0.16–1.02), velocity-based training 0.41 (0.10–0.73). On countermovement jump, APRE 1.08 (0.30–1.87) and VBT 0.62 (0.23–1.02) beat it. Percentage-based training ranked last or near-last on both (SUCRA 7.4% and 23.6%).

This is a fourth axis on resistance-training-programming, distinct from the three it already holds. Load is how heavy; effort is how close to failure within a set; stability is on what surface. Prescription is how the number gets chosen in the first place — from a stale table, or from what the body is doing today. It is also the only axis on that map where the headline finding is positive rather than a null.

The contrasts are more trustworthy than the ranking. Unlike the SUCRA papers this vault has refused, these are direct comparisons against the reference node with intervals excluding zero. But the ordering among the three winners rests on heavily overlapping intervals, and the winner flips by outcome — see resistance-training-modality-ranking-flips-by-outcome. Trust "autoregulation beats a fixed percentage"; do not trust "VRT is the best."

The practical column undercuts the evidence column. VBT needs a velocity transducer; VRT needs bands or chains; APRE needs repeated near-maximal sets to find the day's load. The one method that needs nothing but attention — RPE/reps-in-reserve — is the one that did not beat percentage-based training on either outcome. Against GOAL.md's adherence-first and least-equipment philosophy, the methods that won are the ones that cost most.

⚠ APRE carries this map's knee gate at full strength. Probing toward maximum effort to calibrate the day's load is the most load-uncertain protocol in the network, and no adverse-event or injury data appears in any of the 27 trials.
