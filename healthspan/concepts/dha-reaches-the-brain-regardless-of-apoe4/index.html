High-dose DHA demonstrably gets into the brain — and APOE ε4 does not stop it

In a registered, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 2 g/day of DHA raised the cerebrospinal-fluid DHA-to-arachidonic-acid ratio by 0.19 (95% CI 0.16–0.21) against placebo at six months, p < 0.0001 — a large, unambiguous demonstration that oral supplementation changes the fatty-acid composition of the central nervous system. Critically, the effect did not depend on APOE ε4 status (interaction p = 0.71), in a sample that was 47% ε4 carriers.

This closes a specific and widely used escape hatch. The omega-3 cognition literature's standard defence of its own null results is that the fatty acid never arrived: the dose was too small, the participants were already replete, or — most specifically — APOE ε4 carriers have altered omega-3 metabolism and fail to transport DHA into the brain, so trials that enrol them are testing delivery failure rather than inefficacy. This trial was designed around that hypothesis, enrolled people with low baseline intake (<200 mg/day) where uptake should be most visible, stratified randomisation by ε4, and measured the outcome in CSF rather than in plasma. Delivery worked, and it worked equally in carriers.

Graded strong, which is unusual for this vault's supplement shelf and is earned narrowly. The appraisal is reliable (registered, publicly funded by the NIA, double-blind, placebo-controlled, no manufacturer involvement), and this was the trial's pre-specified primary endpoint — the thing it was designed and powered to answer. The strength applies only to the target-engagement claim. It says nothing about whether engaging the target helps, which is the separate and considerably less flattering finding in dha-brain-delivery-without-cognitive-or-structural-benefit.

A biochemical surrogate is not a health outcome. Per shared/evidence-model.md and the operating rule of biological-age-clocks, moving a marker is not moving an outcome. Status held emerging on omega-3-cognition — a demonstrated CSF ratio change cannot promote a map whose hard-outcome evidence is unchanged.
