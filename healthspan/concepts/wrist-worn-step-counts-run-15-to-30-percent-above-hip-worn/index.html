The same person, the same day, two trackers — up to 2,200 steps apart

Fifty-nine studies put two or more activity trackers on the same person over the same days and compared what they recorded. Wrist-worn and ankle-worn research-grade accelerometers read 15–30% higher than hip-worn ones — about 1,000 to 2,200 extra steps per day.

The design is stronger than "59 observational studies" implies, and that matters for how much weight this carries. Because the comparison is within-person and simultaneous, every confounder that normally wrecks an observational finding — fitness, age, gait, weather, how busy that week happened to be — is held identical by construction. The two lenses this vault applies hardest to observational work, confounding and reverse causation, simply do not apply. It is close to the cleanest comparison available short of randomisation.

And the result is mechanistically predictable, which is what separates it from a curiosity. A wrist sensor registers arm motion that is not locomotion — gesturing, cooking, driving. A hip sensor sits near the body's centre of mass and moves once per stride. You would predict both the direction and the rough magnitude from where the devices are worn, and that is what was found (wear-location-dominates-step-count-disagreement).

The reassuring half deserves equal billing. Consumer-grade wrist devices — the kind people actually own — are only 3–15% high, and most consumer devices, pedometers, and the thigh-worn and hip-worn research devices agree within 10% and fewer than 700 steps/day (consumer-wearables-and-pedometers-agree-within-ten-percent). The large disagreement is a research-instrument problem, not a consumer one.

⚠ Graded suggestive, and the cap bites on magnitude rather than direction. The retrieved record reports these headline figures as ranges across devices without confidence intervals — and a range across devices is not an uncertainty interval, so no single percentage can be quoted precisely. Add unversioned firmware across a decade of studies (a "Fitbit" in 2016 and in 2024 are different counting algorithms), a non-random set of device pairings, and no registration or GRADE.

What it costs daily-steps: precision, not the lever. The map's mortality claim (daily-steps-mortality-dose-response, HR 0.53 at ~7,000 versus ~2,000 steps) pooled cohorts using a mixture of devices and wear locations, so the threshold carries its measurement mix (step-count-targets-inherit-the-device-that-measured-them). The map already held that a specific step target is not a physiological boundary; this supplies an independent, purely metrological reason.
