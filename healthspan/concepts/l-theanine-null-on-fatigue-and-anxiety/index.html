L-theanine does not reduce fatigue, and its anxiety effect is inconsistent (31-RCT meta-analysis)

Across 31 randomized placebo-controlled trials, L-theanine showed no significant effect on fatigue, and its effects on anxiety were inconsistent and non-significant — with a single exception: one trial in psychotic anxiety (SMD 0.54, 400 mg/day over 8 weeks), a clinical population and a chronic dosing schedule that has nothing to do with a healthy adult reaching for a calming supplement.

Why this matters for the vault. These are the nulls that give the source its credibility and tighten the map. A meta-analysis reporting only its positive attention result would have fired the p-hacking lens in shared/study-appraisal.md; this one publishes its nulls in the abstract. Taken with the finding that the pre-specified stress outcome is bias-driven (l-theanine-stress-benefit-driven-by-biased-trials), the affective case for L-theanine in a healthy adult is now: stress, weakly and unreliably; anxiety, no; fatigue, no. What survives is a cognitive effect (l-theanine-acute-attention-benefit).

Bounded honestly (why suggestive and not strong). A null is only as informative as the power behind it, and 31 trials sharing 1,168 participants is a thin base for excluding small effects. The source's mixed reliability verdict caps strength at suggestive (shared/evidence-model.md). Read this as no demonstrated effect, not as demonstrated absence of effect — the distinction the vault insists on elsewhere (see rt-intensity-does-not-change-bmd).

The practical shape of the claim. The single anxiety signal sits in psychotic anxiety at 400 mg/day for 8 weeks. Generalizing that to "L-theanine calms you down before bed" is exactly the population-transfer error shared/evidence-model.md keeps study_population in the frontmatter to prevent — the same error the vault flagged on melatonin, whose benefits were measured in cognitively impaired elders.
