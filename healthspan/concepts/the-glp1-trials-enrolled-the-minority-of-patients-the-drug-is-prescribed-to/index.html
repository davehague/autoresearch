The trials that made this a heart drug were built out of the patients who least resemble the people taking it

Across the eleven trials that established the cardiovascular reputation of GLP-1 receptor agonists, 74% of participants (95% CI 0.73 to 0.74) already had cardiovascular disease at baseline. Per-trial the figure runs from 22.9% to 100%; no trial enrolled only people without it. Meanwhile, cardiovascular disease affects roughly a third of people with type 2 diabetes internationally, and in real-world prescribing fewer than a quarter of people starting a GLP-1 have a cardiovascular history.

⚑ The mismatch has a name and a number. A participation-to-prevalence ratio divides a group's share of a trial population by its share of the real patient population. A ratio of 1.0 means the trial mirrors reality; 0.8 to 1.2 is the accepted band for representative enrolment. For primary prevention in these trials it is about 0.4 — roughly half the representation the standard asks for, in the group that is the clear majority of actual patients.

Why that is a design fact rather than an accident. Cardiovascular outcome trials are powered by events, not by people. Enrolling participants who already have the disease raises the event rate, which shrinks the sample and the follow-up needed to detect a difference — so it is cheaper and faster. The trials were optimised for statistical efficiency, and the population that produces it is not the population the drug is sold to.

⚑ The consequence, which the authors state as a policy point rather than a statistical one. Real-world data show GLP-1 use is lower in secondary prevention than in primary prevention — the reverse of where the evidence is strongest. The drug's evidence and the drug's use point in opposite directions, at a time of global shortage and rapidly rising cost. The authors argue this is a reason to weigh cardiovascular status explicitly when prescribing and when setting coverage.

⚠ What it does not establish. Unrepresentative enrolment is not evidence that a drug fails in the under-represented group; it is evidence that the group was not tested. The finding is about what is known, not about what is true. Capped suggestive; drawn from trial characteristics rather than from a randomised comparison.
