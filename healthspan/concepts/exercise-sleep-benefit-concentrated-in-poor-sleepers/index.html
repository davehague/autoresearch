Exercise improves sleep on demand: the benefit is concentrated in bad sleepers, and it is smallest in people who already sleep fine

Across 200 randomised trials (n = 23,523), analysed with Bayesian multilevel models, MetaForest and structural equation modelling, baseline sleep quality and population type were the main determinants of how much exercise helped. Populations with poorer baseline sleep were much more likely to reach a clinically important improvement than good sleepers. The authors name the pattern themselves: "benefit-on-demand."

This confirms an inference the vault made from far weaker evidence, and the confirmation is worth being precise about. On 2026-07-13, reading Kong 2026, the vault saw a single moderator coefficient (β = −0.302, baseline sleep badness) and wrote a conclusion larger than one coefficient could support: that the exercise-and-sleep literature is "almost in its entirety, a treatment literature" (walking-sleep-benefit-scales-with-baseline-sleep-problem). Twenty times the evidence now reports that structure as its headline. The vault's caution was directionally right.

What it confirms is who, not how much. Liang establishes the conditionality; it does not supply a trustworthy magnitude. No pooled effect estimate for the headline is readable, and the paper's own certainty rating is very low (exercise-sleep-certainty-very-low-at-200-trials). So this note carries the shape of the effect, not its size.

The direction of the practical implication is the opposite of what a positive finding usually means. For someone whose sleep is a foundation to protect rather than a problem to treat — which is how GOAL.md describes David's — a finding that the benefit concentrates in poor sleepers is a reason to expect less from it, not more. The largest study in the field has just made the vault's conservative read the best-supported one rather than merely the safe one.

What it is not. Population type is a moderator across trials, not a randomised stratification within one — this is meta-regression, so the poor-sleeper/good-sleeper contrast is observational at the study level and confounded with modality, duration and setting. It also cannot rule out floor effects: good sleepers have less room to improve on a bounded questionnaire, which would produce this same pattern without any difference in underlying biology.

Nothing is promoted on this. daily-steps remains recommended on its mortality evidence (~7,000 vs ~2,000 steps/day → HR 0.53, daily-steps-mortality-dose-response) and counted once. The sleep bonus stays logged and deliberately ungraded.

Rebutted by: the-only-low-risk-student-sleep-trial-studied-good-sleepers — in the one student-population review where risk of bias can be read against baseline severity, the trials enrolling the worst sleepers are also the ones with unblinded outcome assessment, and the single low-risk-of-bias trial studied students who already slept well; the benefit concentrating in poor sleepers and the benefit concentrating where measurement is weakest are not separated by that evidence. Holds as a claim about where the signal appears, not yet as a claim about who it helps.
