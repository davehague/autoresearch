The largest supplement-on-top-of-training trial in the review: mass moved, nothing else did

Roschel 2021 randomised 200 pre-frail and frail older adults to sixteen weeks of resistance training plus protein, leucine and creatine, or resistance training plus placebo supplements. Lean mass increased. Strength and function increased — in both arms, from the exercise. Frailty status showed no added benefit. The review's own one-line verdict: "Exercise effective; supplements provided no additional benefit."

⚑ The comparator is what makes this worth carrying. Both arms trained; only the supplement varied. That isolates the supplement, which is exactly the contrast this vault repeatedly records as missing from nutrition literatures, and it is the largest such contrast in a 35-trial review.

⚑ And it directly contradicts the review's own abstract, which states that "combined exercise and nutrition interventions generally produced greater improvements in muscle mass and strength than single interventions alone." The largest trial testing that proposition against an active placebo says: mass yes, strength no, frailty no. The review's body text concedes the general form — "although several interventions increased muscle mass, these changes were not always associated with meaningful improvements in strength, mobility, or frailty status" — and the abstract does not.

⚑ Mass is not function, and this vault keeps finding that supplement literatures report the mass. fat-free-mass exists for this dissociation; protein-timing-distribution-null-on-lean-mass and morning-protein-null-on-muscle-mass-and-strength-in-older-adults circle it from the timing side. Roschel supplies the cleanest version: an isolated supplement contrast, in the population most likely to respond, where the body-composition endpoint moved and every functional endpoint did not.

⚠ Scope. This is a frail and pre-frail population — the group with the most anabolic headroom and the most to gain. A null there is a harder null than a null in healthy adults. It does not overturn protein-target's daily-total claim, which rests on far better evidence about total intake rather than added supplements; it bears on the supplement-on-top question specifically. Capped suggestive — this is one trial read through a narrative review, not the primary report.
