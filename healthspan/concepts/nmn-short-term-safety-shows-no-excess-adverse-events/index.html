Short-term oral NMN produced no excess adverse events — the first pooled evidence behind a tolerability claim this vault had been making without one

Across 10 randomised trials and 383 people, oral nicotinamide mononucleotide at 250–2000 mg/day for two weeks to six months showed no signal of harm on any pooled safety endpoint.

Any adverse event: risk difference −0.008 (95% CI −0.061 to 0.045), I² = 0%. In plain terms, out of every 100 people taking NMN, somewhere between 6 fewer and 4.5 more report a side effect than on placebo — an interval centred on nothing and tight enough to rule out anything resembling a doubling of side effects at these doses over these durations. Withdrawals because of adverse events were likewise flat (risk difference −0.001, −0.036 to 0.034), which is the more behaviourally meaningful version of the same question: people were not stopping.

Liver enzymes — the standard first place a supplement's toxicity shows up — were flat and precisely measured. Alanine aminotransferase −1.03 U/L (−2.51 to 0.44) and aspartate aminotransferase −0.24 U/L (−1.43 to 0.94), both with I² = 0%, across 10 comparisons and 330 people. These two enzymes leak from damaged liver cells into blood; a narrow interval straddling zero on both is a real, if narrow, reassurance rather than an absence of data. The authors are careful to read them as short-term biochemical safety, not as a hepatoprotective benefit — no liver-lowering claim is made or supported.

⚠ The one endpoint where the interval is genuinely uninformative is the one that matters most. Serious adverse events: risk difference −0.003 (−0.036 to 0.031), supplementary Peto odds ratio 0.61 (0.18 to 2.14). Serious events were rare, so that odds-ratio interval spans everything from a 82% reduction to a doubling of serious harm. The review's own GRADE assessment rates this outcome very low certainty. A trial base this small cannot see rare harm, and this estimate does not claim to.

Why this note exists, and what it replaces. nad-precursors has stated in prose since 2026-07-22 that "NMN/NR are well tolerated; the finding is absence of benefit, not harm" — with no citation attached. That is an ungraded assertion on a safety-adjacent question, which shared/safety.md explicitly forbids. This is the first pooled, PROSPERO-registered, Cochrane-RoB-2-assessed, GRADE-rated safety appraisal of oral NMN in the vault. The assertion holds, and it now has a source and a boundary.

The boundary, stated as precisely as the paper states it. The authors write that their results "should not be interpreted as excluding potential rare, delayed, long-term, high-dose, or population-specific risks." Three specific limits sit behind that: 383 people is small for a safety question; the longest trial ran 24 weeks, which says nothing about a supplement people take for years; and the pooled populations are heterogeneous by the authors' own admission, running from recreational runners to hospitalised patients with acute kidney injury. That last group was excluded in a pre-specified sensitivity analysis and every safety outcome stayed null — which handles one problem and not the general one.

So the sentence this supports is narrow and worth getting exactly right: at studied doses, over studied durations, in a small pooled sample, there is no short-term safety signal. That is not the same claim as "safe." The distinction is the same one this vault applies to efficacy nulls (subgroup-nulls-reported-as-insufficient-evidence-not-equivalence) — an absence of a detected effect is not a demonstration of absence — applied here in the direction of harm rather than benefit.

A deliberate asymmetry, recorded. shared/safety.md says interaction and harm evidence is treated asymmetrically: raise the flag on any credible signal. No flag is raised here, and that is a finding rather than an oversight — three independent reviews of this compound class have now looked for harm and not found it, while looking for benefit and also not finding it (nad-precursors-null-on-muscle-mass-function, nad-precursors-null-on-metabolic-outcomes).
