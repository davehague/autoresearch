Stair climbers die of cardiovascular disease 39% less often, on relative risks with no baseline attached

Nine studies, 480,479 participants. Cardiovascular mortality RR 0.61 (0.48–0.79), p = 0.0002, pooled from five studies and 455,619 people. All-cause mortality RR 0.76 (0.62–0.94), p = 0.01. The morbidity endpoints the review was designed to measure — myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke, heart failure — are reported in the abstract as "morbidity was also associated with stair climbing", with no estimate of any kind.

⚠ Relative risks with no denominator. shared/study-appraisal.md opens its Universal lens set with exactly this failure: "only a relative number given; baseline hidden." A 39% reduction on an unstated baseline cannot be turned into a decision. Whether that is 4 fewer cardiovascular deaths per 1,000 people over a decade or 40 is the entire practical question, and it is not in this paper's abstract.

⚠ The pool is one cohort wide, in all probability. Five studies contribute 455,619 people. The largest stair-climbing cohort in this literature is UK Biobank at roughly 450,000 participants. Unless these five are unusually evenly sized, one cohort carries almost all the weight — an inference from arithmetic, not a retrieved fact, and unverifiable behind the Springer paywall.

Where it sits on the map. daily-steps holds the vault's anchor for incidental ambulatory activity against hard endpoints: ~7,000 versus ~2,000 steps/day → all-cause mortality HR 0.53, on 24 cohorts and more than 160,000 adults, with a non-linear curve inflecting near 5,000–7,000 (daily-steps-mortality-dose-response). Stair climbing is the same behaviour with a gradient on it, and this estimate is directionally consistent with that anchor while being far weaker in every other respect — fewer studies, no dose-response, no absolute risks, an unverifiable pool. ⚑ No stair-climbing map is coined; splitting incidental daily movement across a third map would be the proliferation this vault refused for a second magnesium map.

⚠ And this claim arrives with a published rebuttal already attached (stair-climbing-association-carries-a-published-reverse-causation-rebuttal), which is the reason the direction is banked and the magnitude is not.

Rebutted by: stair-climbing-association-carries-a-published-reverse-causation-rebuttal — a peer-reviewed opinion paper published ten weeks before this article argues that the association reflects baseline functional capacity rather than a protective effect, and anchors that on the finding that people who stopped climbing stairs carried higher cardiovascular risk than people who never climbed.
