Two unrelated designs, two different questions, the same clock: DunedinPACE

A biomarker earns the right to be a trial endpoint by clearing three separate bars. It must predict the outcome; it must respond to interventions; and moving it must move the outcome. This vault now holds independent evidence on the first two, and both name the same instrument.

Leg one — prediction. In the Berlin Aging Study II, all fourteen aging biomarkers consented by an expert panel for use as intervention outcomes were raced against 316 actual deaths in one cohort with one covariate set. DunedinPACE was the strongest and most consistent predictor of all-cause mortality, corroborated across cause-of-death strata (dunedinpace-strongest-mortality-predictor-among-consensus-biomarkers). Nine of the fourteen predicted nothing.

Leg two — responsiveness. In TranslAGE's 51-study intervention database, sixteen clocks were recomputed through one pipeline and scored on how often and how far they moved:

  • Significantly decreased in 16 interventions, increased in 1 — the best ratio in the panel.
  • Largest pooled effect size of any clock: mean −0.0891, P = 0.0012, surviving multiple-testing correction.
  • Never disagreed with the majority of other clocks that fired in the same study — alone among the sixteen.
  • ⚑ The only clock that responded equally in healthy and diseased populations (difference 0.07, P = 0.25), while PCPhenoAge, SystemsAge, PCGrimAge and GrimAgeV2 all responded 0.14–0.28 SD more strongly in disease (epigenetic-clocks-barely-respond-in-healthy-populations).

⚑ Two designs that share no data, no cohort, no analytic pipeline and no question converged on one instrument. Nothing else in this vault's measurement literature has done that.

⚠ Three qualifications, and none of them are small.

(1) The convergence does not build a surrogate. Prediction plus responsiveness is necessary and not sufficient. The third leg — that a change in DunedinPACE corresponds to a change in mortality or healthspan — has never been tested by anyone, and the paper's authors say so (clock-responsiveness-does-not-establish-surrogacy-and-there-is-no-defined-minimal-important-difference).

(2) The responsiveness result carries its own doubt, raised by its own authors. DunedinPACE was the only significant clock in 17.6% of the interventions where it fired. The paper concedes this is either unique sensitivity or a unique false-positive rate and that it cannot distinguish them.

(3) Both sources are appraised mixed and the effects are small. Vetter's DunedinPACE finding rests on a methylation subgroup with 90 deaths, not the full 316; Sehgal's rests on within-arm before-and-after t-tests with no randomised contrast, in a paper whose authors patent and sell several of the competing clocks. On the authors' own conversion (0.2 SD ≈ one year of epigenetic age), a pooled −0.089 is roughly five months.

The operating consequence for this vault. When a source reports an epigenetic-age outcome, the reading discipline is now specific rather than generic: which clock? A generation-1 Horvath or Hannum result agrees with the rest of the panel barely above chance and mostly tracks stochastic drift. A DunedinPACE or PCGrimAge result is the version of this evidence worth reading — and still is not a health outcome.
