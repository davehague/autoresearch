Brief movement breaks lowered post-meal glucose on average, and the paper published the interval saying the next trial might find nothing

Interrupting prolonged sitting with "exercise snacks" — bouts of 1 to 6 minutes of walking or bodyweight resistance work every 20 to 60 minutes, four times a day, totalling 4 to 42 minutes — reduced the incremental area under the postprandial glucose curve by 5.69 mmol·h/L (95% CI −9.38 to −2.00, P = 0.003) across five randomised crossover trials in adults at metabolic risk. Incremental area under the curve is the total glucose exposure above baseline over the hours after a meal: less area means the same meal produced a smaller and shorter blood-sugar excursion.

The confidence interval and the prediction interval say different things, and this paper prints both. The confidence interval describes uncertainty about the average effect across trials already run, and it excludes zero. The prediction interval describes where the effect in a new trial of the same kind would be expected to land — and at I² = 85.39%, where most of the variation between trials is genuine disagreement rather than sampling noise, it is much wider: −13.53 to +2.15. It contains zero. The honest two-sentence summary is therefore: on average, across these eight trials, movement breaks lowered post-meal glucose; in the next comparable trial, they might not.

Publishing that interval is why this source is graded rather than discounted. Most meta-analyses at this level of heterogeneity report the confidence interval and stop. These authors computed the statistic that limits their own headline, printed it, and wrote their conclusion around it: exercise snacks "cannot yet be recommended as a specific, evidence-based clinical prescription."

Bounded hard, and the binding limit is the horizon. Every one of the eight trials is acute — one laboratory day, one set of meals, immediate measurement. All eight are rated "some concerns" on RoB 2, driven by the impossibility of blinding someone to whether they are getting up every half hour. The endpoint is a surrogate: post-meal glucose predicts glycaemic control, which predicts complications, and nobody in these 159 people had an outcome. There is no GRADE assessment. The moderator the study was built to test — whether baseline metabolic status changes the effect — came back null at P = 0.176, with diabetes/insulin-resistance subgroups at −8.28 and uncomplicated obesity at −3.36 on overlapping intervals.

Where it lands on the map. sedentary-behavior rests on an umbrella review that publishes no pooled effect size at all, so its magnitude has been genuinely unknown. This supplies one, honestly bounded. It does not close the map's standing gap, which is that its mortality claim is chronic and observational while every experimental claim it holds is acute. More acute trials pooled together is a better acute estimate, not a chronic one.
