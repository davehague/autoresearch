Where the device sits, not what it costs, decides what it counts

Across 59 concurrent-wear studies and 13 manufacturers, the primary source of disagreement between step counters is wear location. Devices sharing a hip or thigh position agree within 10% and 700 steps/day despite differing in brand, sensor and price; devices worn on the wrist or ankle sit 15–30% above them.

The physical explanation is simple and is why the finding is credible rather than merely observed. A hip-worn sensor sits near the body's centre of mass and rises and falls once per stride — a clean, periodic signal that maps onto walking. A wrist sensor registers arm motion, much of which is not locomotion: gesturing, cooking, washing up, driving. An ankle sensor is closest to the foot and catches small non-locomotor movements that a stride-detection algorithm counts anyway. The direction and the rough size follow from geometry, and that is exactly what the data show.

This is the useful, transferable form of the finding, because it turns a table of device names into a rule that survives new hardware: ask where it is worn before asking what it is. A step count is not a property of a person's behaviour alone — it is a joint property of the behaviour and the sensor's position on the body.

⚠ Graded suggestive. The retrieved record gives no formal moderator analysis quantifying how much variance wear location explains relative to brand or algorithm — the claim is stated as the authors' summary conclusion, supported by the pattern of ranges rather than by a reported statistic. Unversioned firmware across a decade of studies is a second limit: algorithms change, and a pooled estimate averages over a moving target.

What it means for daily-steps. It is the reason a step threshold cannot be device-agnostic (step-count-targets-inherit-the-device-that-measured-them) and simultaneously the reason the practical damage is small — because the devices most people wear cluster together (consumer-wearables-and-pedometers-agree-within-ten-percent).
