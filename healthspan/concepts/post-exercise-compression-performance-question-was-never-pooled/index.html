The performance half of the compression question was never pooled — and the cleanest trial in it has fourteen people

Compression garments are sold on two claims: you will hurt less tomorrow, and you will perform better tomorrow. The soreness claim got a pooled estimate too wide to use (compression-garment-soreness-estimate-spans-harm-and-large-benefit). The performance claim got no pooled estimate at all.

The reviewers state why: performance findings "were not pooled because of heterogeneity or non-isolatable recovery effects," and were synthesised narratively instead. The outcomes across the five contributing studies were treadmill time to exhaustion, 5-km running time trials, repeated cycling bouts, 40-km cycling time trials and an 8-km cycling time trial — different distances, different modalities, different recovery windows from 60 minutes to 14 days.

The individual results, and most of them favour compression:

  • de Glanville 2012 (n = 14 crossover) — 24 hours in a full-leg graduated garment after a 40-km time trial, garments removed before the second test: performance time −1.2 ± 0.4%, mean power +3.3 ± 1.1% versus a similar-looking non-compressive placebo. ⚑ This is the cleanest recovery-only design in the review — a look-alike placebo, and the garment off before the outcome was measured. It has fourteen participants.
  • Driller 2013 (n = 10) — 60 minutes of lower-body compression vs loose shorts, cycling power better maintained, 1.8 ± 1.3%, p < 0.05. No placebo, no blinding.
  • Armstrong 2015 (n = 33 analysed) — treadmill time to exhaustion 14 days after a marathon: compression +52.4 ± 103.3 s, placebo socks −61.7 ± 129.6 s, p = 0.0091. ⚠ 59 participants were randomised and 33 analysed — a 44% attrition the reviewers retained as a risk-of-bias concern.
  • Brophy-Williams 2017 (n = 12) — repeated 5-km time trials, between-condition difference 10.6 ± 14.5 s favouring compression, p = 0.14 — not significant.
  • Williams 2021 — improved day-2 8-km time trial with high-pressure compression, but the garment was worn during recovery and during the time trial. ⚑ The reviewers refused to treat this as recovery evidence and carried it as indirect only. It cannot distinguish "recovered better" from "performs better while wearing it."

⚑ Look at the sample sizes: 14, 10, 33, 12. Every point estimate above comes from a group smaller than a typical office team, in an intervention nobody can be blinded to unless a placebo garment was used — and two of the four did not use one.

⚠ The honest position on performance is that the question is open, not answered either way. No pooled estimate exists. The two best-designed studies (de Glanville's placebo-controlled crossover, Armstrong's placebo-sock parallel trial) both favour compression; one has fourteen people and the other lost 44% of its randomised sample. That is a hypothesis worth testing properly, not a finding.

⚑ What makes the review's refusal to pool the right call rather than a gap. Averaging a 60-minute recovery before repeated cycling bouts together with a 14-day recovery after a marathon would produce a number describing nothing. Declining to compute it is the more informative output, and it is the reason this note records an open question rather than a null.
