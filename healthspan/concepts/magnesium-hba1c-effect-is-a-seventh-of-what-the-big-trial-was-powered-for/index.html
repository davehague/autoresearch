A hedge written eleven days ago turns out to have been the right reading, and now it has a number

On 2026-08-02 magnesium-glycinate ingested Al-Maqbali 2026 — the largest and longest magnesium trial in this vault, n = 247, twelve months, prospectively registered — and recorded its null primary endpoint: HbA1c median −0.30% vs −0.05%, p = 0.145. The vault then flagged the trial's own power assumption as the limit that mattered: it was powered to detect a 1.0% absolute HbA1c reduction, "the effect size of adding a glucose-lowering drug," and observed about a quarter of that (magnesium-glycemic-null-is-powered-for-an-implausible-effect). The conclusion the vault drew, deliberately narrower than the paper's: "the claim is 'no drug-sized effect,' not 'no effect.'"

Tonight the pooled estimate across 78 trials arrives: HbA1c −0.15% (−0.26 to −0.03), at GRADE moderate certainty.

That is roughly one-seventh of what Al-Maqbali was built to find, and about half of what it observed. A trial powered for 1.0% has essentially no chance of detecting 0.15%; its null was a foregone conclusion the moment the power calculation was written. The hedge the vault wrote instead of accepting the headline was not caution for its own sake — it was the correct reading, and it is now measurable.

This is the vault's cleanest instance of a discipline paying off. The habit is stated on several maps: an underpowered null is an absence of a demonstrated effect, not a demonstration of absence. It is applied constantly and its value is usually invisible, because the confirming measurement never arrives. Here it arrived in eleven days, from an independent base five times the size, and it landed exactly where the hedge predicted: a real, tiny, non-zero effect that the big trial could never have seen.

⚠ And the payoff cuts both ways, which is why this is extends and not a promotion. Being right about why the null was uninformative does not make −0.15% worth anything. Against an HbA1c scale where clinical decisions turn on whole percentage points, a fifteen-hundredths shift with an upper bound of −0.03 is at the edge of detectability and nowhere near the edge of mattering. The correct summary is not "magnesium does lower HbA1c after all." It is "the effect is real enough to measure across 78 trials and small enough that a 247-person year-long trial was structurally blind to it — and neither of those facts is a reason to take it."
