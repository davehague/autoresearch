The trials that randomised Pilates against a control found nothing; the network's headline number came from routing around them

A network meta-analysis estimates effects two ways. Direct evidence comes from trials that actually randomised A against B. Indirect evidence infers A versus B by chaining A versus C and C versus B through the network. The whole method rests on those two agreeing — the transitivity assumption. Node-splitting is the diagnostic that checks it.

Here it fired.

Pilates versus controlHedges' g (95% CI)
Direct randomised evidence⚠ 0.07 (−0.65 to 0.78)
Indirect network evidence1.01 (0.43 to 1.60)
Disagreement−0.95 (−1.87 to −0.02), P = 0.045
Published pooled estimate0.64 (0.18 to 1.09)

⚑ The trials that ran the actual experiment — randomise people to Pilates or to a control, measure their balance — produced an effect of 0.07 standard deviations, with an interval running from moderately worse to moderately better. The published 0.64 is what you get when the network borrows strength from Pilates-versus-conventional-exercise and conventional-exercise-versus-control comparisons instead.

The paper does not hide it. The direct–indirect diagnostic is printed in the sensitivity table, and the disagreement is given as the reason the Pilates node was downgraded to very low certainty. ⚠ But the abstract reports "0.64 (0.18 to 1.09) for Pilates" with no mention of it, and the node's headline number is what will be cited.

Why this is pushes-back rather than a footnote. balance-training holds balance-modality-rankings-rest-on-overlapping-credible-intervals — the ordinary sparse-network problem, where a ranking exists but its intervals overlap so the order means little. ⚑ This is the sharper failure: not an uncertain ranking, but a published effect pointing the OPPOSITE WAY from the randomised trials underneath it. An overlapping-intervals warning tells a reader to distrust the order. This tells them to distrust the number.

The structural reason it happened is stated by the authors in their own words. Their transitivity assessment "identified important imbalances across populations, mechanisms of balance impairment, intervention platforms, comparator intensity, control subtypes, outcome domains, and reporting of concurrent physical activity," and concluded transitivity was "plausible only at a broad balance-oriented exercise-category level and clinically fragile." ⚑ When the populations being chained differ that much — five of twelve studies are in multiple sclerosis, two are non-geriatric — the indirect estimate describes no real population.

⚠ What this does not say. It does not say Pilates does not improve balance. The direct evidence is 3 arms and 56 people at very low certainty — an interval from −0.65 to +0.78 is compatible with a substantial benefit as easily as with none. It says the network's confident-looking 0.64 is not supported by the experiments that tested it, and that a pilates candidate map cannot be coined on a number whose direct evidence is null.

The reading rule this licenses, generally. ⚑ Before believing a network meta-analysis estimate, ask whether direct evidence existed for that comparison and whether it agreed. Most syntheses in this vault do not report node-splitting at all; this one does, and it changed the answer.

Capped suggestive on a mixed appraisal.
