Full-intensity lifting didn't beat a light "sham" version for mood, in one small trial

In 55 young women screened positive for elevated anxiety (not clinically diagnosed), 8 weeks of progressive resistance training at 70–80% of one-rep max produced a large within-condition reduction in depressive symptoms (SMD 1.53), but so did a low-intensity "sham" version at 20% 1RM (SMD 1.04) — both sustained at one-month follow-up. The between-group difference was not significant (d=0.17, 95% CI −0.36 to 0.70; interaction p=0.806). The authors call the intensity-specific advantage "unconfirmed," not disproven — the trial is almost certainly underpowered (n=55 total, no power analysis reported) to detect a difference this small.

This lands the same week as, and is consistent with, the much larger resistance-training-depression-benefit-shrinks-against-active-comparators (O'Sullivan's concurrent 80-RCT meta-analysis, which puts RET's edge over active comparators at Δ=0.21 — real but small relative to what a 55-person trial can resolve). Because the "sham" here is still resistance training at reduced load, not an inactive control, this single trial cannot separate "intensity doesn't matter for mood" from "any structured, supervised, attention-matched movement helps roughly the same amount." Graded single-study; does not move resistance-training's Status (surrogate mood outcome, a new population data point on an existing thread, not a new strand).
