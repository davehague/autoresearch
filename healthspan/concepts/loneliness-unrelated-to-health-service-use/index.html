Loneliness was not associated with health service use

Of the five health-outcome families coded in this meta-analysis — general health, physical health (functional disability), sleep function, sensory acuity, and health service use — health service use was the only one that came back null in healthy populations. Every other domain reached significance (r between −.12 and −.23).

Why a single null is worth its own concept note here. Health service use is the outcome in this set with the most behavioural and externally-recorded character: it asks whether lonely people actually turn up to healthcare more, rather than whether they report feeling less healthy. In a literature where nearly everything correlates with everything — 92% of variance genuine between-study heterogeneity, five domains, 334 effect sizes — the domain that requires something beyond a self-descriptive questionnaire is the one that did not move.

Read alongside the measurement moderator, this is a coherent picture rather than an isolated null. The same source finds the loneliness–health association halves when health is measured objectively rather than subjectively (loneliness-health-association-halves-on-objective-measures). Health service use is the most objective-flavoured outcome in the set and it returned nothing at all. Two independent signals pointing the same way: the further this literature gets from self-report, the weaker its central claim becomes.

The honest counter-reading. Health service use is a genuinely noisy outcome and does not run in one direction — loneliness could plausibly increase utilisation (somatic complaints, lack of home support, seeking contact) or decrease it (no one to prompt or transport you, less health-seeking motivation). Two opposing real effects can cancel to a null. So this null constrains the "lonely people are visibly sicker in the health system" claim; it does not establish that loneliness has no consequences for care.

Bounded honestly (why suggestive, capped). Source reliability is mixed. Cross-sectional; no hard endpoint; the number of studies contributing to this specific domain is smaller than for general health, so this is an absence of demonstrated association rather than a demonstrated absence.
