A placebo that everybody sees through is not a blind, and this is the documented case

Blinding is the part of a randomised trial that stops expectation from being measured as effect. Randomisation handles confounding — who ends up in which group. Blinding handles what happens after allocation: if participants know they got the real thing, they may report feeling better because they expect to, and unblinded assessors may score them more generously. On a self-reported outcome, the two collapse into each other entirely.

Five of the sixteen trials here tried to solve it properly. They fitted placebo hearing aids — physically identical devices, worn in the ear, providing no useful amplification. That is a genuinely serious attempt at a hard problem, and far more than most behavioural literatures manage.

⚑ In the two Brewster trials, more than 80% of participants correctly identified which group they were in. Cochrane's verdict: "we considered the blinding to be possibly incomplete, resulting in a high risk of performance bias." The device was identical; the experience was not, because one group could hear better and the other could not.

The tally across the whole literature: exactly one trial of sixteen — Adrait 2017 — was judged low risk for both performance and detection bias. Humes 2017 was unclear because the trial co-ordinator who had contact with participants was unblinded. Fernandes 2021 blinded participants only. Every trial published in or before 2005 used waiting-list controls, and six trials in total used a waiting list — where the comparator group knows they will receive the device at the end, a design Cochrane explicitly notes risks overestimating the intervention's benefit, particularly when participants may believe their reported outcomes affect their eligibility.

⚑ The generalisable lesson, and the reason this is a concept note rather than a line in a digest. This vault routinely reads "placebo-controlled" as a quality marker, and it usually is. Here is a documented instance where a well-designed placebo existed, was correctly deployed, and did not blind anybody — in a literature whose largest effect is a self-report questionnaire.

The condition that produced the failure is not specific to hearing aids: it is that the treatment is directly perceptible to the person receiving it. By that test, a large share of this vault's maps are exposed. Cold-water immersion and sauna cannot be blinded at all. Stretching, resistance training, tai chi, balance training, yoga and every exercise map share the problem. Beetroot juice has a nitrate-depleted placebo — the vault's nitrate-depleted-placebo-isolates-the-beetroot-effect treats it as a strength — but beetroot juice also famously stains and tastes distinctive, and no trial in that literature has reported a blinding-success check. ⚑ The Brewster result says the right question is not "was there a placebo?" but "did anyone check whether it worked, and what did they find?" Almost nobody checks.

⚠ This does not invalidate the hearing-aid finding. A device that makes speech audible plausibly should produce a large improvement in self-rated hearing difficulty, and that benefit is real to the person experiencing it whether or not expectation contributes. What it does is set a ceiling on how much of the −1.25 can be attributed to amplification alone (hearing-aids-produce-a-large-improvement-in-hearing-specific-quality-of-life) — and it explains why the objectively measured outcomes in the same trials, the cognitive tests, come back flat (the-hearing-aid-cognition-evidence-is-uncertain-rather-than-positive).

In one sentence: the outcomes that could not be blinded moved enormously and the outcomes that did not need blinding did not move at all, and that pattern is worth checking for everywhere else in this vault.
