Inside a null MIND-diet trial, the people who changed their diet most had better cognitive trajectories

In a post-hoc analysis of the MIND trial (n=590 of 604, aged 65–84), participants whose MIND diet score improved substantially from baseline (mean +4 points on a 0–14 scale) showed a significant annual increase in global cognition: β = 0.035 (95% CI 0.006, 0.064) versus participants whose score did not improve. Modest improvement was not associated with cognitive change. The relationship was not modified by randomised diet assignment (p-for-interaction > 0.167). Adherence was validated against plasma lutein-zeaxanthin (β = 0.088) and β-carotene (β = 0.075).

The comparison is observational, and that is the whole story. Diet change is a post-randomization variable. Sorting a randomised trial's participants by how they behaved after assignment produces a cohort study nested inside an RCT — it inherits every confounder an observational diet cohort carries and forfeits the protection the randomisation was built to supply. shared/study-appraisal.md names this exactly: healthy-adherer bias is RED when a study "compares high vs. low adherence/use, not a true non-user control."

The randomized answer is on the record and it is null. Barnes 2023 (NEJM) assigned 604 older adults to the MIND diet with mild caloric restriction or a control diet with mild caloric restriction, counselling and weight-loss support in both arms, for three years: global cognition rose 0.205 standardized units in the MIND arm and 0.170 in the control arm — difference 0.035 (−0.022 to 0.092), p=0.23. The randomized effect was 0.035 across three years; this analysis reports 0.035 per year. Roughly three times larger, and the difference between the two numbers is randomisation.

Read at its true strength. unreliable, capped single-study — fatal red flag #1: a practice-relevant claim from a small-effect observational comparison (lower bound 0.006) with a major unadjusted confounder (the disposition to sustain a dietary change for three years, at ages 65–84, which is plausibly downstream of cognition itself). The analysis was also generated to explain the null it then overturns, and only the top adherence tertile-equivalent reached significance while the middle group did not. This cannot move mediterranean-diet or any other map on its own, and it does not.

What would resolve it. Nothing available inside this dataset. It would take a new randomised trial with a comparator that separates the diet from the dieter — or, more realistically, acceptance that the randomized test of a MIND-style diet against an active dietary control, on cognition, has been run and returned null.

For David: no change. The Mediterranean-shaped pattern earns its place on mediterranean-diet-lowers-all-cause-mortality — a mortality association across 54 cohorts and 1.8 million people — not on a cognition claim, and least of all on this one.
