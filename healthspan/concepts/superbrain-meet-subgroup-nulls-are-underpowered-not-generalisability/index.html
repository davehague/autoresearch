Seventeen subgroups, none significant, two hundred and seventy-seven people

SUPERBRAIN-MEET tested whether its multidomain intervention's cognitive benefit depended on who received it, across roughly seventeen characteristics — sex, age, education, living arrangement, internet access, frequency of digital device use, baseline MMSE, body mass index, obesity status, smoking, both blood pressures, three lipid fractions, fasting glucose and cardiovascular comorbidity. After false discovery rate correction, no interaction remained significant. The authors conclude that the results "support the generalizability and scalability" of the intervention — immediately after writing that they "should be interpreted with caution, given the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses and limited statistical power."

Both sentences are in the paper. Only one of them is supported.

Why the correction is the right thing to do, and why it does not rescue the conclusion. Test seventeen subgroups when nothing is really there and roughly one will cross p < 0.05 by chance. A false discovery rate correction raises the bar in proportion to the number of tests, bounding the share of claimed discoveries that are false. Applying it is correct and this vault credits it — it is the opposite of the uncorrected moderator screens the vault keeps catching. But FDR correction protects against false positives. It does nothing about false negatives, and a 277-person trial split in two leaves about 138 per cell, which can only detect a very large interaction. "We found no subgroup effect" and "the effect is the same in every subgroup" are different claims, and only the first one is earned here.

This is shared/study-appraisal.md's fatal flag #3 — an underpowered null read as absence — and the vault refuses it here for the same reason it has refused it ten times before. Shen's fall-risk interval reaching 11.11 called "similar safety profiles"; Wu's four hypertrophy nulls; Chen's BFR substitution null; Doan's stability null; Oba's age-generalisation null resting on three middle-aged trials (stretching-age-generalisation-rests-on-three-middle-aged-trials); Yassine's cognition secondaries; a Cochrane attention-control comparison; Almeida's Cr-EDTA tracer null; and Javaid's antiplatelet interaction regression, refused in the cautious direction. The through-line the vault has named: the null a subgroup analysis cannot detect is reliably reported as the null it found, and the resulting claim reliably flatters the paper's own recommendation.

⚠ One of these nulls carries more weight than the rest, and it should be read narrowly. Body mass index is the single moderator whose sign flipped in Look AHEAD — benefit in the overweight, nothing in the obese, a harm signal above BMI 40, interaction p = 0.004 in 3,655 people (multidomain-lifestyle-cognitive-impairment-harm-severe-obesity). SUPERBRAIN-MEET tested BMI and obesity status and found no interaction. That is the first time anything in this vault has looked again — and it is not a refutation. Different population (mild cognitive impairment versus type 2 diabetes), different endpoint (test scores versus incident MCI or dementia), different horizon (24 weeks versus 12–14 years), and a subgroup null in 277 people cannot overturn a subgroup interaction in 3,655. Recorded as a first non-replication attempt, nothing more.

What survives. The main effect (multidomain-lifestyle-improves-rbans-over-24-weeks-vs-usual-care) is kept; the generalisability claim is refused. That is the same split the vault applied to Oba 2026 — keep the primary estimate, refuse the moderator conclusion — and it is applied here to a paper whose methods the vault otherwise admires.
