Omega-3 plus antiplatelet therapy: the interaction is neither shown nor ruled out

omega-3 and aspirin have both carried this as an explicitly un-sourced gap — logged on 2026-07-06 with an instruction not to fabricate a graded concept from well-known pharmacology. This is the concept the gap was waiting for, and its content is a carefully bounded don't know.

What was actually tested. The review regressed bleeding risk against the proportion of each trial's population taking antiplatelet drugs, across eleven trials, and found a risk difference of −0.01 (95% CI −0.02 to 0), p = 0.056. The authors conclude that bleeding risk was "associated with the dose of EPA rather than the background use of antiplatelet therapy."

Why that conclusion outruns the design, in two steps.

It is ecological. A between-trial regression asks whether trials containing more antiplatelet users bled more overall. It cannot ask whether, within a trial, the people on both omega-3 and an antiplatelet drug bled more than the people on one of them. Those are different questions, and only individual-patient data answers the second. The paper's own limitations section concedes it had trial-level rather than individual-level data.

It is an underpowered null read as absence. Eleven data points, at p = 0.056, reported as evidence of no association. This is shared/study-appraisal.md's third fatal red flag — an underpowered null sold as "no effect" — and this vault has now refused it eight times: five on resistance-training-programming; once on stretching-age-generalisation-rests-on-three-middle-aged-trials, where accepting the null would have closed a gap the vault wanted closed; once on bfr-matches-heavy-load-strength-in-knee-rehab; and once on dha-brain-delivery-without-cognitive-or-structural-benefit, where the null was convenient. Here refusing it is the cautious direction rather than the convenient one, which is the version of the rule that costs something.

So the flag stays raised, deliberately. shared/safety.md states that interaction evidence is asymmetric — a missed interaction is worse than an over-cautious flag — and that a credible mechanistic or case-level signal is enough to flag while the strength is tiered honestly in the note. This concept therefore carries safety_flags: [interaction] at single-study strength: strong enough to gate co-recommendation at regimen time, too weak to assert either an interaction or its absence.

What the data do implicate is dose. Bleeding rose 0.24 per gram of EPA (0.05–0.43) and reached RR 1.50 (1.13–1.99) in the two high-dose purified EPA trials (high-dose-purified-epa-increases-bleeding-risk). Meanwhile omega-3 at ordinary doses showed no overall bleeding excess across 120,643 randomised people (omega3-no-overall-increase-in-bleeding-risk). The honest summary is that the measurable variable is grams of EPA, and co-medication remains unmeasured rather than measured-and-clean.

Where the other side of this interaction now sits. Aspirin's own bleeding harm was quantified in the same run: HR 1.24 (1.10–1.39) in a 19,114-person double-blind trial (aspirin-increases-major-haemorrhage-older-adults). So both halves of the feared combination now have sourced bleeding data, and the combination itself still does not.

What would resolve it: individual-patient-data pooling of the existing omega-3 trials stratified by antiplatelet use at baseline, or a trial randomising omega-3 dose within an antiplatelet-treated population, with one adjudicated bleeding definition. Neither has been run.

No dose is recommended (shared/safety.md), and nothing here is a reason to start or stop a medication.
