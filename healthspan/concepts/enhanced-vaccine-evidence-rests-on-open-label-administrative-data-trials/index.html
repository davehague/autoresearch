The half-million-person precision comes mostly from two unblinded trials reading outcomes off hospital records — which is why this literature is capped

A meta-analysis inherits the weaknesses of whichever trials carry the most weight, and weight follows sample size. In this review, two recent pragmatic trials dominate: the authors write that they "contributed substantially to the precision of several pooled estimates and carried considerable weight in the meta-analyses because of their substantial sample sizes." They then state what those trials are: open-label designs that, for several outcomes, ascertained events from routinely collected administrative data, so "outcome misclassification could not be fully excluded." They add that "most included trials were industry-sponsored" — which, for a comparison of an expensive high-dose product against a cheaper standard one, means the manufacturer of the product under test.

Why open-label plus administrative data is the specific combination that matters here. Randomisation protects against confounding — the two groups start alike. Blinding protects against something different: that knowing the assignment changes how outcomes get recorded. A hospital admission coded as "cardiorespiratory" rather than something else is a human judgement written into a record, and in an unblinded trial nothing structural prevents that judgement from being made slightly differently in the two arms. For a hard, unambiguous outcome like death, this barely bites — administrative systems record deaths reliably. For a coded diagnostic category it bites more.

And it bites hardest exactly where the effects are smallest. The review's two largest-population outcomes are cardiorespiratory hospitalisation, RR 0.92, and all-cause hospitalisation, RR 0.97 — reductions of 8% and 3%. There is very little conceptual distance between a genuine 3% reduction and a small systematic difference in how admissions get attributed. The two outcomes with large effects — influenza hospitalisation at RR 0.61 and laboratory-confirmed influenza hospitalisation at RR 0.68 — are much harder to explain this way, and one of them requires a laboratory test, not a code.

What this does not license. This is a reason to cap the strength of the claims, not to dismiss them. Against it stand real strengths: 32 of 49 assessed outcome results at low risk of bias and none at high; four sensitivity analyses — community-based trials only, low-risk-of-bias trials only, excluding cluster-randomised trials, excluding zero-event trials — all reproducing the main results; and I² between 0% and 8.7% on the four headline outcomes. A sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk-of-bias trials that reproduces the result is the specific check this criticism calls for, and the review ran it.

The verdict this drives. Per shared/study-appraisal.md, industry funding is not fatal on its own and becomes a mixed contributor only when it compounds a real design weakness. Here it does exactly that: the sponsorship sits on top of an unblinded, records-based ascertainment method in the trials that carry the precision. The source is appraised mixed and every concept drawn from it is capped suggestive — which is why a review of 581,845 randomised people coins its map at emerging rather than consolidating.

A generalisable caution the vault should carry. Pragmatic trials — enormous, cheap, embedded in routine care, outcomes pulled from existing records — are becoming the standard way to answer questions at this scale, and they trade blinding and adjudication for size. That is often the right trade. But the vault should expect to keep meeting very large randomised evidence whose limiting factor is how the outcome was recorded rather than how the groups were formed, and should stop treating "randomised and huge" as a synonym for "settled."
