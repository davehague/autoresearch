"Dietary supplements" is a shopping aisle, not an intervention — pooling it answers a question nobody is deciding on

The 28 trials in this meta-analysis tested tryptophan, L-theanine, GABA, B vitamins, vitamin D, zinc, magnesium, potassium, omega-3, ashwagandha, crocetin and ergothioneine. They were pooled into a single estimate. These substances share no mechanism, no target, no dose logic and no delivery rationale — a serotonin precursor, a hormone, an enzyme cofactor, a botanical adaptogen and a structural lipid do not belong in one node.

So the pooled effect answers "does taking something help?" That is not a decision anyone faces. Nobody buys the aisle; they buy a bottle. And the paper's own conclusion performs the disaggregation its design forbids — reassigning the benefit to "tryptophan, vitamin D, omega-3, zinc, and antioxidants" — a per-substance claim that a pooled analysis with no readable subgroup estimates cannot license.

This is the sixth attribution failure the vault has caught, and structurally the worst of them. The running list:

  1. creatine + beta-alanine — the combined arm estimable in 1 of 6 domains (creatine-beta-alanine-no-synergy)
  2. protein + probiotic — the probiotic's added value null in 9 of 10 contrasts (probiotic-adds-nothing-to-protein-for-performance)
  3. exercise + CBT — no CBT-alone node in the network (cbt-sleep-effect-not-isolable-no-cbt-alone-node)
  4. unstable-surface RT — surfaces compared at unspecified intensity (unstable-vs-stable-rt-strength-mobility-null-underpowered)
  5. hypoxia + HIIT — the benefit shrinks against exercise controls (hypoxic-hiit-benefit-shrinks-against-exercise-control)
  6. this — a dozen unrelated substances as one exposure

The first five bundled interventions that at least co-occur in practice. This one bundles substances that have nothing to do with each other and are never taken as a set, which makes the pooled quantity not merely unattributable but close to meaningless.

The consequence for the vault's own items is that this source cannot move any of them. l-theanine and magnesium-glycinate are inside the pool and neither appears in the authors' list of drivers — conspicuous, but absence from a narrative sentence is not a subgroup null, and the vault will not read it as one. Notably the amino-acid subgroup — which contains L-theanine and GABA — carries I² = 85%, the worst internal consistency in the paper, so even the subgroup that exists is not interpretable. Nothing here rescues either map and nothing here sinks them.

What the pooled number is still good for is the negative, category-level conclusion, which is the only claim its design supports: the supplement approach to sleep, taken as a whole, produces an effect below its own instrument's clinical threshold (supplement-sleep-effect-small-and-below-clinical-threshold). That is enough to decline the category and not enough to grade any member of it.
