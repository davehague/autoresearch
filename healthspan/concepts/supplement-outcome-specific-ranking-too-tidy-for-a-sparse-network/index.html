Each supplement wins exactly one outcome — which is what happens when each supplement was only tested on one outcome

The paper's headline conclusion is an "outcome-specific efficacy pattern": creatine best for strength (SUCRA 82.4%), protein best for endurance (85.2%), omega-3 best for recovery (88.7%). Three interventions, three outcome families, three clean first places, no overlap.

Consider what would produce that pattern in the total absence of any real division of labour. Creatine trials overwhelmingly measure strength and power. Protein trials measure body composition and endurance capacity. Omega-3 trials measure soreness, inflammation and perceived recovery. Each supplement then "wins" the outcome its own literature happens to have measured, because in that cell the competitors contribute almost no direct evidence and the network fills the gap by indirect inference. The result is an evidence-availability map presented as an efficacy map.

The network is thin enough for that to be the leading explanation. 35 trials and 1,211 participants spread across three interventions × three outcome families is roughly four trials per cell. SUCRA is well-documented as unstable in sparse networks — rank order can invert on a single added trial — and it converts thin evidence into a confident-looking percentage that carries no indication of how thin it was. A tidy 82/85/89 partition across a network this sparse is more plausibly an artifact than a finding.

⚑ And this time the vault did not have to infer the discount — the paper's own GRADE table supplies it. Certainty is rated moderate for each supplement versus placebo, and LOW for the indirect comparisons between active interventions, downgraded "primarily due to imprecision." The "outcome-specific efficacy pattern" is built on the low-certainty half of the analysis; the moderate-certainty half is the unglamorous vs-placebo kind. The vault's standing rule — trust the CI-backed contrast against control, distrust the leaderboard — is here restated in the source's own summary-of-findings table. That is the strongest external validation the rule has received, and it arrives in a paper whose headline ignores it.

Two further details from the archived full text sharpen the picture. The review was not prospectively registered (the authors say a protocol was written before analysis, which is weaker). And funnel testing on the strength outcome returned Egger's p = 0.08, Begg's p = 0.14 — asymmetry the authors report and then describe as "a small study effect, but it does not imply a cause for concern." A p of 0.08 on a funnel test is a reason to discount an estimate, not a reason to be reassured.

The rule that emerged and is applied here: trust the CI-backed contrast against control, distrust the leaderboard. All three intervals in this paper exclude zero, so the vault banks that creatine improved strength (creatine-strength-benefit-replicates-in-trained-athletes) and that protein and omega-3 moved their respective outcomes. It declines the claim that these three supplements have distinct, targetable specialities — which is the paper's actual conclusion and the commercially useful part of it.

One thing the paper does better than several of its predecessors, recorded fairly: it tested network consistency and reported no direct/indirect disagreement. Zhou's network failed that check in three comparisons, including one sitting on its winning node. Passing it does not rescue a sparse network from ranking instability, but it is a real methodological credit and the vault notes it.

Practical consequence: nothing is bought on this. "Targeted supplementation aligned with training objectives" is a purchasing framework, not a health finding — every endpoint here is an athletic surrogate in trained athletes, and GOAL.md ranks on evidence × benefit × effort for a middle-aged adult, not on sport-performance ranking probabilities.
