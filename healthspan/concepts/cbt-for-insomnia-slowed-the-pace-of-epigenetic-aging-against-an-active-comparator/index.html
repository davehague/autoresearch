The first randomised test of whether treating insomnia slows an ageing clock returns one significant clock out of three

In a secondary analysis of NCT01641263 — a UCLA randomised trial of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) against sleep education therapy (SET), an active contact-matched comparator, in adults aged 60+ with diagnosed insomnia disorder — 92 participants with paired blood methylation at baseline and a mean 24.5 months later showed a slower pace of biological ageing on DunedinPACE: −0.02 years per year (95% CI −0.04 to −0.01), FDR-adjusted p=0.03. GrimAge (−0.33 years, −0.68 to 0.03, p=0.11) and PCPhenoAge (−0.49 years, −1.34 to 0.36, p=0.26) did not reach significance.

The authors searched from database inception to 2025-06-16 and found no prior randomised trial of insomnia treatment on biological ageing. CBT-I also outperformed SET on the thing it is for: full remission 34% (16/47) versus 13% (6/45).

Why capped suggestive despite the randomisation. Source appraised mixed, and every qualification comes from the paper. (1) The between-group difference is produced by the control arm accelerating, not the treatment arm slowing (the-cbt-i-clock-result-is-the-control-arm-getting-worse). (2) The two clocks disagree about which arm moved (two-clocks-in-one-trial-disagree-about-which-arm-moved). (3) Only 96 of the completers were assayed, selected on cost and specimen availability — the authors' own leading limitation names "a loss of randomisation integrity." (4) Post-hoc power was 73%, computed against a disjunctive "at least one of three clocks" target. (5) Sex was imbalanced 73% versus 45% female in a 92-person sample.

What it is not. It is not evidence that CBT-I extends healthspan. −0.02 years/year over two years is about fifteen days of accumulated biological age on a surrogate for which, as Sehgal's authors state, no minimal clinically important difference exists. No health outcome was measured. The homocysteine literature ingested in the same run is what happens when the third leg finally gets tested: b-vitamins-do-not-prevent-heart-attack-or-death-and-the-sequential-analysis-says-stop — a marker that predicted, responded, and changed nothing.

Rebutted by: the-cbt-i-clock-result-is-the-control-arm-getting-worse — scoped rather than absolute: the between-group effect holds, but it is produced by the control arm accelerating (+0.03, FDR p=0.02) rather than by the treatment arm slowing (−0.01, FDR p=0.42), so the defensible claim is "CBT-I prevents an acceleration seen in untreated insomnia," not "CBT-I slows biological ageing."
