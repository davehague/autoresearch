A 0.64% HbA1c reduction, and the paper's own bias correction moves it across zero

HbA1c is the standard measure of average blood-sugar control over roughly three months. A 0.5% reduction is the conventional threshold for a clinically meaningful change — real enough to matter to a person, and roughly what a moderate dose of a glucose-lowering drug achieves.

The headline here clears that threshold: −0.64% (95% credible interval −0.87 to −0.41), from 22 trials, with a direction probability of 100% and a Bayes factor of about 12,000 against the null.

⚑ And in the same paper's GRADE paragraph: "the PET-PEESE conditional estimate was attenuated and crossed the null."

What PET-PEESE does, and why it is the check that matters. Small studies are noisier, so they produce a wider spread of results — and because striking results get published more readily than boring ones, the small studies that do get published are systematically the ones that found large effects. PET-PEESE uses the relationship between each study's precision and its reported effect to estimate what a hypothetically infinitely-precise study would have found. If the relationship is steep, the pooled effect is largely an artefact of small studies, and the corrected estimate shrinks. ⚑ Here it shrinks past zero. The authors downgraded HbA1c to low certainty on exactly that basis and described the decision as conservative.

⚠ The four bias checks disagree, and that disagreement is the actual finding. For HbA1c: Egger's and Begg's tests found no funnel asymmetry; trim-and-fill estimates stayed below zero; PET-PEESE crossed the null. Three say fine, one says the effect may be nothing. ⚑ The paper rates on the discordance rather than on the majority, which is the right call — you cannot vote your way out of a bias question — and it means the honest reading is "small-study effects could not be excluded," not "the effect is real" and not "the effect is zero."

For fasting glucose the picture is worse in one way and better in another. Egger's test found asymmetry at p<0.001 — clear evidence of a lopsided funnel. But trim-and-fill and PET-PEESE both produced smaller estimates that stayed below zero (−1.24 to −1.27 against a headline −1.36). So for glucose the direction survives correction and the magnitude is probably inflated; for HbA1c, the direction does not survive.

⚑ The extreme Bayes factors are the number most likely to be misquoted, and they do not mean what they appear to. A Bayes factor of 12,000 measures how much better the data fit "there is an effect" than "there is none" inside the model. It is blind to whether the studies feeding the model are biased. Twenty-eight unblinded trials against waiting lists, none at low risk of bias (twenty-eight-yoga-trials-and-not-one-active-control), will produce an extreme Bayes factor whether or not yoga does anything — it is extreme evidence that something systematically differed between the groups.

The prediction interval says the same thing more plainly. A credible interval describes the average effect across these trials; a prediction interval describes what the next trial would find. For HbA1c it is −1.17 to −0.09, roughly three times wider — a new trial could plausibly find almost nothing. For fasting glucose it is −2.72 to −0.03, whose upper bound the authors themselves note "approached the null."

In one sentence: the paper reports the statistic that undercuts its own headline, acts on it in its certainty rating, and thereby earns more trust than the finding does.
