Tai chi outranked interval training and aerobic exercise for blood sugar — and a network meta-analysis is structurally incapable of telling that apart from having been tested against less

The result. In a 34-trial network, traditional Chinese sports — tai chi, qigong and related practices — finished second of five on HbA₁c at SUCRA 76.56%, with a mean difference of −0.80% (95% credible interval −1.09 to −0.52). It beat high-intensity interval training (−0.63%) and continuous aerobic exercise (−0.48%), on 9 trials — the second-largest node in the whole network.

⚑ Flagged as a candidate intervention and NOT adopted — the vault's eleventh candidate refusal, after whole-body vibration, beta-alanine, probiotics, collagen (twice), hypoxic training, cocoa flavanols, green exercise, residential greenspace and air pollution. This refusal is not about the practice. It is about what this design can and cannot measure.

The defect, and the vault has been tracking it for two days. On 2026-07-29 an item was opened noting that shared/study-appraisal.md has no lens for what the control arm was asked to do — after three sources in a single night turned out to be separated by exactly that variable: walking trials against active comparators came back null; a 74-trial network against wait-list controls produced large positives; and a multidomain trial against usual care got 0.28 standard deviations where its active-controlled sibling got 0.11 per year. This paper is the fourth instance, and the cleanest.

Why a network meta-analysis is the worst possible design for hiding this. A network estimates never-tested-head-to-head comparisons by chaining interventions through shared comparators. The control node is the reference point everything hangs from — so if the trials feeding one node used wait-list or usual-care controls while the trials feeding another used active exercise comparators, the two nodes are being measured against different zeros, and the model has no way to detect that. It will faithfully report the resulting ranking as if it were a comparison between the interventions. This review never characterises its control arms anywhere.

Two features of the pattern point that way rather than at a real effect. The result is modality- inconsistent: traditional Chinese sports ranks second on HbA₁c and fourth of five on fasting glucose (SUCRA 44.14%), which is a strange profile for a genuine physiological effect and an ordinary one for comparator heterogeneity. And the physiology is hard to argue: these are low-to-moderate intensity practices, and the same paper's own dose-response analysis says the driver is total MET-minutes (exercise-glycemic-benefit-starts-near-500-met-minutes-and-plateaus) — a metric on which tai chi scores low. A modality that delivers less measured dose while outranking modalities that deliver more is either revealing something the dose metric misses, or is being measured against a weaker baseline.

⚠ What the vault is not saying. It is not saying tai chi does not improve glycaemic control — nine trials found significant benefit against their controls, and the practice is joint-friendly, cheap and highly adherable, all of which GOAL.md values. The claim is narrower: this evidence cannot distinguish "works better than interval training" from "was compared against less."

⚠ And one disclosure, recorded rather than weighted. Two of the review's author affiliations are institutions dedicated to traditional Chinese sports culture, and traditional Chinese sports is one of the six ranked nodes. The paper declares no financial conflict and the vault does not treat this as one — but per the bias-watch rule it is recorded, and it means this particular node is read with the same care the vault gives an industry-funded supplement arm, not less.

What would close it. Trials of traditional Chinese sports against an active, dose-matched comparator — the same MET-minutes of ordinary exercise — and, generally, network meta-analyses that report comparator type per node so a reader can see which zeros are being used. Neither exists in this literature.
