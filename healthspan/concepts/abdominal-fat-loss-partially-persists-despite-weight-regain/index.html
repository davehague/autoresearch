The weight came back. The fat, partly, did not.

Two 18-month randomized lifestyle trials — CENTRAL and DIRECT-PLUS, both testing Mediterranean-diet variants plus structured physical activity — brought their participants back for MRI 5 and 10 years after the intervention ended, and reached 366 of 381 (96%).

Everyone had regained the weight. BMI 30.5 at baseline, 30.6 at follow-up: complete regain, which is the usual and dispiriting result of long-term lifestyle-trial follow-up, and the authors report it plainly.

And their abdominal fat was still partly down. Waist circumference, visceral adipose tissue, deep subcutaneous fat and superficial subcutaneous fat all partially preserved their intervention-induced reductions years later (FDR ≤ 0.01 for all). Two depots did not: intrahepatic fat was fully regained, and intrapancreatic fat came back worse than baseline (intrapancreatic-fat-rebounds-above-baseline).

Why this is the trustworthy half of the paper. The exposure is measured by MRI, not by a food-frequency questionnaire; retention is 96%, which all but eliminates the attrition bias that usually destroys long-term follow-up; the participants came from randomized trials, so baselines are trial-grade; and the change being described is within people, from a period when they were randomized. This is a well-instrumented, well-retained, randomized-origin observation — and it is a different thing entirely from the paper's headline causal claim, which is not (visceral-fat-diabetes-association-is-observational-not-randomized).

The implication that actually matters: the scale is a bad instrument for what the intervention did. Every "lifestyle intervention failed, participants regained the weight" headline — and there are decades of them — is measuring the thing that came back and missing the thing that stayed. Body weight is the outcome that is cheapest to measure and least specific about what tissue changed.

And this opens a lens the vault has been missing: durability — what survives after you stop. The only other member of this class is cbt-i-durable-depression-benefit, where CBT-I's effect on depression was still measurable 12 months after treatment ended, and the vault wrote: "a substance stops working the day you stop taking it; a skill does not." Here, a behavior leaves a mark on tissue that outlasts it by a decade. On this axis, every supplement in the vault scores exactly zero by construction — creatine, magnesium, l-theanine, omega-3, urolithin A all stop the day the capsule stops, and the vault has never once asked the question. Whether durability becomes a graded axis in shared/decision-rule.md is a human decision — proposed and logged in docs/open-gaps-log.md, not adopted.
