The epigenetic clocks respond in sick people; in healthy people nothing survived correction

Population health status was the strongest of eight study-level predictors of how much a clock moved — stronger than sample size, intervention duration, mean age, age spread, minimum age, age range or percent female. The standardised association strengths for the most responsive biomarkers were PCPhenoAge Z = 4.83, SystemsAge 4.65, PCGrimAge 4.07.

Split the database and the asymmetry is stark.

PopulationClocks that responded
Disease (10 studies)PCPhenoAge −0.32 (P = 0.0057), PCGrimAge −0.22 (0.0022), SystemsAge −0.213 (0.0105) — all three survive multiple-testing correction
Healthy (41 studies)DunedinPACE −0.074 (0.0143), PCGrimAge −0.043 (0.0191) — ⚠ neither survives multiple-testing correction

⚑ Four fifths of the database is healthy people, and in that four fifths not a single one of sixteen clocks produced a response that held up once the number of tests was accounted for.

The between-group tests confirm it is not a sample-size artefact. Comparing effect sizes directly between the healthy and disease sets, PCPhenoAge differed by 0.28 SD (P < 0.0001), SystemsAge by 0.21 (P < 0.0001), PCGrimAge by 0.18 (P = 0.002) and GrimAgeV2 by 0.14 (P = 0.02) — differences larger than most of the effects themselves.

⚑ One exception, and it matters. DunedinPACE responded equally in both populations (mean difference 0.07, P = 0.25). The authors attribute this to training context — DunedinPACE was derived in a younger, healthier cohort, whereas PCPhenoAge and SystemsAge were trained on older or more clinically diverse populations and are therefore "more sensitive to changes in dysregulated systems." It is the only clock in the panel with a defensible claim on a healthy user, and that claim is one uncorrected P value in a database of 41 healthy studies.

The authors' explanation is the ordinary one and they state it plainly: interventions "often have stronger observable effects in populations with greater physiological impairment, where there is more room for improvement."

⚠ The commercial mismatch is the point. Consumer epigenetic-age testing is sold to healthy people who want to know whether what they are doing is working. This is the population in which the instruments were least able to detect that anything was done. Combined with a whole-panel mortality discrimination of C-index 0.65 (aging-biomarker-panels-discriminate-mortality-weakly), a healthy buyer is purchasing a measurement that sorts individuals weakly and moves rarely, with no defined threshold for what a meaningful change would even be (clock-responsiveness-does-not-establish-surrogacy-and-there-is-no-defined-minimal-important-difference).

⚠ Scope. Every effect here is a within-arm before-versus-after paired t-test, not a randomised contrast, on studies of ~61 samples each; the 10 disease studies are a small and clinically miscellaneous set. This is a description of instrument behaviour, not a claim that interventions work better in sick people. Capped suggestive on a mixed appraisal, and the appraisal is mixed partly because several of the clocks named here are owned or sold by the paper's own authors.
