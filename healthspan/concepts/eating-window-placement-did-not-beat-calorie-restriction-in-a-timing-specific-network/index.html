The clock was the last live objection to "fasting is just eating less," and a network built to test the clock did not overturn it

What the map believed going in. Since 2026-07-21 maps/intermittent-fasting has held, on the BMJ's 99-trial network meta-analysis, that intermittent fasting is a delivery format for energy restriction — it works by cutting calories, not by some property of the fast itself (intermittent-fasting-matches-caloric-restriction-for-weight, intermittent-fasting-cardiometabolic-markers-match-caloric-restriction).

The strongest objection to that was resolution. Those networks treated "time-restricted eating" as one thing. Circadian physiology predicts it is not: insulin sensitivity and fat oxidation are higher earlier in the day, and growth hormone and testosterone follow daily rhythms — so the same calories eaten at 8 a.m. and at 8 p.m. should not behave identically. A network that lumps all eating windows together cannot see a timing effect even if one exists.

⚑ This is that objection tested at the right resolution, and it does not hold. 39 trials, 3,236 participants, with time-restricted eating split by clock time — early (first calories before 10:00), midday (10:00–12:00), late (after noon) — each with and without exercise, against calorie restriction and against control. Against calorie restriction, no pattern won on any of eighteen outcomes. The authors' own conclusion says so, and their discussion goes further: the improvements "appear to be driven predominantly by weight loss regardless of eating window."

⚠ What the paper found instead is a ranking, and a ranking is not an effect. SUCRA — surface under the cumulative ranking curve — is computed whether or not any treatment differs from any other. Early TRE ranked above calorie restriction on most outcomes (e.g. body weight: eTRE+EX 94%, eTRE 68%, CR 44%). Those percentages sit on top of comparisons that contain zero. The authors describe the preference as "suggesting that meal timing may influence health outcomes" and immediately add that certainty was "very low to moderate for most outcomes."

⚑ One subgroup result is the most quietly damaging thing in the paper for the timing hypothesis, and it was pre-specified. Splitting trials by whether energy intake was prescribed or ad libitum, eTRE and mTRE looked better in the ad libitum trials — and so did calorie restriction. Verbatim: "the CR intervention showed numerically more favorable outcomes in the subgroup of studies where TRE was implemented ad libitum." When both arms move together across a subgroup split, the split is describing the trials, not the interventions.

⚠ What this does not settle. It does not say timing is irrelevant — see early-eating-window-is-the-only-one-that-moved-glucose-and-blood-pressure, where early eating was the only node to move four separate glucose and pressure endpoints against a control. The claim here is narrower and it is the comparison that matters for a decision: against the alternative of simply eating less, no window placement demonstrated an advantage. Certainty was very low to moderate; the exercise nodes are two and three trials; and significant local inconsistency was detected on body weight, BMI and body fat percentage.

Capped suggestive. stance: confirms — the map's central claim survives its hardest test to date.
