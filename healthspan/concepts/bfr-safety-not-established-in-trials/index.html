Twenty-one randomised trials of restricting blood flow to a limb, and still not one adverse-event tally

⚠ Updated 2026-07-13 — the absence replicates. A second, independent synthesis has now landed (bfr-patellofemoral-pain-meta-hou-2026: 11 RCTs, n=508 patellofemoral-pain patients — the clinical population, not athletes), and it also fails to produce a systematic adverse-event tally. What Hou does report is, if anything, mildly unfavourable: BFRT groups "commonly reported higher ratings of perceived exertion and greater post-exercise muscle soreness." No serious adverse events or cuff-related complications are detailed — because nobody systematically collected them.

Twenty-one randomised trials. Two independent meta-analyses. Two different populations. Zero adverse-event data. The flag was originally set on the absence in one synthesis; the absence has now replicated, which is a stronger reason to keep it than the reason it was set. "No serious adverse events reported" is not "no serious adverse events occurred" when the trials were not designed to count them — and the honest reading of twenty-one silent trials is that the safety question has not been asked, not that it has been answered. safety_flags: [high-dose-risk] stands.

The one thing that did change: Hou closes the population gap (blood-flow-restriction now holds evidence from people whose knees actually hurt) and demonstrates a clinically meaningful analgesic effect (bfr-reduces-patellofemoral-pain-during-loaded-tasks). That makes the missing safety data more urgent, not less — a technique nobody will use is one thing; a technique with a real benefit that people will now reach for, whose risks nobody has counted, is another.

The vault's first blood-flow-restriction source pooled 10 RCTs (n=181 athletes) and reported no adverse-event data at all. The authors' own future-research section asks for trials with "transparent pressure determination procedures, progression strategies, and adverse event reporting" — which is a polite way of saying the existing trials did not do it.

Unstudied is not the same as safe, and the distinction is the whole reason this note carries a flag. BFR's mechanism is not incidental to the safety question — it is the safety question. The technique works by applying a pneumatic cuff to partially restrict arterial inflow and restrict venous outflow, producing localized hypoxia and metabolite accumulation. Outcomes and safety, in the paper's own words, "depend strongly on cuff characteristics and pressure prescription" — and the included trials rarely reported cuff bladder design, taper, autoregulation, or the distinction between set and interface pressure. Pressure should be individualized to arterial occlusion pressure; the authors note that uniform absolute pressures "may result in suboptimal or excessive restriction across individuals."

So the vault holds: a technique that deliberately restricts limb blood flow, whose safety depends on equipment settings, tested in ~18 healthy young athletes per trial, with those settings inconsistently reported and zero adverse events counted.

safety_flags: [high-dose-risk] — set on absence of evidence, deliberately. shared/safety.md is explicit that interaction and safety evidence is asymmetric: a missed safety issue is worse than an over-cautious flag, and a credible mechanistic signal is enough to flag. The flag caps any regimen expression of BFR at "proposed — review with your doctor / physio," which is where it will stay until a source characterizes the contraindication profile in a non-athlete population. No cuff pressure, no load, and no protocol appear anywhere in this vault — none was reliably reported to record, and none will be invented (shared/safety.md: never synthesize a dose).

Why this matters more here than it would elsewhere. The reason BFR is attractive to this vault is resistance-training's knee gate — David is rehabbing a March 2026 patellar contusion, and GOAL.md names knee/joint safety as a hard constraint. The person most tempted by BFR is therefore exactly the person least represented in its evidence base: not a healthy collegiate athlete, but someone using it because a joint is compromised. An intervention pitched as safer than heavy loading that has never been safety-audited cannot be adopted on that pitch.
