A leaderboard is not evidence, and this is the cleanest demonstration the vault holds

The paper reports that no drug class differs significantly from any other, and then concludes that two of them have "potential neuroprotective effects" and that antidiabetic selection "should consider long-term neurological benefits." The bridge between those two statements is SUCRA.

⚑ Rank probabilities always return a first place. SUCRA is a deterministic function of the posterior distribution: feed it a network in which every comparison is indistinguishable and it will still order all seven nodes and report one as best. It measures where the point estimates happened to land, not whether they landed anywhere distinguishable. The word "consistently" in the abstract means "the point estimates kept coming out in the same order," which is what point estimates drawn from a single shared posterior do.

⚑ The vault's SUCRA discipline has five prior instances and this is the purest. Previously the objection was that a ranking was unstable under quality filtering (integrated-aerobic-resistance-training-survives-the-quality-filter-that-sinks-circuit-training), that no effect sizes were printed at all (frailty-network-ranks-bundles-without-reporting-effect-sizes), or that the winning node was too vague to interpret (the-winning-vascular-node-is-the-category-of-everything-that-did-not-fit). Here the underlying comparisons are not merely uncertain — they are uniformly, exhaustively null, and the ranking is the entire result.

⚠ The paper's own discussion supplies the refutation. "The observed association with lower PD risk with SGLT2i and GLP-1RA may partly reflect their favourable cardiometabolic profiles rather than direct neuroprotective effects." Three positions coexist in one paper — the abstract's neuroprotection, the discussion's cardiometabolic alternative, and the results section's flat null — and only the strongest travels.

⚑ Standing rule, reaffirmed: ranking recorded, leaderboard declined.
