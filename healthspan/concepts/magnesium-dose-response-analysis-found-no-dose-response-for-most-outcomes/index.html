A dose-response meta-analysis that found no dose-response for most of its own headline effects

Mohammadi 2026 is built as a dose-response synthesis — fractional-polynomial models for nonlinear associations, meta-regression for linear ones, run across every outcome. Its own dose-response paragraph reads, verbatim:

"No significant linear (Figures S2 and S3) or nonlinear (Figures S4 and S5) dose–response relationships were observed between Mg dose or trial duration and changes in BFP, FI, BW, HOMA-IR, HDL-C, SBP, TC, CRP, BMI, AST, IL-6, TNF-α, or ALT."

That list contains four of the paper's ten significant headline outcomes: body weight, HOMA-IR, systolic blood pressure and IL-6. The supplement moved them, and how much of the supplement you took did not predict how much they moved.

Only two genuine dose associations survived across twenty-one outcomes: diastolic blood pressure, nonlinearly (coefficient −2.58, p = 0.025), and LDL-C, linearly (β = −0.02, p = 0.042). Every other significant "dose-response" result in the paper is an association with trial DURATION, not with dose — waist and hip circumference, fasting glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides and creatinine. Duration and dose are different claims: one says more magnesium does more, the other says longer trials report more, and the second has a well-known alternative explanation in which nothing pharmacological is happening at all.

Why this matters more than a missing subgroup analysis usually would. Dose-response is one of the oldest and least-replaceable arguments for causation. shared/study-appraisal.md names it directly as the GREEN condition that lifts an effect out of confounding range: "large, dose-responsive effect confounders can't plausibly explain." These effects are small AND non-dose-responsive — both halves of that condition fail at once. A small effect with a clean dose gradient would be more persuasive than a large one without; here there is neither.

⚠ Read as an open question rather than as a debunk, for two honest reasons. First, the trials cluster: 79.5% used ≥300 mg/day and evidence above 500 mg/day is described as limited, so the tested dose range may simply be too narrow to reveal a gradient — an absence of a demonstrated dose-response, not a demonstration of its absence. Second, magnesium is a repletion nutrient rather than a drug, and repletion effects can be genuinely threshold-shaped rather than graded. But the study that could test that — one recruiting on baseline magnesium status — is precisely the one this synthesis reports it could not perform, because "the majority of included RCTs were not designed to recruit participants according to Mg status."

So the plausible mechanism, the plausible dose shape, and the plausible responder subgroup are all untested in the same paper. Logged as the map's sharpest remaining metabolic gap: a magnesium RCT that recruits on measured magnesium status and varies dose.
