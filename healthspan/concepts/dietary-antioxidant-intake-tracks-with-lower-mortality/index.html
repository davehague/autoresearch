The nutrients whose pills failed four randomised trials look strongly protective when they come from food — which is the oldest confounding story in nutrition, arriving one more time

Rebutted by: dietary-antioxidant-index-internal-inconsistencies — from inside the same paper: four of the six nutrients have no significant dose-response trend, the two statistical methods name different active ingredients, the association reverses in non-Hispanic Black participants, and it vanishes entirely in adults 65 and over. The magnitude below is recorded and treated as unusable.

The association. Among 34,955 American adults followed for a median of roughly a decade, those in the top fifth for dietary vitamin E intake died at HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.58–0.83) relative to the bottom fifth — a 31% lower rate — with matching results for cardiovascular (0.67) and cancer (0.67) mortality and a significant trend across quintiles for all three. Carotenoids: 0.77 (0.67–0.88). A composite antioxidant score covering six nutrients put the top fifth at 0.82 (0.70–0.97) for all-cause and 0.61 (0.45–0.84) for cancer mortality.

Set that beside what this vault already holds, from randomised trials of the same nutrient class in pill form. COSMOS randomised 21,442 older adults to a daily multivitamin and found no cardiovascular or cancer-mortality benefit (multivitamin-no-cvd-cancer-mortality-benefit-cosmos); an umbrella of 19 meta-analyses across 5.5 million people found no all-cause mortality benefit (multivitamin-no-mortality-benefit); VITAL found the same for vitamin D on cancer and cardiovascular disease (vitamin-d-no-cancer-cvd-benefit).

Two readings, and only one of them is supported by this design.

Reading one: the food matrix does something the isolated nutrient does not. Plausible, frequently argued, and completely untested here. It requires new biology and would need a trial to establish.

Reading two: dietary antioxidant intake is a marker for the kind of life that produces lower mortality. People eating the most vitamin E and carotenoids are eating nuts, seeds, oils and vegetables — and are, on average, wealthier, better educated, less likely to smoke, more likely to exercise and more likely to have a doctor. The model adjusts for income, smoking, alcohol and physical activity. It adjusts for none of education, overall diet quality, or healthcare access. This reading requires no new biology and explains the randomised nulls at the same time.

The vault takes reading two, and it does not have to reason from a base rate to get there. shared/study-appraisal.md instructs default skepticism toward lone observational causal claims given the field's replication record — but here the vault holds the trials that already ran, on the same nutrients, and they were null.

Two structural details that push the same way. The paper's own weighted quantile sum analysis assigns the largest weights to vitamin A or C, while the single-nutrient results make vitamin E the standout — two methods on one dataset disagreeing about which nutrient matters, which is what a composite looks like when it is capturing something correlated with all six rather than any of them. And four of the six nutrients have no significant dose-response trend at all (dietary-antioxidant-index-internal-inconsistencies).

⚑ One conclusion in the paper that its own design cannot support, stated because it is the sentence most likely to be quoted. The abstract argues diverse dietary antioxidants are "superior to single-nutrient strategies." Dietary supplement use was excluded from the exposure entirely — the authors say so as a limitation. So a person in the lowest dietary quintile who takes a vitamin E capsule daily is counted as low-exposure. The study never measured a single-nutrient strategy and therefore never compared anything to one.

What does survive, and it is the smaller claim. The restricted cubic splines are U- or L-shaped for vitamins A and C and zinc, and U-shaped for selenium against cardiovascular death: the association weakens or reverses at the highest intakes. That is the same shape the vault carries from three unrelated literatures — benefit appears early, then stops, and past some point more is worse. "More is not better" survives here even though the causal claim does not.

Graded open-question, capped suggestive, and the magnitude treated as unusable. Two 24-hour recalls standing in for a decade of eating, roughly ninety uncorrected quintile-by-nutrient-by-outcome comparisons, no registration or prespecified analysis plan, and funding from the research institute of a commercial nutrition company — recorded per the bias-watch rule, though notably the conclusion does not favour supplementation.
