A dose-response about supplement grams, fitted without knowing total grams — which is the only quantity that matters

The problem in one sentence. If you give 30 g of whey to someone already eating 1.6 g/kg/day and to someone eating 0.8 g/kg/day, you have run two different experiments — one of them adds protein to sufficiency, the other repairs a deficit — and a curve that plots "supplemental grams" against "lean mass gained" pools them as if they were the same.

⚑ The paper says this itself, three separate times, in three separate sections. In the dose-response results: "the dose–response model used supplemental protein dose as the predictor, without accounting for baseline habitual protein intake, which was not consistently reported across studies. Individuals with low habitual intake may respond differently to the same supplemental dose." In the limitations: baseline intake "was not consistently reported across the included studies and could not be summarized by treatment node… this represents an important source of uncontrolled heterogeneity that may confound both the dose–response findings and the indirect comparisons." And in the discussion, spelling out the arithmetic: "The initial 20 g/d of supplementation may simply be compensating for the dietary protein intake that is lacking, with the real 'supplemental' effective dose occurring only above this replacement level."

⚑ Why this is the load-bearing caveat for this vault specifically. maps/protein-target has held since 2026-07-04 that the lever is the daily total, plateauing around 1.6 g/kg/day, and that timing and distribution are refinements that land null. Every finding in this network is expressed in the wrong unit for that belief. A 30–35 g/day supplemental plateau does not translate into a total-intake statement without the number the paper does not have.

⚑ It contaminates more than the dose curve. The same missing variable is one of the effect modifiers the authors list as unevenly distributed across treatment nodes, which is why they write that node-splitting and net heat plots "do not directly establish transitivity." So the type ranking inherits it too: if the whey trials happened to recruit people eating less protein than the casein trials did, part of whey's lead is diet, not dairy.

⚑ And it explains the plant-protein result without invoking botany. The authors' own diagnosis of why plant proteins look worse here than in some head-to-head trials: "trials matching total protein intake closely (rather than supplementing a fixed dose on top of habitual diet) tend to narrow the gap between animal and plant sources." Three findings in one paper, one confound underneath all of them.

The vault has met this before, from the other side of the literature. protein-source-benefit-unseparable-from-deficit-and-exercise recorded a 15-trial review in 1,161 women concluding that "when analyzed independently of energy restriction and physical exercise, the effects of consuming both proteins on body composition were less consistent, making it impossible to confirm benefits from an isolated increase in protein intake." ⚑ A Bayesian network of 36 trials and a narrative review of 15 have now independently named the same missing denominator.

What would close it. A protein-supplement trial — or better, an individual-participant-data meta-analysis, which the authors list as future work — that recruits on baseline habitual intake and reports the dose-response conditional on it. Until then the honest statement is: supplemental protein helps, the marginal return flattens somewhere in the 30–35 g/day region for people whose diets look like the ones in these trials, and nobody in this literature knows what those diets were.

Capped suggestive. stance: confirms — it confirms the map's existing "the lever is the daily total" position by showing what a supplement-denominated analysis cannot resolve.
