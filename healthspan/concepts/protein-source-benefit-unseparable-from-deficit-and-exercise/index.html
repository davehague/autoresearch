Fifteen trials could not show that more protein, by itself, changed body composition

The review's load-bearing sentence is the one that undercuts its own title: "When analyzed independently of energy restriction and physical exercise, the effects of consuming both proteins on body composition were less consistent, making it impossible to confirm benefits from an isolated increase in protein intake."

The trials that "worked" were trials that also cut calories or added supervised training. Animal protein's fat-loss signal appeared "especially when combined with calorie restriction and supervised exercise"; soy's appeared "when associated with exercise." Remove the co-interventions and the finding goes with them.

⚑ Graded confirms, because this is exactly what protein-target already says. The map's standing clause is that protein's effect is additive to training, not a substitute — the benefit is "protein added to training" (protein-supplementation-augments-resistance-training), and creatine-lean-mass-requires-resistance-training records the identical structure in a different supplement: the gain exists with resistance training and vanishes without it. A review that cannot demonstrate a protein effect once the training and the deficit are removed is not a challenge to this map. It is the map's own clause, arrived at by accident and stated as a limitation.

This is the tenth attribution failure the vault has catalogued, and its shape is now familiar. Yesterday's HMB trial gave one arm HMB plus 24 g protein plus fibre plus fat plus vitamins against maltodextrin, and named itself after one of seven differences (hmb-trial-cannot-separate-hmb-from-the-24-grams-of-protein-beside-it). The multidomain lifestyle literature has the same problem at programme scale (multidomain-nutrition-exercise-bundle-not-separable). Here the bundle is protein + deficit + training, and the review names the protein.

⚠ What this does NOT establish, stated because a null this shape invites over-reading. It does not show protein is useless — the map's anchor, Morton 2018's meta-regression across 49 RCTs, sets a ~1.6 g/kg/day plateau and is untouched by an unpooled 15-trial narrative synthesis at rct tier. Highest-tier-wins is not a tiebreak; it is the rule. It also does not show the co-interventions are doing all the work — a review without a meta-regression cannot apportion anything. What it shows is that these fifteen trials were not designed to isolate the variable their review is named after.

One genuine addition: the population. This vault's protein and muscle evidence is heavily male — Gu 2026 is men aged 18–30, the bolus work is young men. 1,161 women across the menopausal transition is a correction the map has been missing, and it opens a gap rather than closing one: the ~1.6 g/kg/day target has never been separately validated across menopause, a period with documented lean-mass loss and fat redistribution.
