Short sleep during a calorie deficit shifts what you lose away from fat

In the two randomised trials that manipulated sleep directly, adding sleep restriction to calorie restriction produced consistently reduced fat-mass loss, with one trial also finding significantly lower total percentage weight loss. This is the only causal claim in a 30-study review; the other 28 studies treated sleep as an observed exposure.

It says something more specific than "sleep is good for you." It says that when an energy deficit is created while short on sleep, the composition of the loss changes — less of it is fat. The magnitude is unavailable: the review could not meta-analyse, so no pooled estimate exists.

And it pairs with a finding graded three days earlier from a completely different literature. Deller 2026 (34 RCTs, n = 1,455) found that adding exercise to calorie restriction preserves +0.87 kg of fat-free mass — about half the loss — without costing weight loss, with meta-regression rejecting the obvious confound (exercise-preserves-fat-free-mass-during-calorie-restriction). Two independent literatures, two different co-factors, one rule: during an energy deficit, what you do alongside the deficit determines which tissue you give up. Exercise protects lean mass; short sleep costs fat loss. Neither is about how much weight comes off.

Bounds. Two small RCTs, in adults with BMI ≥ 25 during active weight-loss interventions — not healthy-weight adults and not a general sleep question. No pooled magnitude, no duration threshold, and no sleep target is prescribed (shared/safety.md). The parent review appraised mixed: preregistered and duplicated, but over a corpus where all studies carried at least moderate risk of bias (11 serious, 1 critical among the non-randomised) and sleep was mostly self-reported.
