A break can cost you lean tissue while the scale still reads like a win

In the medium-term detraining stratum (2–6 months off), fat-free mass fell significantly below baseline — the only outcome in a twenty-item panel to move in the harmful direction. In that same window, body mass and waist circumference were still significantly improved.

So the two numbers a person actually checks — weight and waistline — kept reporting success while the tissue underneath was going the other way. The authors name the risk directly: exclusive focus on body mass, BMI or waist circumference during a layoff "may be limited," and they advise "vigilance for the potential risk of occult sarcopenia during detraining."

The mechanism is not mysterious. Skeletal muscle is mechanosensitive: withdrawing mechanical load rapidly downregulates mTOR-related protein-synthesis signalling and drops muscle-fibre protein synthesis rates. Prior meta-analyses show muscle size and strength declining after resistance-training cessation, particularly in older adults.

Bounded honestly. This is one subgroup estimate in a panel run without multiplicity correction, from a pooled evidence base where only 5% of trials are at low risk of bias. It is a signal to watch, not a measured rate of loss — and the review's baseline-versus-post-detraining contrast means it cannot say how much lean mass was lost from peak (detraining-residual-effects-measured-against-baseline-not-peak). Note also that muscle mass and lean body mass showed no significant post-intervention gain to begin with, so their flatness during detraining is non-responsiveness rather than decay.
