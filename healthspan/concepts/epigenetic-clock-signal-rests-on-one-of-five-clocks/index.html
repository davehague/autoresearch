Five epigenetic clocks, one hit at p=0.03, twenty-four people, eight controls — this is what noise looks like

The paper's title claims "favorable changes in epigenetic aging markers." The evidence: five DNA-methylation clocks were measured in 24 participants (8 control, 16 intervention). One moved — DNAm PhenoAge (control +4.1 years vs intervention −0.9, p = 0.03), with its matching Rate of Epigenetic Aging (p = 0.03). Horvath, Hannum, GrimAge2 and FitAge were all nonsignificant. No multiplicity correction is reported.

The vault has a settled reading for this arithmetic and consistency requires applying it. When Zhu 2026 reported exactly one significant comparison out of roughly thirty-six BFR hemodynamic cells — significant at 60% occlusion but not at 80%, the higher dose — the vault called it "a dose-response that skips its own top dose is a noise signature" and declined the recommendation built on it (bfr-walking-hemodynamics-modest-young-adults). One hit among five correlated tests at the conventional threshold, uncorrected, in n = 24, is the same object. Under shared/study-appraisal.md's p-hacking lens — many endpoints, no correction, only the winner headlined — this is the canonical pattern.

Two details make it worse rather than merely underpowered.

First, the clocks that failed are the better ones. GrimAge2 and FitAge carry the strongest mortality validation in the wider epigenetic-aging literature. They showed nothing. The one that fired — PhenoAge — is constructed from clinical chemistry markers (albumin, inflammatory and blood-count variables). A six-month nutritional supplement that improves albumin, total protein, calcium and folate — which this trial explicitly reports it did — will move PhenoAge directly, through its inputs, with no change in biological aging required. That is the clock reading the intervention's own nutritional effect back to itself, and it is the most parsimonious explanation on offer.

Second, eight controls. The comparison generating the headline has eight people on one side, in a non-random subsample (COVID disrupted sample collection). Whatever this is, it is not a stable estimate.

Consistency check — the vault applies this to results it likes too. The vault has repeatedly noted that free exercise beats supplements on the supplements' own surrogate markers, and an epigenetic-clock win for a lifestyle intervention is a result it would enjoy. Shan 2026 (n = 145,465) found physical activity associated with lower GrimAge/Horvath and null on Hannum/PhenoAge — note that the clock that fired here is one of the two that failed there, in a study six thousand times larger. The pattern across sources is inconsistent, which is what a noisy surrogate literature looks like.

So the vault carries no epigenetic-aging claim from this source. Not refuted — unestablished. The functional results stand on their own and do not need this (multidomain-intervention-improves-frailty-and-function-frail-elders). And these are surrogates regardless: no participant in this trial lived longer, and epigenetic clocks are markers of an association with aging, not outcomes.

Rebutted by: multidomain-lifestyle-null-on-five-of-six-cerebrovascular-mri-markers — the same one-of-N shape recurs in US POINTER at a far better sample size (one of six markers, n=959, prespecified stratum), and there too the marker that moved was the newest while the validated one stayed flat. Read together they say the pattern is about which instruments are novel, not about which interventions work.
