Standing instead of sitting tracked with less cancer — by a very small amount

Using a dual 24-hour compositional analysis that decomposed the same day twice — once by posture, once by intensity — and minute-by-minute isotemporal substitution, reallocating 15 minutes of sedentary time or sleep to "moving" associated with HR 0.98 (95% CI 0.97–0.99) for physical-activity-related cancer incidence, with a similar reduction from 30 minutes of extra standing. No exercise intensity was required for the association to appear.

What this adds to sedentary-behavior is architectural. The map previously held an observational mortality association across 2.78 million people with no pooled effect size (sedentary-time-independent-of-exercise-mortality-risk), a randomised metabolic result in 16 people (sitting-breaks-add-insulin-benefit-beyond-exercise), and a randomised cognitive result across 21 acute crossover trials (sitting-breaks-small-acute-cognitive-benefit). This is the first source to combine a chronic exposure, a device-measured instrument, a hard registry endpoint, and an effect size — and it names standing as a behaviour in its own right rather than folding it into "activity."

And the effect size, having finally arrived, is tiny — the authors say so themselves. Their stated limitation is that "the small E-values leaving open the possibility for residual confounding nullifying findings smallest in magnitude." An HR of 0.98 per fifteen minutes is exactly the magnitude that ordinary unmeasured confounding — general healthiness, undiagnosed illness, socioeconomic gradient — can manufacture. "Standing is associated with less cancer" is defensible; "standing prevents cancer" is not.

The design earns real credit on the two things this vault usually complains about. Reverse causation was actively probed by sequentially removing cancers diagnosed in follow-up years 1 and 2, and confounding was quantified with E-values rather than waved at. Against that: isotemporal substitution is a regression counterfactual, not an intervention — nobody in this study was asked to stand up — the sample is 96% white with known healthy-volunteer bias, exposure was measured once over a week, and wrist accelerometry infers posture rather than measuring it.

Capped suggestive; Status held emerging on sedentary-behavior — a cohort-tier mixed-reliability source cannot promote a map, and these effects sit at the magnitude its own authors say confounding may account for. The map's open gap is untouched: every randomised result on it is still a single acute session, and nothing bridges those to this chronic association.
