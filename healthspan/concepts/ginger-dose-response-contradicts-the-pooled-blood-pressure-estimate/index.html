The dose subgroup meant to explain ginger's effect is weaker than the effect, and the diastolic dose-response runs the wrong way

A dose subgroup earns its place by showing that the effective dose carries a larger effect than the diluted pool. This one does the opposite.

  • Pooled systolic effect: −5.03 mmHg.
  • The ≥ 2000 mg/day subgroup: −3.76 mmHg.

The supposedly effective dose underperforms the average of all doses. That is not what a dose-response looks like; it is what noise looks like when it is cut two ways.

⚑ And the modelled dose-response contradicts the pooled diastolic result outright. The nonlinear models report a systolic coefficient of −1.03 (p = 0.028) and a diastolic coefficient of +11.35 (p = 0.019). The diastolic coefficient is positive while the pooled diastolic effect is −2.03 mmHg. More ginger, higher diastolic pressure — from the same paper that reports ginger lowering it. Both are cited in support of the conclusion that "doses ≥ 2000 mg/day may be needed."

⚠ The protocol was not prospectively registered, by the authors' own statement in their limitations. So no subgroup, threshold or model in this paper can be distinguished from a post-hoc choice — including the 2000 mg/day cut that is the paper's only actionable-sounding number.

⚠ Six or more stratifications, no family-wise correction reported. Dose, baseline SBP, baseline DBP, sex, health status and duration, across two outcomes. shared/study-appraisal.md's p-hacking lens describes many comparisons with no correction and only the winners foregrounded; the abstract foregrounds the winners, and the health-status split — the one partition that should have been robust — is null on both sides (ginger-blood-pressure-effect-vanishes-in-both-halves-of-the-population-split). This is the fatal flag that lands the source at unreliable.

⚑ Why this note exists separately from the population-split note. They are two independent failures of the same estimate, and either alone would be a caveat. Together they mean the pooled number is not describing a dose of a treatment: it does not survive the population split, and it does not organise by dose. shared/safety.md forbids synthesising a dose no source credibly states — and 2000 mg/day is exactly the kind of number that would otherwise escape a paper's limitations section and become a label claim.
