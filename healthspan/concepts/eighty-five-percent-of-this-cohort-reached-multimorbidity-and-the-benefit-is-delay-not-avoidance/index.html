Eighty-five percent of them ended up with multiple chronic conditions anyway — what the lifestyle arm bought was time

The headline is a hazard ratio of 0.79 for intensive lifestyle versus placebo. The counts underneath it are:

ArmReached 2+ chronic conditions
Intensive lifestyle316 / 385 (82%)
Metformin327 / 385 (85%)
Placebo350 / 403 (87%)
All997 / 1,173 (85%), median 5 conditions (IQR 3 to 7)

⚑ A five-percentage-point gap in who got sick, and a 21% gap in the rate at which they got there. Those are both correct descriptions of the same data, and they land very differently on a reader.

A hazard ratio compares the instantaneous rate of an event, not the eventual cumulative share. When almost everybody eventually has the event — and here 85% did, with a median of five conditions each — a hazard ratio is measuring how long people went before it happened, and can be substantial while the endpoint percentages sit within a few points of each other.

For a vault whose north star is healthspan rather than lifespan, delay is the point. GOAL.md is explicit: healthspan, not lifespan; supporting the body while living a happy life. Years lived before the fifth chronic condition arrives is the outcome of interest, and a rate-based measure captures it better than a binary "did you get sick" ever could. The result should not be discounted for being about timing.

⚠ It should equally not be inflated. "A 21% lower risk of multimorbidity" invites the reading that a fifth of the lifestyle arm escaped chronic disease. They did not: 82% of them did not escape it. This vault's first appraisal lens is relative-versus-absolute risk, and this is the case where the two framings of an honest result diverge most sharply. The defensible sentence is: three years of coached diet and exercise in midlife was associated with reaching multiple chronic conditions later, not with avoiding them.

A second consequence worth recording. The analysis is discriminating among people who nearly all got sick, in a cohort selected for surviving 25 years and reaching Medicare age (the-dppos-multimorbidity-result-carries-36-percent-of-the-randomised-cohort). That is a high-event-rate setting, which is good for statistical power and bad for extrapolating the absolute benefit to a healthier, younger population — where both the baseline rate and the achievable absolute reduction would be smaller.
