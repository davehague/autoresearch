Two estimates in the whole network beat exercise; the rest only beat doing nothing

A network meta-analysis with two comparators lets you ask two different questions, and they are not equally interesting. Non-intervention (NI) is sitting still. Routine intervention (RI) is conventional exercise or usual care. A result against NI says movement beats stillness. Only a result against RI says the digital delivery contributed something.

Hu 2026 ran 45 network comparisons across three cognitive outcomes. Two cleared the active comparator.

  • Non-immersive exergame vs routine intervention, global cognition: SMD 0.32 (95% CI 0.12-0.53). Console-based motion-sensor games — Wii, Kinect, step mats — measured on untrained cognitive outcomes, beating conventional exercise by about a third of a standard deviation.
  • Remote exercise vs routine intervention, memory: SMD 1.22 (0.15-2.28). Home training delivered by videoconference or app, without gamification.

Everything else that reached significance did so against non-intervention only: exergame vs NI for global cognition (0.51) and executive function (0.39), immersive VR vs NI for global cognition (0.74), remote exercise vs NI for memory (1.30).

⚑ The two survivors are the two least technologically elaborate categories in the network. A console game on a television and a person leading a class over Zoom beat conventional exercise; head-mounted virtual reality and VR-plus-cognitive-training did not. The category that topped the SUCRA ranking is not among the two — see immersive-vr-tops-the-cognition-ranking-on-four-trials.

⚠ Both survivors are heavily qualified. The exergame estimate's prediction interval runs -0.20 to 0.86, so the next trial could find a small harm. CINeMA rated this comparison low certainty — and it is the best rating in the global-cognition network, where the other 14 comparisons are very low. The remote-exercise memory result is the paper's most robust estimate by prediction interval and, by the authors' own statement, "rests on a single direct comparison."

What makes this worth having anyway: the outcomes were required to be untrained. A trial could not qualify by measuring the task it drilled. That eligibility rule attacks the transfer problem directly, which is what cognitive-training exists to worry about, and it means these estimates are about generalisation rather than practice effects.
