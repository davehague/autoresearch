Three sessions and a hundred and twelve sessions produced the same effect, and that is not good news

What was tested. The included regimens spanned an extraordinary range: one trial ran three 30-minute sessions over three days; another ran ten sessions a week for ten weeks (roughly 100); the maximum was 112 sessions. A meta-regression of session count against effect size returned β = −0.0079 (95% CI −0.0203 to +0.0045), p = 0.21 — flat.

⚑ The authors read this as convenience and the vault reads it as a signature. Their conclusion: "This suggests that the frequency is not a key factor in improving sleep quality. The study found that performing PMR once a day for three consecutive days and twice a day for eight consecutive weeks improved sleep quality, which confirms this view." ⚑ A 37-fold difference in exposure producing an indistinguishable outcome is not usually what a physiological intervention looks like. It is what an intervention whose effect is delivered by the act of being enrolled looks like.

⚑ The comparison to the rest of this vault is the argument. Interventions in this corpus with real, measured effects show dose gradients:

  • exercise for blood pressure has a fitted curve peaking at ~1,200 METs-minutes/week (exercise-blood-pressure-dose-response-peaks-at-1200-met-minutes-then-rebounds);
  • protein supplementation has a plateau at 30–35 g/day and a credible per-gram coefficient (supplemental-whey-dose-response-plateaus-at-30-to-35-grams-and-later-with-age);
  • daily steps has a non-linear mortality dose-response (daily-steps-mortality-dose-response). Three separate literatures, three shapes. Here, a straight flat line across a 37-fold range.

⚠ The honest counter-arguments, and why they do not rescue it.

  • Ceiling effect — maybe three sessions is already enough. Possible, but a ceiling reached in three sessions of a relaxation technique in people with cancer, COPD and end-stage kidney disease is itself a remarkable claim requiring its own evidence.
  • Underpowered regression — 14 data points is few, and a flat line could be noise. True, and the interval (−0.020 to +0.005) does not exclude a modest gradient. But that cuts against reading the flatness as a feature, which is what the paper does.
  • Confounded by population — trial length correlates with disease severity and setting. Also true, and the authors could not meta-regress anything else because "there was insufficient information" on every other variable.

Where this lands. Taken with the-relaxation-sleep-effect-was-measured-almost-entirely-against-doing-nothing and the-relaxation-sleep-literature-fails-its-own-publication-bias-test, three independent signatures point the same way in one paper: no active comparator, a failed publication-bias test, and no dose-response. Any one is a caveat. All three together are why this source is appraised unreliable and moves nothing.

Capped single-study. stance: pushes-back — against the source's own reading of its null meta-regression as a practical convenience.
