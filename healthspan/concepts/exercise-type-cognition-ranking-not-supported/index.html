"Which exercise is best for your brain" — the ranking is noise, and the paper producing it says so in its own Results

A network meta-analysis of 18 RCTs (n=1,316) ranked aerobic, resistance, mixed, cognitive and stretching training for cognition in older adults, then prescribed a dose. Its own Results section says:

"Forest plot results indicated no statistically significant differences in efficacy among the various intervention types. Accordingly, the SUCRA-based ranking should be interpreted with extreme caution, as it may reflect only small and uncertain differences rather than clear superiority of any single exercise type."

The abstract, the conclusion and the title proceed as if that sentence were not there.

The tell that settles it: the sham wins. "ST" is defined in the paper's own abbreviation key as Stretching training — the inert active-control arm of the entire exercise-science literature. It ranks first on MoCA (SUCRA 94.7) and first on WAIS (SUCRA 98.6), above both aerobic and resistance training. Either stretching is the most powerful cognitive intervention yet discovered in older adults, or this network is measuring enrollment in a supervised programme — attention, structure, social contact, expectancy — and scattering that effect across whichever arm the noise favours.

Everything else in the paper agrees with the second reading. A different modality wins on each of the four scales (MMSE→mixed; MoCA→stretching and resistance; WAIS→stretching and aerobic; WCST→aerobic), with no correction for multiplicity. MoCA's pooled effect crosses zero (SMD 0.93, −0.03 to 1.89) with I² = 97.2%. WAIS rests on 4 trials, n=211. WCST is 2 studies of aerobic training — no network was estimable at all, so the "network meta-analysis" for that outcome is a two-study pairwise pool.

Why this note is filed as pushes-back, and what it pushes back against. Not against exercise — against the claim structure that a ranking table implies. The vault holds cognition claims for creatine-memory-older-adults, multivitamin-modest-cognition-bp-benefit, omega3-no-global-cognition-benefit and multidomain-lifestyle-cognitive-impairment-benefit-overweight. It would be easy — and wrong — to now add "and resistance training is second-best for MoCA." There is no vault-quality basis for ranking any exercise modality against any other for cognition, and the source that appears to offer one refutes itself on page 6.

The third specimen of the same failure in eight days. rt-intensity-safety-comparison-underpowered (falls RR 2.68, CI to 11.11, called a "similar safety profile") and rt-non-failure-nulls-hypertrophy-endurance-power-underpowered (four underpowered nulls called "as effective") were both caught by reading past the abstract into the numbers. This is the same error in its purest form: statistically indistinguishable arms, presented as a ranked prescription. The abstract is where evidence gets laundered.

Practical consequence, and it cuts against the paper: its recommended "60–90 min per session, ≥3×/week, ≥12 weeks" is a dose built on comparisons the authors admit cannot distinguish the arms. GOAL.md asks for the least work for the maximum benefit, and the vault's three most recent training results all point the same way — less is enough (rt-non-failure-beats-failure-dynamic-strength, high-intensity-rt-beats-moderate-for-lower-limb-strength, resistance-training-lowers-mortality's ~90–119 min/week plateau). A 60–90-minute prescription derived from a null comparison is not a reason to spend more of a day.
