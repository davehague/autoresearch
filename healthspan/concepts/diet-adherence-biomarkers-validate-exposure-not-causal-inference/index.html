A nutrient biomarker proves people ate the food — it says nothing about who eats it

Dhana 2026 validated self-reported MIND-diet adherence against plasma lutein-zeaxanthin (β = 0.088, 0.038–0.137) and β-carotene (β = 0.075, 0.001–0.150): participants who reported eating more plants had measurably more plant nutrients in their blood. This is real, unusual, and worth crediting — the food-frequency questionnaire's known measurement error is one of the standing objections this vault raises against every self-reported dietary claim it holds.

And it does not do the work it is implicitly asked to do. Biomarker validation answers "was the exposure really received?" The question that decides whether the association means anything is "what else is true of the people who received it?" Those are different problems, and the first is routinely offered as an answer to the second.

The confounding survives the validation intact. The people who raised their MIND score by four points over three years, in their seventies, while enrolled in a trial, differ from those who did not — in executive function, in household support, in health literacy, in baseline cognitive trajectory. A carotenoid measurement confirms that those people ate more carrots. It does not make them comparable to the people who did not.

The rule this vault exports from it. A validated exposure raises the ceiling on what an observational diet study can claim; it does not change the tier, and it does not touch healthy-adherer bias, reverse causation, or residual confounding. Applied here and to every future diet source: a nutrient biomarker upgrades recall/information bias from RED toward GREEN in shared/study-appraisal.md's observational lens set — one lens of seven — and leaves the rest where they were.

Read at its true strength. single-study, from an unreliable source. This is a reading discipline, not a finding — recorded so that a future run does not promote an FFQ-based dietary claim to higher certainty on the strength of a plasma nutrient level.

Where it applies in this vault today. mediterranean-diet rests on self-reported adherence scores across 54 cohorts; fermented-foods rests on self-reported intake across 50 cohorts; protein-target's daily-total claim is measured in trials rather than questionnaires and is unaffected. None of the three is strengthened by a biomarker appearing in a fourth paper.

For David: nothing to act on. The point is defensive — when a diet study advertises "biomarker-validated," that phrase describes its exposure measurement, not its causal claim.
