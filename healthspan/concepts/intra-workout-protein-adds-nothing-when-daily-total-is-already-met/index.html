Protein sipped during the workout bought nothing measurable at the level of the muscle fibre — in men who were already above the daily protein target

Seventeen recreationally active young men trained a supervised whole-body resistance programme for eight weeks while drinking, during every session, either carbohydrate plus ~12–15 g of protein or the identical calories as carbohydrate alone. Muscle was biopsied before and after and analysed fibre type by fibre type.

Nothing separated the groups. Not the pre-designated primary outcome (mean muscle fibre cross-sectional area); not fibre size at the 10th, 50th or 90th percentile of the size distribution, for any fibre type; not capillary supply; not lean mass by DXA; not bench-press or knee-extension strength; not VO₂max; not fat oxidation; not testosterone or IGF-1.

Why this is worth having despite n = 17, and it is not the usual reason. This vault refuses underpowered nulls as demonstrations of equivalence, and this one is refused on the same terms — no power calculation was performed, 41% of those randomised did not complete, and the authors state plainly that "both positive and negative effects cannot be excluded." What makes it informative anyway is what the paper measured alongside the outcome: total protein intake. Both arms landed at or above the ~1.6 g/kg/day plateau that protein-target holds as the actual lever, and the arm receiving no intra-workout protein ate more protein overall — 1.81 versus 1.65 g/kg (protein-timing-null-arms-both-sat-above-the-daily-plateau).

So the honest reading is not "the trial failed to find a benefit." It is "there was no benefit available to find." Morton 2018's meta-regression across 49 randomised trials — the anchor that put this map at consolidating — puts the muscle payoff plateauing around 1.62 g/kg/day, with nothing further above it (protein-supplementation-augments-resistance-training). Both arms were on the flat part of that curve. A null there is what the map predicts, and getting it at the resolution of a muscle biopsy is a more direct confirmation than another pooled estimate would be.

The measurement quality is the reason to take it seriously at all. Hypertrophy was assessed by immunohistochemistry on biopsied tissue with fibre-type-specific morphometry — not a tape measure, not bioimpedance — cross-checked against DXA lean mass, which agreed. Fibre-size change was analysed with quantile mixed models at three points across the size distribution rather than only at the mean, so a benefit confined to the smallest or largest fibres would have been visible. And blinding held through the statistical analysis, with appearance- and flavour-matched drinks prepared outside the study team.

⚠ What this does not generalise to, and it is the case that actually matters. Because both arms were fed a pre-exercise meal, both received an identical post-exercise protein drink, and both exceeded the daily target, this trial cannot speak to anyone whose protein intake is inadequate — which is the one scenario where intra-workout protein is plausibly useful, and which the authors name as their open question: "particularly when protein intake is suboptimal or training is performed in the fasted state." The null generalises to people already hitting the target and stops there.

Where it sits in the timing thread. protein-target's timing/distribution sub-thread already holds two independent pooled failures — Casuso 2025 in general adults (protein-timing-distribution-null-on-lean-mass) and Ikeda 2026 in adults over 60 (morning-protein-null-on-muscle-mass-and-strength-in-older-adults). Ikeda's was carried at a weakened reading because its comparator was "morning protein versus no morning protein," confounding timing with total intake (morning-protein-comparator-is-no-protein-not-redistribution). This trial is free of that confound, which is its distinctive contribution: it is the vault's cleanest instance of the timing question asked in the only condition where the answer means anything — with the total already met.

Bias-watch, running the rare direction. The trial was co-funded by a dairy cooperative and the protein arm did not win. This map already carries dairy funding on Morton, a dairy-manufacturer co-author on Trommelen and a protein-brand advisory seat on Schoenfeld; a sponsor-adverse null is the one configuration in which industry funding is an argument for a result rather than against it (sponsor-adverse-findings-and-sponsor-friendly-framing-can-coexist).
