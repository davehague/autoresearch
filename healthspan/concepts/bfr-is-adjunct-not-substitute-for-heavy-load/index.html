BFR is an adjunct to load, not a substitute for it — the escape from the knee gate is still not the escape

blood-flow-restriction was coined on 2026-07-12 for one reason: resistance-training caps heavy lower-limb loading at "proposed — review with your doctor/physio" because the paper recommending ≥70% 1RM reported falls RR 2.68 (CI 0.65–11.11) and called it "similar safety" — an underpowered null sold as reassurance (rt-intensity-safety-comparison-underpowered). That leaves a genuine trap: the loading that builds the most lower-limb strength is the loading a recovering patella cannot have.

Occlusion training is the textbook escape — heavy-load-like adaptation at 20–40% 1RM. The map's logged gap named exactly what would settle it: low-load BFR vs heavy load, in middle-aged adults, with a knee to protect, with adverse events counted — and predicted where to look ("BFR in ACL rehabilitation and return-to-sport"). The next sweep found that literature. It does not close the gap.

What the two syntheses actually tested. Xu 2026 (10 RCTs, athletes) added BFR to training. Hou 2026 (11 RCTs, PFPS patients) compared BFRT against general rehabilitation exercise — a comparator set spanning sham/no-restriction, usual care/physiotherapy, high- or low-load resistance training, and non-training control. Hou concedes the contrasts were often not dose-matched: "the intervention and control groups could also differ in exercise content, total training dose (sets × repetitions × load)." Neither synthesis isolates the question — can occlusion buy the heavy-load adaptation without the heavy load?

And the authors settle the framing against the vault's hope, explicitly:

BFRT should be considered "a potential adjunctive load-management strategy for patients who have difficulty tolerating higher-load exercise, rather than a replacement."

Read that carefully, because it is not a disappointment — it is a more honest tool. BFR is not a way to skip the load. It is a way to tolerate it. What Hou demonstrates is analgesia during loaded knee tasks (bfr-reduces-patellofemoral-pain-during-loaded-tasks) and a small strength gain (SMD 0.28), with no improvement in reported knee function (bfr-knee-function-null-in-patients). The paper's own explanation for that functional null is that improvement in PFPS "may depend more on the overall quality of exercise delivery and adherence than on any single technique."

Which is the whole finding, compressed: the exercise does the work; the cuff makes the exercise possible.

Consequence for the vault: the knee gate on resistance-training stays exactly where it is. BFR does not lift it, because BFR does not do the thing the gate needed doing. What BFR is now is a plausible answer to a different and equally real question — how do you keep loading a knee that hurts too much to load? — and that question belongs to a physio, not to this document. safety_flags: [high-dose-risk] stands on bfr-safety-not-established-in-trials.
