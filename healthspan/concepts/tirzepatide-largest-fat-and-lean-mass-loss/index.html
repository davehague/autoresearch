The drug that takes the most fat also takes the most muscle: tirzepatide, −25.7% fat and −8.3% lean mass

Across 19 weight-loss drugs compared in a BMJ network meta-analysis, tirzepatide reduced fat mass the most (−25.7%) — and reduced lean mass the most (−8.3%). Both superlatives belong to the same agent, in the same comparison.

Why this matters for the vault. glp1-lean-mass-loss-magnitude already gave the vault a number for lean-mass loss (−1.74 kg pooled, −5.44 kg on semaglutide, from 7 placebo-controlled RCTs). This extends it into a gradient, and the gradient is the useful part: the harder a drug pulls weight off, the more of that weight is muscle. Efficacy and muscle cost are not independent axes to be traded against each other — they rise together. A person choosing the most effective agent is, on this evidence, choosing the largest lean-mass loss.

It also re-frames the companion finding glp1-body-composition-proportion-improves, which reported lean mass rising as a share of total weight (+1.81%). That share can rise while 8.3% of the lean tissue itself disappears, because fat falls faster. The proportional number is a denominator effect; the absolute number is what carries strength, metabolic rate, and fall resistance into old age.

Bounded honestly (why suggestive, from a reliable source). The source's reliability verdict does not cap strength here, but the evidence does. Body composition was reported by only a subset of the 262 trials, measured with varying instruments (DXA, BIA), over trials of 12–172 weeks; and lean mass is an imaging compartment, never muscle function. The vault's standing gap holds and is unchanged by this paper: no pooled trial measured strength, power, or physical function during treatment. Do not fabricate the functional cost — and do not assume it is proportional to the mass.

The practical shape of the claim. safety_flags: [prescription, interaction]. The countermeasure stack — resistance-training + protein-target (+ creatine-monohydrate) — is not optional garnish, and it scales with the drug's potency. Per shared/safety.md, GLP-1 is a prescription and is never ranked in a protocol; this is a clinician-conversation item, flagged in both directions so it cannot be missed.
