Four times the antigen prevents hospital admissions in people over 65 — decisively, consistently, and by about 29 admissions per 10,000 people vaccinated

The comparison is high-dose influenza vaccine (60 micrograms of haemagglutinin per strain) against standard-dose (15 micrograms). Haemagglutinin is the viral surface protein the immune system learns to recognise; older immune systems respond to it weakly, which is the reason a four-times-stronger version exists. Everyone in every trial was vaccinated — this is a dose question inside an already-vaccinated population, not a case for or against vaccination.

Four outcomes moved, all at GRADE high certainty, which is the top of a four-level scale describing how much further research would be expected to change the estimate:

All-cause hospitalisationRR 0.97 (0.95–0.99)958 → 929, 29 fewer (−48 to −10)I² = 8.7%

I² is the fraction of the spread between trial results that reflects genuine disagreement rather than chance. At 0% the trials are telling one story. Across half a million randomised people that is unusual, and it is what earns the high certainty rating.

The most important line in the table is the one with the weakest relative effect. All-cause hospitalisation falls by 3% — and that 3% is the largest absolute benefit in the paper, 29 admissions avoided per 10,000, because the baseline rate is 958 per 10,000 and three per cent of a large number beats thirty-nine per cent of a small one. Reading this literature off relative risks alone inverts the ranking of what matters.

What it is worth in plain terms. Roughly one hospital admission of any kind avoided for every 345 older adults given the high-dose shot instead of the standard one, per season. That is a real public-health quantity and a modest individual one — which is close to how the review's own authors put it: the evidence "does not support routine preferential use across all older adults irrespective of context," and the value depends on baseline risk, cost and supply.

Why this is graded open-question rather than a confirmed benefit. The precision behind these estimates rests disproportionately on two very large open-label pragmatic trials that read outcomes off routine administrative records, in a literature the review states is mostly industry-sponsored (enhanced-vaccine-evidence-rests-on-open-label-administrative-data-trials). For the two smallest relative effects — 0.92 and 0.97 — there is not much distance between a real small effect and differential coding in unblinded data. The source is appraised mixed and this claim is capped suggestive accordingly.

Bounded in four named ways. Antigenic match could not be examined — how well each season's vaccine matched the viruses actually circulating is the dominant driver of flu vaccine performance, and the pooled numbers are averages across good and bad seasons. Participants were mostly community-dwelling and mostly in high-income countries, with care-home residents and immunocompromised people barely represented — exactly the groups where the benefit might be largest. Adjuvanted and recombinant vaccines were excluded, so this says nothing about the two other ways of enhancing a flu shot. And pneumonia hospitalisation, the outcome closest to the mechanism, was the one that did not hold up — RR 0.85 (0.66 to 1.08) with I² of 72%, graded low certainty.

No safety signal. Serious adverse events came in at RR 0.97 (0.93 to 1.01) across 548,464 people, graded low certainty for imprecision and risk of bias — so the higher dose is not buying its hospitalisation benefit with a measurable harm, on a base large enough that a common one would show.
