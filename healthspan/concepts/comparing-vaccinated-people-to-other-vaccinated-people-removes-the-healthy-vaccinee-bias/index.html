The trick that makes this vaccine study different: everybody in it got vaccinated

Healthy-vaccinee bias is the reason most observational vaccine-benefit findings are worth very little. People who show up for optional vaccinations are, on average, more health-conscious, more engaged with medical care, better off, and more likely to be doing five other things that lower their risk of nearly everything. Compare them to unvaccinated people and the vaccine gets credit for all of it. The bias is large, it points in the flattering direction, and adjusting for measured covariates does not remove it because the relevant differences are mostly unmeasured.

⚑ This study removes it by construction rather than by adjustment. In October 2017 the United States switched rapidly from the live attenuated shingles vaccine to the recombinant one. The authors compare people vaccinated in April–September 2017 (98.6% received the live vaccine) with people vaccinated in April–September 2018 (93.5% received the recombinant one).

Both groups are people who chose to get a shingles vaccine. They differ in which product happened to be on the shelf when they turned up. The same months a year apart also cancels seasonal effects. Whatever makes vaccinated people healthier than unvaccinated people is present in equal measure on both sides and cancels out.

This is instrumental-variable reasoning, and the authors argue each assumption separately rather than asserting them. The instrument is the calendar window. Monotonicity — nobody would have chosen the recombinant vaccine when unavailable and refused it once available — is argued as having no realistic behavioural mechanism. Exclusion restriction — the calendar window affects cardiovascular outcomes only through which vaccine was given — is defended by the null negative-control outcome and the null tetanus-vaccine comparison. Interference is dismissed because cardiovascular events are not contagious.

⚑ And the paper contains its own demonstration that the bias is real and large. The authors also ran the conventional comparison — recombinant shingles vaccine recipients against influenza or tetanus vaccine recipients — and got larger effects on the primary endpoint and every component. They attribute the difference to exactly the bias their main design avoids. The same data, analysed the old way, gives a more flattering answer. That is the cleanest illustration of healthy-vaccinee bias this vault holds.

⚠ What the design does not fix, and it is the same thing every time. Removing healthy-vaccinee bias does not remove secular drift in who gets vaccinated. The transition followed updated recommendations from the US Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, which the authors concede "might have influenced who got vaccinated." They matched on 83 covariates and achieved standardised mean differences ≤0.1 on all of them — but socioeconomic status is not in electronic health records and lifestyle is only a proxy (an-e-value-of-1-4-is-a-low-bar-for-an-unmeasured-confounder).

⚑ The generalisable lesson for this vault, which ingests observational claims constantly: the question to ask is not "did they adjust for confounders?" but "did they build a comparison where the confounder cannot differ?" Adjustment works on what you measured. Design works on what you didn't. This vault's other example of the same move is alcohol, coined on a Mendelian randomisation review — a different technique aimed at the same target, using genetic variants fixed at conception so they cannot be caused by lifestyle. Both are worth more than a large adjusted cohort, and both are still observational.

In one sentence: comparing vaccinated people to other vaccinated people is the design move that makes this result worth arguing with, and the paper proves the point by showing what the conventional analysis of the same data would have claimed instead.
