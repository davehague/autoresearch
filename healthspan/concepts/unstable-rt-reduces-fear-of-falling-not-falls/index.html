Unstable-surface training improved the questionnaire about falling — nobody counted the falls

A meta-analysis of 7 RCTs in older adults found that unstable resistance training (URT) beat stable resistance training (SRT) on the Falls Efficacy Scale-International after 10–12 weeks, and on Timed Up and Go at 24 weeks.

FES-I measures concern about falling. It is not a fall count. No fall incidence, injury count, or adverse event appears anywhere in this paper's outcome set — in a synthesis of deliberately destabilised loading in older adults, where the risk of falling is the obvious question to ask. Feeling steadier and being less likely to hit the floor are different quantities, and interventions that improve the first without the second are common enough that the distinction has to be held.

And this is the most expectation-prone endpoint the design could have produced. URT vs SRT is unblindable — a participant knows perfectly well they spent twelve weeks balancing on unstable surfaces — the outcome is self-reported, and it asks about confidence in balance, which is the exact construct the intervention advertises. Expectation and endpoint point the same way. The same lens that discounted resistance-training-depression-effect-heterogeneous-unblindable and walking-subjective-sleep-quality-large-effect applies here, and it is why this note is graded single-study despite sitting on a meta-analysis-rct tier — the design ceiling is high, the reliability this study earns is not (bayesian-certainty-cannot-repair-biased-inputs is the general form).

The 24-week Timed Up and Go result is a single positive stratum among roughly ten comparisons (strength, mobility, fear of falling × two time points) with no correction for multiple testing reported, in the thinner of the two strata of a seven-trial corpus. Recorded, not banked.

It does not touch the gap resistance-training-programming has open. That gap asks for a trial powered on falls and adverse events comparing training intensities in adults ≥50 (rt-intensity-safety-comparison-underpowered). This paper compares surfaces, at unspecified intensity, and measures fear. Two substitutions, neither of which closes it.

For David: an unstable surface is exactly the condition under which a healing patella is asked to stabilise unpredictably, and nothing here enrolled anyone in orthopaedic recovery. No new safety_flag is coined — the map's existing proposed — review with your doctor/physio cap on lower-limb loading already governs, and unstable-surface variants inherit it. There is a real, self-reported confidence benefit on offer; there is no demonstrated fall-risk benefit to weigh against a knee.
