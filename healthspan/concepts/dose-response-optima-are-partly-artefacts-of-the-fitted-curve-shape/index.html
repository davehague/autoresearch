A parabola always has a peak — so "the optimal dose is 1,500" is partly a fact about the model

A Bayesian dose-response network meta-analysis fits a curve of effect against dose instead of lumping all exercise together. That is a genuine advance. The catch is that the curve's shape is chosen, and the shape decides what kinds of answers are possible.

Here, eight candidate dose-response functions were compared and the quadratic fit best. A quadratic is a parabola: it has exactly one turning point, always. The model could not return "no optimum," "monotonically increasing," or "we can't tell." It was structurally obliged to produce a peak somewhere; the data only decided where.

⚑ The paper contains its own proof that this is a live failure, not a quibble. For mind-body exercise, the fitted optimum landed at 140 MET-min/week — entirely below the observed data range of 300–500 for that modality. The authors flag it in the table, the figure caption and the text as "an unreliable extrapolation that should not be interpreted as an effective dose." A model that returns an optimum outside its own data has demonstrated that it manufactures optima.

They caught that one. There is no equivalent guarantee for the others — only that the remaining optima fall inside the observed range, which is a weaker check than it appears: the top-ranked doses (1,200–1,600 MET-min/week) sit precisely where the authors say data "thinned out at the higher doses."

The operating rule this establishes. A modelled "optimal dose" is a claim about a fitted function, not a discovered biological threshold. Check three things before believing one: what curve shape was fitted, whether the optimum falls inside the observed data, and how many trials sit near it. Here: quadratic, mostly yes but in the sparse tail, and 9–11 trials for the winning modality.

Credit where due. Marking your own out-of-range output is the behaviour that makes the rest of the table readable. The authors also rated every one of their 13 network comparisons low or very low certainty (CINeMA) and called their doses "provisional, model-based prescription anchors rather than ready-to-apply clinical thresholds."
