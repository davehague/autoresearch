Hydrogen-rich jelly did not beat placebo for sleep — in a trial built well enough to have found it

0.9 mg of hydrogen per day for 8 weeks, in 44 adults recruited for poor sleep, produced no significant advantage over a matched placebo on any sleep or stress instrument the trial measured — not the pre-specified primary outcome (OSA-MA), not the VAS for sleep quality, not the PSQI, not the STAI. Both groups improved from baseline. Neither improved more than the other.

The reason this null carries weight is the trial's construction, and it is worth listing because sleep-supplement trials this careful are rare. It was pre-registered before enrolment (UMIN000053237). It had a real a priori power calculation — 21 per group for a 2.5-point OSA-MA difference at 90% power with a Holm-adjusted 1% alpha — and it enrolled 22 per group. The placebo was matched on appearance, texture, taste and smell, and blinding was formally tested and held: only 18 of 44 participants (40.9%) guessed their allocation correctly, which is chance. Adherence was 95%. Dropout was zero.

So this is not the usual underpowered null. shared/study-appraisal.md fatal flag #3 exists to stop "we saw nothing" being read as "there is nothing" when no power analysis was ever done. Here one was done, in advance, for a specified effect size, and the trial met it. The honest reading is: at this dose, over this duration, in this population, hydrogen did not produce the effect the trial was designed to detect.

Three limits keep it at suggestive rather than strong. The source is appraised mixed, which caps strength — chiefly because the funder manufactures the product (hydrogen-trial-sponsor-made-the-product-whose-primary-endpoint-failed). n = 44 is small, and a trial powered for a 2.5-point difference remains blind to a genuinely smaller one. And every outcome is a questionnaire — no polysomnography, no actigraphy, nothing objective.

What it does establish, plainly. Hydrogen-rich water and hydrogen tablets are marketed into the longevity and wellness market on antioxidant and anti-inflammatory claims, sleep prominent among them. The best-designed trial of the sleep claim, paid for by a manufacturer, found nothing. That is the founding claim of molecular-hydrogen, and the map opens on it.

What the paper is actually about is a different, weaker analysis — a post-hoc split of participants by how much their sleep improved, which found that the hydrogen arm's improvers had fewer hydrogen-producing gut bacteria at baseline (hydrogen-responder-subgroup-is-defined-by-a-post-randomisation-outcome). That analysis does not change this one.
