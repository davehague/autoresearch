Blood flow restriction meaningfully reduces patellofemoral pain during exactly the loaded tasks a bad knee can't do

Eleven RCTs in 508 patients with patellofemoral pain syndrome — the first BFR evidence in this vault drawn from people whose knees actually hurt, rather than from collegiate athletes. Adding blood flow restriction to rehabilitation exercise cut pain during the specific movements a compromised patella fails: shallow single-leg squat MD 2.13 (1.72–2.54), deep single-leg squat 2.38 (1.69–3.08), step-down 2.05 (1.58–2.52) — and the analgesia was still measurable 45 minutes after the session ended. Over longer follow-up, overall pain fell SMD 0.37 (0.16–0.57, I²=22%) and worst pain SMD 0.38 (0.14–0.62, I²=0%).

This clears a bar the source itself sets, which is why it is graded as a benefit rather than a curiosity. Hou cites the minimal clinically important difference for the NPRS in PFPS at ~1.16 points and for a 10-cm VAS at ~2 cm. The observed reductions meet or exceed both. The authors' own verdict — "this magnitude of reduction may be clinically meaningful" — is, for once, an understatement rather than a sales pitch.

Why the vault cares, specifically. GOAL.md names knee/joint-safe as a hard constraint and records David's March 2026 patellar contusion, with return to pickleball gated on consistent stretching. resistance-training carries safety_flags: [high-dose-risk] precisely because the loading that builds the most lower-limb strength is the loading the knee cannot take (rt-intensity-safety-comparison-underpowered). This is the first finding in the entire vault that addresses that constraint directly rather than working around it.

But read what it is, not what it would be convenient for it to be. This is analgesia, not adaptation. It makes a loaded knee task hurt less while you do it. It does not produce the heavy-load strength adaptation the knee gate was looking for a substitute for (bfr-is-adjunct-not-substitute-for-heavy-load), and it does not improve the function the patient actually reports (bfr-knee-function-null-in-patients). The strength gain that accompanies it is small — SMD 0.28 (0.06–0.49).

Capped at suggestive, and the map keeps its high-dose-risk flag. The reasons are on the source: no GRADE assessment, 2 of 11 trials at high risk of bias, comparisons that are not dose-matched (some of the pain benefit may be the extra session rather than the cuff), and — across 21 randomised trials and two independent syntheses now — still no systematic adverse-event tally for a technique that deliberately restricts arterial inflow to a limb (bfr-safety-not-established-in-trials). Cuff pressures in the included trials ranged 50–143.33 mmHg / 60–80% limb occlusion pressure at 20–50% 1RM; the authors say this heterogeneity makes it "difficult to derive clear, quantifiable prescription guidance." No protocol is recommended here, because none was reliably published (shared/safety.md).

Status of the item: proposed — review with your doctor/physio. Not a protocol entry. A specific, answerable question to take to a clinician, which is exactly what this system exists to produce.

Rebutted by: bfr-added-nothing-to-pain-or-physical-function-against-a-sham-cuff — in the same condition, 84 service members randomised to 80% limb occlusion pressure versus a 20 mmHg sham cuff showed no advantage on the Anterior Knee Pain Scale (group P = .15, interaction P = .26), VAS pain (P = .58/.58) or physical quality of life (P = .15/.57); the benefit here rests on trials comparing BFR plus exercise against exercise alone, and it does not survive a comparator that isolates the occlusion itself.
