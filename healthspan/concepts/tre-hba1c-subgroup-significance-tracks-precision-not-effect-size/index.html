The "significant" subgroup has the tiny effect and the "non-significant" one has the large effect

Viple 2026 reports that time-restricted eating improved HbA1c in prediabetes but not in type 2 diabetes. Read as a sentence, that says the intervention works in the milder group. Read as numbers, it says the opposite about magnitude:

SubgroupHbA1c changepVerdict as printed
Prediabetes−0.09 percentage points0.02significant
Type 2 diabetes−0.43 percentage points0.09not significant

The type 2 diabetes effect is nearly five times larger and did not reach significance. Its interval (−10.08 to +0.72 mmol/mol) is simply much wider — fewer trials, more variable participants, more variable control. Significance here is reporting how precisely each subgroup was measured, not how much the intervention did.

And the significant result is clinically empty. A 0.09 percentage point change in HbA1c is smaller than the routine day-to-day and assay-to-assay variation in the test. No clinician would act on it, and no guideline threshold is anywhere near it. So the subgroup that "worked" produced a change nobody could use, and the subgroup that produced a possibly-useful change could not be measured precisely enough to call it.

This is the statistical-versus-clinical-significance lens applied twice in one table, in opposite directions — a paper simultaneously reporting a significant-but-trivial effect and a meaningful-but-imprecise one, and labelling only the first as a finding. shared/study-appraisal.md lists this as a Universal lens precisely because it is common, and it is unusually clean here.

Graded single-study and recorded as a reusable pattern, not as evidence about fasting. It makes no claim about what time-restricted eating does. Its content is how to read the source's own subgroup table, and it is filed because this vault will meet the same shape again — it is a close cousin of the vault's standing refusal to read a SUCRA leaderboard off a sparse network (supplement-outcome-specific-ranking-too-tidy-for-a-sparse-network) and of its repeated refusal to read an underpowered null as equivalence.

⚠ Both estimates are also subgroup estimates from a 10-trial corpus with no stated multiplicity correction, which makes them the least stable numbers in the paper regardless of which way they point. The pooled result — a null at moderate certainty — remains the trustworthy one (time-restricted-eating-null-on-hba1c-in-glucose-dysregulation).
