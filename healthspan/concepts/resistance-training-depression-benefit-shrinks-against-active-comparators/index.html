Resistance training's mood benefit is real but modest once the comparator is active, not inactive

Across 80 RCTs (n=6,123) — more than double this map's prior depression evidence base — resistance exercise training (RET) reduces depressive symptoms with an overall effect of Δ=0.32. That number splits sharply by what RET is compared against: Δ=0.54 vs inactive controls (no treatment, waitlist) but only Δ=0.21 vs active comparators (aerobic exercise, relaxation, mind-body work, education). The effect is also larger in people with a diagnosed mental illness (Δ=0.40) than without (Δ=0.11).

This replaces, for anyone not in treatment for diagnosed depression, the much larger SMD −0.94 this map already carries from resistance-training-reduces-depression-symptoms (Chang 2025, 29 RCTs, non-exercise control only, diagnosed-depression patients). That number was already discounted for heterogeneity and an unblindable, diagnosed-patient design (resistance-training-depression-effect-heterogeneous-unblindable); this much larger, active-vs-inactive-split synthesis shows concretely why — against the fairer test of whether lifting specifically helps, not just "doing something instead of nothing," the effect is small (Δ=0.21, or Δ=0.11 without a diagnosed illness).

Corroborated the same week by a second, independent RCT in a different population: resistance-training-sham-rct-no-intensity-advantage-for-depression (O'Sullivan 2026, young women with elevated anxiety) found no detectable mood advantage of full-intensity lifting over a low-intensity sham — consistent with a small active-comparator effect that a single ordinary-sized trial cannot reliably resolve.
