No weight-loss drug meaningfully improves quality of life (43 RCTs, 45,663 participants)

Within a BMJ network meta-analysis of 262 randomized trials, the quality-of-life question drew on 43 trials and 45,663 participants — and no drug convincingly improved quality of life. Every mean difference fell below 5 points against an established minimally important difference of 10. The same synthesis found no drug convincingly reduced kidney failure.

Why this matters for the vault. GOAL.md opens by naming what David is optimizing for: healthspan, not lifespan; not maxxing every biomarker — supporting the body while living a happy life. Quality of life is the closest thing the trial literature has to a direct measurement of that goal. The most intensively studied weight intervention in modern medicine has now been tested against it in 45,663 people, and the answer is nothing — while the same drugs reliably move the scale, and (in high-risk people) prevent heart attacks.

This is a pushes-back note, and it is important to be precise about what it pushes back on. It does not challenge the cardiovascular benefit — glp1-mace-benefit-rct-select stands, and glp-1 stays consolidating. It challenges the unstated inference that a drug which improves weight, MACE, and body composition must therefore improve how life is actually experienced. On the vault's own terms, that inference is now measured and absent.

Bounded honestly (why strong, and what that does and doesn't mean). The source is reliable (independently funded, PROSPERO-registered, GRADE-rated, RoB 2, with its conflicted authors firewalled from extraction and bias assessment), so strength is not capped. 43 trials and 45,663 participants, consistent across 19 drugs, below the MID: this is about as well-established as a null gets. The honest limits: generic quality-of-life instruments are blunt and may miss changes that matter to an individual; trials ran 12–172 weeks, so a decades-long healthspan effect is untested; and the pooled populations had overweight or obesity, often with comorbidity — a person whose weight is genuinely disabling may experience relief that a population mean cannot see.

The practical shape of the claim. safety_flags: [prescription] — this note never ranks GLP-1 into a protocol (shared/safety.md). What it does is change what belongs in the clinician conversation: the randomized evidence supports weight loss and, in established cardiovascular disease, fewer cardiovascular events. It does not support "you will feel better." Someone weighing ~$100/month against a stated goal of living well deserves to know which of those two things has been measured and which has not.
