In people who have not already had a heart attack, the GLP-1 heart benefit has never been shown — and the trials stop short of being able to show it

Pooling the primary-prevention subgroups of eleven cardiovascular outcome trials, GLP-1 receptor agonists produced a hazard ratio for major adverse cardiovascular events of 0.88 (95% CI 0.74 to 1.05). Not significant. Every secondary outcome — death from any cause, cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke, serious adverse events — was also not significant. GRADE certainty: low.

What the interval's width rules out, which is the whole point. The lower bound, 0.74, is compatible with a 26% relative reduction — a benefit that would be decisive. The upper bound, 1.05, is compatible with a 5% relative increase. This is not a demonstration that the drug does nothing; it is a demonstration that these trials cannot tell. In absolute terms over four years: 81 major cardiovascular events per 1,000 untreated people, 71 per 1,000 treated — 10 fewer, with a range from 21 fewer to 3 more.

⚑ The paper turns "not significant" into "not yet answered," with arithmetic. A trial sequential analysis — the same logic as an interim monitoring boundary, applied to accumulated evidence — put the sample needed to detect the expected 13% relative reduction at 20,119 participants. The primary-prevention subgroup supplies 16,672. The evidence base is roughly 3,400 people short of the amount required to decide, before adjusting for heterogeneity, which would raise the requirement further. Underpowered and ineffective look identical from a p-value; this is what distinguishes them.

⚠ Why this is a scope condition and not a contradiction. The landmark positive result this vault holds — SELECT, HR 0.80 (0.72–0.90), NNT 67 (glp1-mace-benefit-rct-select) — enrolled people with established cardiovascular disease. Nothing here disputes it. The two findings are about different people, and the honest combined statement is: shown in secondary prevention, not shown in primary prevention.

⚠ Harms in this subgroup are effectively unmeasured. Only one trial reported serious adverse events for the primary-prevention subgroup. Four of the five secondary outcomes rest on two point estimates each. The authors state that this prevents any benefit-risk assessment in the population the finding is about — and note that with no strong evidence of benefit, the balance may not favour treatment even if harm is low.

⚠ The subgroup boundary is soft in a direction that favours the drug. "No previous cardiovascular disease" meant the absence of a diagnosis, and silent heart attacks are common in type 2 diabetes; how hard each trial looked is unknown. Undiagnosed disease inside the primary-prevention group would raise its event rate and increase the analysis's power to find a benefit — so this bias works against the null, not for it. Capped suggestive; every included trial is manufacturer-sponsored and rated high risk on the sponsor-bias domain.
