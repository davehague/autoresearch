The number that disqualifies the paper's most quotable results is in the paper, three sections later

The primary outcome was null. Then eight brain regions were examined separately, without adjustment for multiple comparisons, and three results crossed p < 0.05: autoregulation phase in the left premotor cortex (p = 0.020) and left orbitofrontal cortex (p = 0.023), and gain in the left orbitofrontal cortex (p = 0.05). The effect sizes look large — Cohen's d of 1.52, 1.14 and 1.02.

Why testing eight regions changes what p < 0.05 means. A p-value of 0.05 is a promise that a result this extreme happens by chance one time in twenty when nothing is going on. Test eight things and you have taken eight draws, so the chance of at least one false positive rises to roughly one in three. The standard correction divides the threshold by the number of tests: 0.05 ÷ 8 = 0.00625.

⚑ The paper computes that exact number itself. In its future-directions section, planning what a properly powered trial would need: "Sample size estimates for a future adequately powered clinical trial (β = 0.80) with appropriate multiple comparison correction (α = 0.00625) that is based on observed effect sizes from regional dCA analyses in the current study indicate 40 individuals with equal allocation between groups would be sufficient."

⚑ By the standard the authors themselves state, three lines later: 0.020 is not below 0.00625, 0.023 is not below 0.00625, and 0.05 is not below 0.00625. None of the three exploratory findings survives. The paper is not hiding this — it labels the analyses hypothesis-generating, states that they were unadjusted for multiplicity, and says in the discussion that they "need to be explored more thoroughly in an adequately powered RCT rather than suggesting physiological efficacy." The disqualifying arithmetic simply lives in a different section from the results it disqualifies.

⚠ And there is a second reason to discount them, which the authors also supply and then withhold. Randomisation failed on two variables directly relevant to nitric-oxide biology — baseline HDL cholesterol differed at p<0.001 (45 ± 4 mg/dL versus 60 ± 9) and triglycerides at p=0.049. The authors report that adding these as covariates in post-hoc sensitivity analyses "slightly attenuated significant findings (p = 0.06–0.08; data not shown." ⚑ The one analysis that would let a reader judge the exploratory results is described in a parenthesis and not published. Attenuation to 0.06–0.08 means they lose conventional significance too — twice over, once for multiplicity and once for confounding.

A third, from the limitations: end-tidal carbon dioxide was not measured. The authors concede this "cannot rule out the possibility of reductions in PetCO₂... biasing TFA metrics, including artificially increased phase and decreased gain." ⚑ That is a description of precisely the pattern reported as the exploratory finding.

⚑ The standing habit this note exists to install. This is the third time in three days that the number governing how to read a paper has turned out to live outside its results section. 2026-08-26: the CoQ10 analysis's narrative prose rendered interval bounds with a spurious minus sign and only the tables were coherent — "when a paper's prose and its tables disagree, the tables win." 2026-08-27: Mekonnen 2026's stated conclusion asserted a direction its own subgroup analyses measured in reverse. Tonight: the corrected alpha that voids three findings is in the future-directions paragraph. ⚑ Before quoting an exploratory p-value, read the paper's own power and alpha statements — they are usually in the discussion, and they are frequently decisive.

In one sentence: an honest paper reported three exploratory positives, labelled them hypothesis-generating, and then published the corrected threshold that rules all three out — which is why it is trustworthy and why the findings are not.
