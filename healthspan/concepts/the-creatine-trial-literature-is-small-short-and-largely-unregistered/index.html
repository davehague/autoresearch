What every creatine conclusion actually rests on: 24 people for four weeks

A complete census of the randomised creatine literature — 357 reports across 127 journals, mapping to 343 distinct trials published 1994–2023 — gives the base rates:

median participants randomised24
median study arms2
median follow-up4 weeks
protocol registration reported65 of 357 (18.2%)
creatine monohydrate specified275 of 343 (80.2%)
chemical form not specified — just "creatine"53 (15.5%)

The commonest publication venues are Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise and the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. The commonest populations, together about two-thirds of all reports, are healthy participants, athletes, trained or recreationally active people, and older adults.

What this does not do. It does not overturn the meta-analytic findings this vault holds on creatine. Pooling many small short trials is precisely what meta-analysis exists for, and creatine's strength and lean-mass evidence comes from syntheses of 22, 37, 52 and 61 trials — enough that the median trial's size stops being the binding constraint on precision.

What it does do. Three things.

  1. It calibrates how much any single new creatine trial should move anything. The modal new arrival in this field is roughly 24 people for four weeks with no registered protocol. That is a hypothesis, not a result.
  2. It bounds what four weeks can even measure. A median follow-up of four weeks is long enough to saturate muscle stores and detect an acute performance change; it is far too short for bone geometry, for cognitive decline, or for anything the healthspan question actually cares about. The vault's longest-horizon creatine claims all come from the minority of longer trials.
  3. It makes the 15.5% unspecified-form figure a real problem rather than sloppiness. A trial that reports only "creatine" cannot be pooled by formulation, which is exactly the pooling that every-head-to-head-creatine-formulation-trial-gave-monohydrate-the-larger-molar-dose shows nobody has done. The review's own inference is that the true monohydrate share is "over 95%" — an assumption, and a reasonable one, but not an extraction.
