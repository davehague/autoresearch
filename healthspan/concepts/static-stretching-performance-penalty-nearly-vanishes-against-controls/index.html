"Don't static stretch before you train" survives only without a control group

The claim that static stretching before activity impairs performance is one of the most confidently repeated results in applied exercise science. Pooled across 23 acute studies in 627 athletes, static stretching versus a control condition returned −0.07% (95% CI −0.39 to 0.25) — trivial by the review's own pre-defined magnitude bands, and centred essentially on zero. The long-duration (>60 s) version, the one that should be worst, landed at −0.02% vs control.

The uncontrolled version is roughly twelve times larger. Measured pre-to-post within the same participants, static stretching returns −0.88% (−2.17 to 0.40). That gap between −0.88% and −0.07% is the entire finding: the performance penalty lives in the comparison that has no control group in it.

Why that pattern is diagnostic rather than curious. A pre-post comparison absorbs everything that happens between the two measurements — fatigue from the testing protocol itself, time elapsed since warm-up, motivation decay, the ordering of tasks. A control condition that does something other than stretch during that same interval subtracts all of it. When an effect is present in the first comparison and absent in the second, the most economical explanation is that the effect belonged to the interval, not to the stretching.

The other modalities land in the same place, and this is the part the abstract glosses. Dynamic stretching +0.55% (−1.10 to 2.20), combined protocols +0.60% (−0.33 to 1.53), PNF +1.04% (−0.52 to 2.61) on two studies — every one of them describable as "small improvement" and every one of them containing zero. The honest summary of this entire review's performance analysis is that no stretching modality was shown to change acute performance in either direction.

Capped single-study and the source appraised unreliable. The review presents these intervals as directional findings without a power analysis (shared/study-appraisal.md fatal flag #3), computes percentage changes rather than standardised effect sizes, and rests two-thirds on low-quality studies. So this note does not establish that static stretching is harmless — it establishes that this evidence base does not show harm, which is a weaker and more accurate claim.

What it licenses for a recreational adult. Not a protocol change, because stretching has no pre-activity protocol to change. What it licenses is the removal of a worry: there is no credible signal here that stretching before activity costs anything measurable. Combined with the map's standing position that flexibility itself is a weak-to-null independent predictor of health outcomes (flexibility-weak-predictor-of-health-outcomes-adults), the practical upshot is that this is a low-stakes decision in both directions — which is a reasonable thing for a vault to say out loud about a behaviour people agonise over.
