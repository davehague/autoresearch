One in five left, and they left unevenly — which cuts both ways and the trial cannot say which

Fifty-three of 247 participants withdrew over twelve months — 25.6% of the placebo arm against 17.0% of the magnesium arm. The paper reports an intention-to-treat analysis but states no imputation method, so the ITT is in practice a complete-case analysis on an unbalanced remainder.

HbA1c is measured only in people who come back. That makes the direction of the bias genuinely ambiguous, and the vault declines to guess:

  • If the placebo participants who left were doing worse — the ordinary reason someone disengages from a diabetes trial — then the placebo arm's surviving average is flattered, and the true magnesium difference would be larger than reported.
  • If they left because nothing was happening to them, the surviving placebo arm is unrepresentative in the other direction and the reported difference is inflated.

Neither reading is testable from what the paper reports. Reasons for withdrawal are not broken down by arm against baseline characteristics, and with no imputation there is no sensitivity analysis to lean on.

Why this is graded pushes-back rather than recorded as a limitation. It pushes back on the trial's own claim to have delivered an intention-to-treat result. shared/study-appraisal.md's fatal flag #4 fires on a per-protocol analysis concealing non-random dropout, and it does not fire here because ITT is nominally reported — but the margin is thin, and the thing that saves it is a label rather than a method. Recorded at single-study, resting on one trial's internal bookkeeping.

⚠ The asymmetry is a differential-dropout problem, not a magnitude problem. 21.5% attrition over a year in a diabetes trial is unremarkable on its own; 8.6 percentage points of imbalance between arms is what makes it worth a note. For comparison, the vault's best-conducted recent trials ran 5.9% attrition with multiple imputation (Jiang 2026) and zero (Buehler 2026, local-cooling-does-not-speed-recovery-at-any-timing).
