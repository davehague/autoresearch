When wildly different trials agree perfectly, the number is describing the inputs, not the effect

I² is the share of variation between trials that exceeds what chance alone would produce. Low I² is normally read as reassurance: the trials agree, so the pooled estimate is stable. This synthesis reports I² = 0% for six of its seven outcomes and 2% for the seventh.

Now look at what was pooled. Across the 17 trials in Table 1:

  • Intervention duration: 4 weeks to 72 weeks — an eighteen-fold range.
  • Session length: 25 to 60 minutes. Frequency: 3 to 5 sessions per week.
  • Three distinct practices: Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Yi Jin Jing.
  • Seven different comparator conditions: health education, non-intervention, conventional therapy, routine care, remote guidance, "similar," and resistance training.
  • Group mean ages from 62.15 to 88.8 years.
  • Arm sizes from 6 to 63 participants.

⚑ A pool that clinically heterogeneous returning statistical homogeneity of zero is a signal about the input trials, not a reassurance about the output. Twelve weeks of Baduanjin against health education in 70-year-olds and 72 weeks of Tai Chi against nothing in 64-year-olds did not produce measurably different effect sizes. That is not what real biological effects do.

The usual explanations, none of which the paper addresses:

  1. Publication and reporting pressure. A literature of small trials that all report similar-magnitude positive results will look homogeneous because the heterogeneous and null results were never published. This is the most likely explanation for a pool of 17 mostly-small, mostly-single-country, uniformly positive trials.
  2. Imputed or borrowed standard deviations. The methods state that when standard deviations were not reported they were "calculated from standard errors, CIs, t values, or p values." Derived variances tend to be more uniform than measured ones.
  3. Low power to detect heterogeneity. With arms of 6 to 20 participants, the within-trial variance is so large that between-trial variance is hard to resolve — I² can be near zero simply because the trials are too imprecise to disagree measurably.

⚑ The consequence is concrete, not philosophical. The near-zero I² is the paper's stated decision rule for using fixed-effects models as the primary analysis ("A fixed-effects model was used as the primary analytical approach when statistical heterogeneity was low"). Fixed-effects models assume one true underlying effect and produce narrower confidence intervals than random-effects models, which allow the true effect to vary across settings. Every interval in traditional-chinese-exercise-sarcopenia-effects-are-mostly-below-clinical-thresholds is therefore tighter than a random-effects analysis of the same data would give. The paper does run random-effects as a sensitivity analysis and reports the findings as robust — but those estimates are not the ones in the abstract.

⚠ The companion error compounds it. The same paper declares "no publication bias" on the basis of Egger's regression test applied to pools of 13, 8, 7, 5, 3 and 2 studies. Egger's test is unreliable below roughly ten studies. ⚑ So the two diagnostics that would normally detect a small-positive-trials literature — heterogeneity and small-study effects — both returned reassuring values, one implausibly and one from a test with almost no power, and both are reported as evidence that the pool is sound.

The transferable reading rule this vault adopts. ⚑ Compare a pool's statistical heterogeneity against its clinical heterogeneity before believing either. When trials that differ eighteen-fold in duration and twenty-six years in mean age produce I² = 0, ask what made them agree — and check whether that number was used to justify a narrower model.

Capped suggestive on a mixed appraisal.
