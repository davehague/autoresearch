Wearing a weighted vest while exercising beats not exercising — which is a claim about exercising

This is the review's headline and it is a confounded comparison. Vest + exercise versus usual activity: femoral-neck BMD SMD 0.59 (0.05–1.13), lumbar-spine BMD SMD 0.73 (0.29–1.16) at I² = 0%, and body fat down 2.67 kg (−4.08 to −1.27).

⚑ Every one of those numbers is compatible with the exercise doing all of the work. The vault already holds the evidence that it might: moderate-intensity-resistance-training-raises-hip-bone-density-against-control records resistance training raising hip BMD against control with no vest anywhere in it. A bundle beating no-bundle tells you the bundle works; it does not tell you which part.

The review knows this and ran the correct comparison too. Against exercise alone, the bone benefit disappears (weighted-vest-adds-no-bone-density-once-the-exercise-is-controlled-for) and the fat benefit shrinks from 2.67 kg to 0.73 kg (weighted-vest-adds-three-quarters-of-a-kilo-of-fat-loss-to-exercise-you-already-do). That is why this note is filed as an open-question rather than as a benefit: the estimate is real, and what produced it is unresolved.

⚠ Two reasons to discount the intervals as printed. The review used fixed-effect models, which assume one identical true effect across nine trials averaging ~41 participants and narrow the confidence intervals accordingly — the femoral-neck lower bound of 0.05 is the visible consequence, and a random-effects estimate would very likely cross zero. And participants cannot be blinded to whether they are wearing a weighted vest, so effort, adherence and incidental activity all run in the direction of this result.

⚠ GRADE separates these outcomes and the separation matters. The review rates body-fat evidence high certainty and everything else low to moderate. The bone claim in this note is the low-to-moderate half.
