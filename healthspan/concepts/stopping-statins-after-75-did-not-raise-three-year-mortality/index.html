The first randomised answer to "should a 75-year-old keep taking their statin?" — and what it does and does not say

Before this trial there was no randomised evidence at all. The authors searched PubMed from inception to 31 May 2025 for a randomised trial of continuing versus stopping statins for primary prevention in people aged 75 or older and reported: "No such trials were identified." Guidelines had been recommending caution in this age group (European and US Class IIb) on the basis of subgroup analyses of trials that mostly excluded these people.

What was measured. 1,180 adults were randomised 5:4 to continue or stop; 1,160 entered the primary analysis. Over three years, 7.9% of those who continued and 7.2% of those who stopped died. The absolute difference was −0.68 percentage points, 95% confidence interval −3.95 to +2.60 — meaning the data are compatible with stopping saving up to about 4 deaths per 100 people and with it costing up to about 2.6 deaths per 100.

What "non-inferior" means here, precisely. A non-inferiority trial does not test whether two options are the same. It tests whether the worse one is not worse by more than a stated amount — here 5 percentage points of three-year mortality, fixed before the trial started. The upper edge of the observed interval (+2.60) sits below that line, so the pre-specified claim succeeds: stopping did not raise three-year mortality by more than the trial had declared it would tolerate. That is a bounded claim, not a demonstration of equivalence, and not a statement that statins do nothing.

⚠ The precision is the limit, and it is a self-inflicted one. The trial planned to enrol 2,430 people and enrolled 1,180 — recruitment was halted for slow accrual and exhausted funding, and the sample size was never recalculated. A trial at 49% of its planned size has an interval roughly 40% wider than designed. Everything about how much this result can be leaned on flows from that number. See the-statin-trials-non-inferiority-margin-was-wider-than-its-own-event-rate-justified.

⚠ The secondaries all ran the other way and none of them settle anything. Myocardial infarction 2.1% (stop) vs 1.4% (continue); non-fatal stroke 2.7% vs 1.9%; major cardiovascular events 5.0% vs 4.4%; the composite of death-or-major-event 12.0% vs 11.4%. Every count is numerically worse for stopping while mortality is numerically better for it, and every interval contains both zero and a real harm. Three years is a short horizon for atherosclerotic events, and the authors write that whether the benefits of plaque stabilisation are lost after withdrawal "remains unclear."

Scope, stated plainly. Everyone in this trial had no cardiovascular disease and was already established on a statin. Nothing here speaks to secondary prevention, to people under 75, or to whether a statin should be started. Three larger trials are running: STREAM (n=1,870, aged ≥70, same design), and STAREE and PREVENTABLE, which test statins against placebo for disability-free survival in healthy over-70s.
