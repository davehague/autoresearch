Doing both wins twice — and the version that wins on the leaderboard is the one that vanishes under a quality filter

A Bayesian network of 159 randomised trials and 10,821 adults aged 45 and over ranked seven ways of exercising for blood pressure. Circuit-based training took first place on systolic pressure at −13.52 mmHg (95% credible interval −18.59 to −8.44) — nearly double the runner-up. It does not survive the paper's own sensitivity analyses. Exclude the trials at high risk of bias, or the trials with fewer than 15 people per arm, and combined training overtakes it in both cases.

What the two things are. Combined training means aerobic work and resistance work done in the same programme — a run and a lifting session, sequentially. Circuit-based training means the same two ingredients interleaved into stations with short rests, so heart rate never fully recovers. The paper's own name for the family both belong to is integrated aerobic-resistance training, and its conclusion is about the family, not the winner: the authors write that circuit training's "apparent superiority over CT should be interpreted cautiously, while the overall potential of IART appears more consistent."

⚑ Why the top rank should be discounted, in three facts the paper publishes about itself. Circuit training's estimate comes from 10 arms and 267 participants — the thinnest node in a network of 10,821 people. It carries the network's only statistically significant local inconsistency (circuit versus moderate continuous training, node-splitting p < 0.01), traced to a single trial reporting −27.30/−13.20 mmHg, roughly triple what the rest of this literature produces. And no comparison in the entire network reached high confidence under CINeMA — 4 of 21 were moderate, 16 low, 1 very low.

⚑ What survives is the convergence, and it is now five literatures deep. Combined aerobic-plus-resistance training tops blood pressure here, tops it in an entirely separate 30-trial network of hypertensive adults at −9.52 mmHg with high certainty (combined-training-is-the-best-evidenced-exercise-lever-for-systolic-pressure), and already topped endothelial function (combined-aerobic-resistance-tops-endothelial-dose-response), glycaemic control (combined-aerobic-resistance-tops-glycemic-control-in-older-adults) and all-cause mortality (aerobic-plus-resistance-lowest-mortality). Two of those are blood-pressure networks built by different teams, in different databases, on different populations, with different node taxonomies — and once this one's quality filter runs, they name the same winner.

⚠ What it does not establish. 73 of 152 contributing trials (48%) were at high risk of bias, mostly for missing outcome data. Blood pressure is unblindable and measurement-sensitive, and 63 of 159 studies did not report the posture in which it was measured. Interventions ran a median of 12 weeks, so persistence is unmeasured. The search was English-only. Capped suggestive.
