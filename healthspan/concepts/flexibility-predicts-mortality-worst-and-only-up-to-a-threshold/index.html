Flexibility was the weakest of seven fitness predictors of death, and past a functional range it bought nothing more

Of the seven standardised tests Wu 2026 ran on the same 13,423 older adults, the two flexibility tests came last. Chair sit-and-reach gave an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.79 (95% CI 0.67–0.93) comparing the most to the least flexible fifth — against 0.41 for the 8-foot up-and-go and 0.58 for the cardiorespiratory step test.

⚑ And flexibility did not merely rank low; it behaved differently in shape. Restricted cubic spline models found the back scratch test genuinely nonlinear in both men and women, with a plateau at roughly −10 cm. The authors describe it directly: "extreme stiffness was associated with higher mortality risk, but gains beyond a moderate functional range yielded no further survival benefit." Their own recommendation follows: "clinical interventions for longevity may prioritize balance, strength, and cardiorespiratory fitness once a basic flexibility threshold is met."

⚑ And it closes a gap this vault had already written down. flexibility-weak-predictor-of-health-outcomes-adults pooled 45 prospective cohorts, n = 48,020, and found flexibility a weak-to-null predictor of future pain, disability and frailty — while recording that mortality and falls had too few cohorts to pool and could only be described narratively. That is precisely the hole Wu 2026 fills: a single cohort of 13,423 with 1,631 deaths, flexibility measured objectively by two tests, ranked against six other fitness assessments in the same people. The answer is consistent with the rest of that meta-analysis — flexibility is the weakest thing in the battery — and it is now on the mortality endpoint rather than adjacent to it.

⚑ It is also the second independent hit on flexibility in three days, from a completely different design. On 2026-08-15 a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 423 randomised trials found stretching the only one of nineteen exercise modalities that failed to improve balance (stretching-was-the-only-exercise-modality-that-did-not-improve-balance). That was randomised evidence on a surrogate. This is observational evidence on a hard endpoint. Three sources, three methods, one direction.

⚠ Neither result touches what stretching actually claims, and neither should be read as one. That map's supported claim is range of motion: chronic-static-stretching-improves-lower-limb-flexibility records Hedges' g 0.851 across 79 RCTs. Stretching improves flexibility. What these two sources jointly establish is narrower and more useful: flexibility is a means to function, not a longevity endpoint, and there is a point past which more of it stops paying. A high-tier null on an outcome a map never claimed bounds the map rather than rebutting it, and stretching's Status stays where it is on that reasoning.

⚠ The threshold is not a number to aim at. −10 cm on a back scratch test, in adults averaging 72.9 years, is a population-level inflection in an observational curve. It is not a target, it is not transferable to a younger person, and this vault sets no stretching prescription (shared/safety.md).

⚠ And the direction of causation is unresolved here as it is everywhere else in this cohort. Extreme stiffness in an older adult can be a consequence of the disease that later kills them — arthritis, deconditioning, undiagnosed illness — rather than a cause of anything. The weak association is exactly what you would expect either way.

What survives for a reader deciding what to do: stretch enough to have working range for the activity you want; do not treat additional range as a survival investment, and do not let it substitute for the domains that ranked above it.
