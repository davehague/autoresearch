Twenty genetic studies looked for the heart benefit of light drinking; the four best-conducted ones did not find it

Mendelian randomisation uses inherited genetic variants as a natural experiment. Variants in ADH1B and ALDH2 — the genes for the enzymes that break down alcohol and acetaldehyde — strongly determine how much a person drinks, and they are assigned at conception, long before income, exercise habits or illness can act. Comparing outcomes across genotypes therefore approximates a randomised trial of drinking that nobody could ethically run, and it is largely immune to the sick-quitter and healthy-user biases that make the cohort J-curve untrustworthy.

The tally across 20 such studies: seven null, one significantly harmful, two mixed, and one protective for myocardial infarction, among those using volume or intake frequency. Of the three using AUDIT-C (a validated drinking-problem screener) as the exposure, two found harm and one found nothing. Multivariable analyses adjusting for smoking, systolic blood pressure or cannabis use disorder either left nulls unchanged or knocked a previously positive association out of significance.

⚑ The sentence that matters most is about study quality, not about alcohol. "Four studies were judged at low risk of bias, one at moderate risk and 15 at high risk... Studies at low to moderate risk of bias reported positive [harmful] or null associations." Not one of the five better-conducted studies found alcohol protective for the heart. The protective and ambiguous findings sit in the 15 studies at high risk of bias — driven by weak instruments, genetic confounding, pleiotropy or selection bias.

The review's own summary: "the available MR evidence does not support a protective effect of alcohol consumption on ischaemic heart disease risk at low levels of consumption."

⚠ And the review then argues this is not enough to refute the J-curve, for three reasons that are all technically correct. (1) Seventeen of the 20 modelled only log-linear effects — a study that fits a straight line cannot report a J even if one exists. (2) The non-linear methods that do exist "have been shown to yield spurious, implausible associations," so the three studies that tried are themselves suspect. (3) ⚑ No study separated average drinking volume from heavy episodic drinking — and the cohort J-curve is claimed to hold only in the absence of heavy episodic drinking (the-alcohol-j-curve-requires-the-absence-of-heavy-drinking-occasions). A design that cannot see the condition cannot test the claim.

⚑ This is a pushes-back note whose rebuttal is bounded rather than decisive, and the boundary is worth stating precisely. The genetic evidence removes the two biases most likely to have manufactured the J-curve, and with them removed the protection does not appear. That is a real challenge to the-alcohol-heart-benefit-shrinks-as-each-additional-bias-is-controlled. What it does not do is demonstrate that light drinking is harmful for the heart — most of these studies are estimating an average linear effect across the whole drinking range, where harm at the top will swamp any benefit at the bottom.

What would resolve it, in the authors' words: genetic markers that can distinguish heavy drinking occasions from average volume, and then a test for a protective effect in the latter. Nobody has those instruments.
