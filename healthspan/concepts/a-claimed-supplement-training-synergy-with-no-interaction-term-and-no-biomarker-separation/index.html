A synergy claim tested with the wrong model, on a supplement whose own biomarker did not separate the arms

Two separate failures sit between this trial's design and its conclusion, and either one is enough.

Failure one: the interaction was never tested. The paper's central claim is a synergy — that folic acid and resistance training combine to produce more than either alone. A 2 × 2 factorial design (training yes/no × folate yes/no) is exactly the right structure to test that, and this trial has it. ⚑ Testing an interaction requires fitting an interaction term — a two-way ANOVA or equivalent that asks whether the effect of folate differs depending on whether the participant trained. The analysis run was a one-way ANOVA across four groups on change scores, with Bonferroni post hoc comparisons. That test asks only whether four means differ. It cannot distinguish "additive" from "synergistic," and it cannot support the word "interaction" at all. A perfectly good factorial design was analysed as if it were four unrelated conditions.

Failure two: the supplement's own biomarker did not separate the arms.

ArmPlasma folate change over 8 weeks
Folic acid alone+1.91 ± 2.36 μmol/L (P = 0.031)
Control−0.514 ± 3.337 (P = 0.638)
Resistance training alone−2.209 ± 2.757 (P = 0.032)
Training + folic acid⚠ −1.977 ± 4.030 (P = 0.155)

Between groups: no significant difference at all. ⚑ The arm claimed to benefit from folic acid ended the trial with less circulating folate than it started with, and no more than any other arm. The authors' explanation — "potentially due to increased folate utilization during resistance training" — is a hypothesis offered after the fact, unmeasured and, as posed, unfalsifiable. A supplement trial whose supplement biomarker does not distinguish supplemented from unsupplemented participants has not demonstrated differential exposure, which is the precondition for attributing any downstream difference to the supplement.

⚠ Two further reasons the biomarker cannot be leaned on either way. Folate was measured with a research ELISA kit rather than the microbiological or chemiluminescent assays used clinically, and the reported changes are given in μmol/L — two orders of magnitude above the physiological range for serum folate in those units, an inconsistency the paper does not address. And dietary intake was never recorded or quantified, in a trial about a nutrient whose habitual dietary intake varies severalfold between individuals and is fortified into staple foods in many countries.

⚠ The reporting compounds it. The discussion states the combined arm "exhibited significant decreases in Hcy, TC, TG, and LDL, alongside increased HDL." The results section reports total cholesterol P = 0.128, triglycerides P = 0.855 and LDL-C P = 0.101 — explicitly "no significant between-group differences." Only homocysteine and HDL-C separated.

⚑ What survives, stated fairly. Homocysteine fell more in the combined arm than in control or training alone, and folate lowering homocysteine is settled biochemistry. HDL-C rose more in the combined arm than in control or folate alone, and exercise raising HDL is well established. Neither result requires the muscle claim, neither supports it, and both come from ten participants per arm across ten outcomes with no registration and no correction across outcome families. ⚠ Homocysteine is also a surrogate — the large randomised homocysteine-lowering trials of the 2000s largely failed to deliver the cardiovascular benefit the association predicted, which is the same predictor-versus-responder-versus-surrogate problem biological-age-clocks exists to price (clock-responsiveness-does-not-establish-surrogacy-and-there-is-no-defined-minimal-important-difference).
