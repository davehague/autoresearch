Walking does improve perceived sleep — the direction is real, the magnitude is not bankable

Twenty-one RCTs, 1,707 participants, 66 effect sizes: Hedges' g = −0.76 (−0.99 to −0.55), a medium-to-large improvement in self-reported sleep quality, with a 95% prediction interval entirely below zero (−1.37 to −0.16).

This settles a question the vault deliberately left open one day earlier — in the direction the vault refused to foreclose. walking-subjective-sleep-quality-unresolved recorded Liu 2026's PSQI null (MD −1.41, −3.18 to 0.36) and declined to read it as a negative finding, on the explicit grounds that "the interval contains a clinically meaningful improvement and zero." Kong's estimate lands inside that interval, on the improvement side. The refusal to bank the null was correct, and it was correct within twenty-four hours. That is the clearest evidence to date that the vault's underpowered-null lens does real work rather than rationalising a preference — it was applied against a result the vault wanted, and it held.

And now the same discipline has to run in the opposite direction, which is harder, because this time the flattering result is the positive one.

The magnitude is not trustworthy, and the paper says so. GRADE certainty: VERY LOW, downgraded on three separate domains by the authors' own table —

  • Risk of bias (−1): "No study rated as low risk of bias; 4 rated as high risk." Not one of the 21.
  • Inconsistency (−1): I² = 73.7%.
  • Publication bias (−1): Egger's z = −6.53, p < 0.001; Begg's τ = −0.458, p < 0.001.

And the design makes the effect size structurally uninterpretable. Every trial was open-label with a self-reported outcome, against an inactive control. You cannot blind a person to whether they have been going for walks, and then you ask them to rate how well they slept. The authors concede the point against their own headline: "subjective reporting bias and expectancy effects cannot be excluded" and "the effect sizes reported here may overestimate the true treatment effect."

The 99.97% is not a fact about sleep — see bayesian-certainty-cannot-repair-biased-inputs.

And the population is not a healthy sleeper's. Only 3 of 21 trials enrolled healthy adults; 8 enrolled people with diagnosed sleep disorders. The single strongest moderator in the model is baseline PSQI (β = −0.302) — the worse the sleep, the bigger the effect (walking-sleep-benefit-scales-with-baseline-sleep-problem). This is a literature about fixing broken sleep being read as though it were about protecting good sleep.

What survives, stated at the strength the evidence supports. Walking probably improves how well people feel they sleep, most in people who sleep badly, by an amount nobody can currently quantify. Graded suggestive — not strong — because the reliability appraisal caps it there. No Status change on daily-steps (its emerging rests on the observational mortality signal, HR 0.53), no new Foundation, no number in the regimen, and walking is still counted once. The walk was already recommended. It may be paying twice. Nobody has run the blinded, objectively-measured trial that would prove it.

The source's own reported prescription window, quoted as published and not invented (shared/safety.md): 3–5 sessions/week, 30–60 min, moderate intensity (3.5–5.0 METs), 12–24 weeks — which is a note about how to take a walk David already takes, not a new task.
