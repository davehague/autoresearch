Metformin added nothing to a supervised diet-and-exercise programme — and the trial was built so that the flat line is a result, not a shrug

DEMFOS randomised 114 older adults with obesity three ways for six months: intensive lifestyle intervention plus metformin, intensive lifestyle intervention plus placebo, or monthly diet education plus metformin. The primary outcome was the modified physical performance test — nine timed and scored everyday tasks (walking 15 metres, stairs, standing from a chair, picking a coin off the floor, putting on a coat, a balance test), each scored 0–4 for a total out of 36.

The drug comparison came back at 0.1 points (95% CI −1.2 to 1.4), p = 0.92. The lifestyle comparison, run on the same instrument in the same trial, came back at 2.5 points (1.2 to 3.8), p = 0.0002.

Why this null is informative rather than empty, which is the whole point of it. A study that finds nothing usually cannot distinguish "there is no effect" from "we could not see one." This one can, for three specific reasons.

First, the size of the effect worth finding was declared in advance. The investigators powered the trial at 80% to detect a 1.8-point between-group difference (assumed SD 2.2), at a two-sided threshold of 0.025 after Bonferroni correction for their two prespecified comparisons. The observed interval, −1.2 to 1.4, sits entirely inside ±1.8. Whatever metformin does here, the data rule out its being as large as the amount the trial's designers called meaningful.

Second, the instrument demonstrably works. The same test, scored by the same masked raters, detected a large and highly significant difference between the training arm and the education arm. A null between two arms carries far more weight when a positive control sits in the same experiment (intensive-lifestyle-intervention-improves-physical-function-in-older-adults-with-obesity).

Third, the drug comparison was blinded even though the trial was not. Nobody can be masked to attending supervised exercise, so the lifestyle assignment was open — but participants, staff and outcome assessors were all masked to metformin versus placebo, and participants were told not to disclose their assignment at assessments. Median pill adherence was 100% in both drug arms, so this is not a null caused by nobody taking the tablets.

What it confirms. metformin already held a randomised null on physical function from a different group in a different population — MET-PREVENT, in sarcopenic pre-frail and frail elders around age 80, which found no change in 4-metre walk speed and more hospital admissions on the drug (metformin-null-on-physical-performance-frail-elders, metformin-adverse-events-hospitalizations-frail-elders). DEMFOS is a second independent randomised null on function, in a younger, non-diabetic, obese, trainable population, with a far larger and better-powered design. Two independent randomised nulls pointing the same way is why this note is graded suggestive rather than single-study — the direction has replicated even though the specific outcome measures differ.

Bounded in two ways that matter. The trial has three of the four cells it would need to price metformin on its own: there is no education-plus-placebo arm, so it can say metformin adds nothing on top of intensive lifestyle but cannot say what metformin does in someone who is not training. And the population is 89% men at a single Veterans Affairs centre, aged 65–85, with diabetes excluded by design — so this is not evidence about people taking metformin for glycaemic control, which is nearly everyone who takes it.

What it does not settle. Metformin's healthspan claim is about decades, and the outcome here is six months of physical function. TAME — the large randomised trial with ageing itself as the endpoint — remains the result that could move this map in either direction, and it has not reported.
