Almost every effect in this network could be nothing the next time somebody looks

A credible interval describes the average effect across the trials pooled. A prediction interval describes what a new trial, run in a new setting, would plausibly find. The second is the one that matters for anyone deciding whether to do the thing, and it is almost always wider.

Hu 2026 reports both, which most network meta-analyses do not. The result is that the paper documents its own fragility.

One row out of six excludes zero. The executive-function interval is the worst: -0.48 to 1.29 contains a moderate harm as comfortably as it contains a large benefit.

⚑ And the formal certainty machinery agrees. CINeMA - the network-meta-analysis analogue of GRADE - rated very low certainty for 41 of the 45 comparisons in the paper. One reached moderate. The downgrades were for within-study bias (chiefly the 74.5% of trials with unclear allocation concealment), heterogeneity (I-squared 63-73% in the pairwise analyses), incoherence, and - in the executive-function network specifically - suspected reporting bias flagged on every single comparison, after Egger's test came back significant at P = .005.

This is not a criticism the authors would dispute; it is their own framing. They list very low certainty first among their limitations, note that "evidence is sparse for several modalities," and record that the memory advantage "rests on a single direct comparison."

⚠ The practical consequence for this vault: no number from this source should be quoted without the prediction interval attached. An SMD of 0.51 for exergames reads like a settled finding; -0.03 to 1.07 reads like what it is.
