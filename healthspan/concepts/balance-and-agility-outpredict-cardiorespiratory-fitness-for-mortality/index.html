When several kinds of fitness are measured in the same people, the balance tests predict death best — better than the aerobic test

Taiwan's national fitness survey administered seven standardised performance tests to 13,423 adults aged 65 and over, then linked them to the national death registry for a median of seven years. Because every test was run on every participant, the study can do something almost no fitness-and-mortality paper can: rank the domains against each other in one population.

The ranking, best-fifth versus worst-fifth, after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, socioeconomic and geographic factors, medical history — and self-reported physical activity:

TestDomainAHR (95% CI)
Composite of all sevenmultidomain0.39 (0.32–0.48)
8-foot up-and-gobalance & agility0.41 (0.33–0.51)
One-leg stancebalance & agility0.50 (0.42–0.59)
30-second chair standlower-body strength0.55 (0.46–0.65)
2-minute step testcardiorespiratory fitness0.58 (0.49–0.68)
30-second arm curlupper-body strength0.63 (0.53–0.75)
Chair sit-and-reachflexibility0.79 (0.67–0.93)

What the numbers mean. An adjusted hazard ratio of 0.41 says that over the follow-up, the fittest fifth on that test accumulated deaths at 41% of the rate of the least-fit fifth. The interval 0.33–0.51 is the range of true values compatible with the data — it sits far from 1.0, so this is not a borderline result statistically.

⚑ The adjustment that makes this more than "exercisers live longer." Self-reported physical activity is in the covariate set. The fitness gradient survives it. What a person can do on the day predicted survival on top of what they said they did — which is the case for measuring capacity rather than asking about behaviour.

⚑ The composite beat every individual test (0.39 vs a best single test of 0.41). No one assessment substitutes for the set.

⚠ This is a prediction result, not a treatment result, and the distinction is the whole ballgame. The 8-foot up-and-go asks a person to stand, walk eight feet, turn, walk back and sit — it needs heart, lungs, legs, joints, vision, cognition and vestibular function working together. A test that aggregates many systems will out-predict a test that probes one, whether or not any of those systems is the cause. Subclinical disease degrades balance and gait before it is diagnosed and then kills; the exclusions (dementia, stroke, amputation, catastrophic illness) blunt that pathway without closing it, and no lag analysis dropping early follow-up appears in the retrieved text. Nothing here shows that practising the test changes the outcome.

⚑ Where it sits against the randomised evidence. The one randomised result in this vault on the same causal claim points the same way: balance-and-functional-exercise-cuts-actual-falls-and-fall-related-injuries found a village-doctor-delivered balance and functional exercise programme cutting real falls and fall-related injuries in a cluster-randomised trial. That is the trial this cohort's mechanism story needs, and it exists — though as a bundle that nothing isolates. The cohort and the trial are consistent; neither on its own establishes that balance training causes lower mortality.

⚑ And it re-orders this vault's own priors. The vault's mortality shelf was aerobic and volume-based — cardiorespiratory-fitness-all-cause-mortality, aerobic-volume-mortality-dose-response-nonlinear, daily-steps-mortality-dose-response, moderate-activity-75min-week-mortality-minimum. It held no mortality evidence at all on standing up and turning around. In the one study that measured both in the same people, the aerobic test came fourth.

Sex differences. In men, balance and agility led, then cardiorespiratory fitness. In women, lower-body strength was comparable to balance and agility, then cardiorespiratory fitness.
