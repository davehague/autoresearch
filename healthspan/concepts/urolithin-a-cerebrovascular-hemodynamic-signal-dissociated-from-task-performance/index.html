Urolithin A raised a brain-blood-flow signal during a working-memory task — and the task score did not move

In the same independently-funded RCT that found no peripheral vascular benefit (urolithin-a-null-on-peripheral-endothelial-function-in-obesity-rct), task-evoked cerebrovascular hemodynamic response (measured by fNIRS during an n-back working-memory task) was significantly greater in the urolithin A group than placebo, surviving a brain-wide false-discovery-rate correction (q<0.05), localized to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Working-memory task accuracy and reaction time (0-back and 2-back) did not differ between groups at any comparison (all P>=.12).

Read this as exploratory, not as a demonstrated cognitive benefit. It was not the trial's pre-specified primary outcome, carries no reported between-group confidence interval (only per-arm descriptive values), and — most importantly — did not translate into the behavioral measure it was recorded alongside. The authors suggest a ceiling effect on task accuracy (most participants scored near-perfect at baseline) and propose the signal may reflect a neuroinflammatory mechanism distinct from the peripheral-vascular pathway that was null. Four weeks and 1,000 mg/day may also simply be too little/short to move brain function even if a vascular signal is real.

This is the same shape as urolithin-a-immune-cell-rejuvenation-rct — a coherent surrogate signal with no accompanying functional change — now from an independent (non-Amazentis) trial rather than a manufacturer-funded one.
