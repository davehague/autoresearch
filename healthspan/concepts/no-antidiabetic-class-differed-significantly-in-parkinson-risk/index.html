Twenty-one comparisons, twenty-one intervals crossing the null

The paper's own first result sentence: "No antidiabetic class demonstrated a statistically significant difference in PD risk." The league table bears it out exhaustively — every one of the 21 pairwise risk ratios has a 95% credible interval containing 1, and several span two orders of magnitude. SGLT2i versus α-glucosidase inhibitors is 4.94 (0.44 to 68.64): an interval compatible with the drug quintupling Parkinson's risk and with it cutting risk by more than half.

⚑ This is the finding. Not a ranking, not a signal, not a hint — a network of 712,287 patients that cannot distinguish any drug class from any other on this outcome. The honest one-line summary is "we cannot tell," and this vault records it as such.

⚠ Why it is nonetheless capped single-study rather than treated as a strong null. The source is appraised unreliable (fatal flags #1 and #5), and per shared/evidence-model.md an unreliable result cannot move a map in either direction. The nulls here are not well-powered nulls; they are uninformative intervals. A network this sparse — nine studies, few closed loops, follow-ups from 427 days — fails to detect an effect for reasons that have nothing to do with whether one exists (parkinson-follow-up-here-is-shorter-than-the-prodrome-it-claims-to-prevent).

⚑ What follows is a rule about reading, not about drugs. When a network meta-analysis leads with a ranking, find the league table first. If every interval crosses the null, the ranking is the only finding, and a ranking derived from a null posterior is a description of point estimates, not of evidence (the-neuroprotection-claim-rests-on-rank-probabilities-over-an-entirely-null-network).
