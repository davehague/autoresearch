The same MET-minutes point in opposite directions depending on whether you chose to spend them

Pooling 74 studies and 4.2 million adults, being in the most-active group carried 20% lower dementia risk — RR 0.80 (95% CI 0.75–0.86). That is the number the field has been quoting for a decade. This paper then splits the exposure by where the activity happened, and the association changes sign.

DomainStudiesRR (95% CI)Direction
Leisure-time200.76 (0.65–0.88)lower risk
Household10.85 (0.74–0.98)lower risk
Occupational51.20 (1.01–1.42)higher risk
Commuting21.10 (1.03–1.18)higher risk

Domain significantly moderated the association (p=0.0004). The dose-response curves run opposite ways too: leisure-time activity showed a non-linear inverse association that plateaus (p<0.0001 for non-linearity), occupational a non-linear positive one climbing from 200 to roughly 3,000 MET-minutes per week (p<0.0001).

⚑ The obvious objection was tested and failed to explain it. If manual workers simply accumulate more total movement, adjusting for total activity volume should collapse the occupational result. It did not — the increased risk remained significant after that adjustment, and after adjusting for baseline age and education. The occupational estimates were also restricted to working participants, which removes the healthy-worker bias (retirees dumped into the low-activity reference group) that the authors identify as the reason earlier studies on this question disagreed. The moderator survived leave-one-out and survived dropping every low-quality study.

⚠ What this does not establish is that occupational movement is the harmful thing. The authors' own reading is mostly not about exercise: physically demanding jobs carry psychosocial stress, low autonomy, shift work, air and noise pollution — which has its own dementia association (air-pollution-cognitive-harm-is-consistent-and-unrandomised) — less opportunity for leisure activity, and repetitive low-complexity tasks that build little cognitive reserve. Their words: the association "might partly reflect an accumulation of social, economic, and environmental adversities rather than be fully attributable to occupational physical activity per se." The defensible claim is the negative one: the protective estimate measured in leisure cohorts does not transfer to occupational ones, and pooling them produces a number that describes neither.

⚑ Why this matters to a vault that treats activity as a scalar. aerobic-volume-mortality-dose-response-nonlinear pools 94 cohorts and over 30 million adults, and its own population field records the exposure as "self-reported leisure-time activity." daily-steps-mortality-dose-response counts steps without asking whose. If the sign is domain-dependent for dementia, then those results are claims about discretionary movement specifically, and generalising them to "movement" overreaches the evidence they were built on. This note records the constraint; it does not downgrade those findings, which measured what they measured.

⚠ Grading, honestly: the cells with the surprising signs are the cells with almost no studies. Leisure rests on 20 studies; occupational on five, commuting on two, household on one. The authors' own GRADE certainty is very low to low across every domain. Heterogeneity is considerable (I²=97.4% overall, 96.6% within leisure alone), exposure is self-reported nearly everywhere, and — the authors note this pointedly — accelerometry cannot currently distinguish domains at all, so the variable that defines the paper is one no objective instrument can yet measure. Capped suggestive by the source's mixed appraisal.

Rebutted by: the-dementia-protection-signal-is-largest-where-reverse-causation-is-most-likely — the magnitude of the protective leisure-time estimate is inflated by reverse causation the included cohorts almost never tested for; the domain contrast recorded here is the one claim that failure mode cannot manufacture.
