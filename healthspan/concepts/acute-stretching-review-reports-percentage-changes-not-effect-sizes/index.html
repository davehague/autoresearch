It is a systematic review, not a meta-analysis — and the difference decides what the numbers mean

Belli 2026 synthesises its 23 studies by thematic analysis plus percentage-weighted mean change — (post − pre)/pre × 100, weighted by sample size, with confidence intervals attached. It reports no standardised effect sizes, no I², no τ², no funnel plot, no Egger's test and no GRADE assessment. The review says so; the distinction is easy to miss because the output looks meta-analytic.

Three things a percentage change cannot do, each of which bears on a headline in this review.

It cannot make different outcomes commensurable. A 2% improvement in a sit-and-reach reach distance and a 2% change in countermovement jump height are averaged here as if they were the same quantity. They are not: the two measures have different scales, different measurement error, and different natural variability. A standardised mean difference — which re-expresses every trial's effect in units of how spread out its own scores were — exists precisely to solve this, and is what every other effect estimate in stretching is denominated in (chronic-static-stretching-improves-lower-limb-flexibility reports Hedges' g = 0.851). The numbers in this source cannot be compared with the numbers in the rest of the map.

It cannot weight by precision. Weighting by sample size treats a noisy 20-person trial and a tight 20-person trial identically. Meta-analytic weighting uses the inverse of each estimate's variance, so imprecise studies contribute less. Here they do not.

It cannot detect or model heterogeneity or publication bias. Without I², nothing states whether the pooled studies agree. Without a funnel plot or Egger's test, nothing states whether the null results were published. In a corpus that is two-thirds low quality (acute-stretching-evidence-base-is-two-thirds-low-quality) and built on small samples, both are exactly the questions worth asking.

The practical consequence, stated plainly. Every directional claim in this source — the 60-second duration hinge, the modality ordering, the performance penalty — should be read as descriptive summary of a literature, not as an estimate of an effect. The confidence intervals attached to those percentages express sampling variation in a weighted average; they do not express uncertainty about a treatment effect, and most of them contain zero anyway.

Why this is recorded rather than simply used to discount the source. ⚑ The presentation gap is the durable lesson. A PROSPERO-registered, PRISMA-guided systematic review with confidence intervals and pre-defined magnitude bands reads, at a glance, like a meta-analysis — and its abstract's language ("moderate improvements," "small negative performance effects") is meta-analytic language. This vault's rule: check what was pooled before believing what was pooled, and record the answer where a later reader will trip over it.
