At ordinary doses, omega-3 did not increase bleeding across 120,643 randomised patients

Pooling 11 randomised clinical trials and 120,643 patients, omega-3 supplementation produced no detectable increase in bleeding: rate ratio 1.09 (95% CI 0.91–1.31), p = 0.34 — bleeding in 2.6% of those taking omega-3 against 2.4% of controls, an absolute difference of 0.07% (−1.4% to 1.6%).

The serious bleeding types were individually flat too. Haemorrhagic stroke 0.41% vs 0.32%, RR 1.29 (0.92–1.81); intracranial bleeding 0.28% vs 0.31%, RR 0.90 (0.64–1.26); gastrointestinal bleeding 3.1% vs 2.9%, RR 1.07 (0.95–1.21). The haemorrhagic-stroke point estimate leans toward harm and its interval does not exclude it, but nothing here reaches significance.

This pushes back on a claim the vault was carrying without a source. omega-3 logged an anticoagulant/antiplatelet bleeding concern on 2026-07-06 and explicitly marked it as un-sourced pharmacology awaiting a proper review, with instructions not to fabricate a graded concept. The review says the generic version of that worry — that fish oil thins your blood — is not supported at the doses these trials used.

Two limits keep it at suggestive rather than becoming a clean bill of health.

Heterogeneity, with a named cause. I² = 65% means the eleven trials disagreed substantially, and the paper explains why: bleeding was not defined the same way across them. A pooled null over inconsistently defined outcomes is a weaker object than a pooled null over one adjudicated definition. Compare aspirin-increases-major-haemorrhage-older-adults, where a single trial's single adjudicated definition produces a much sharper estimate.

It is a null with a live exception inside it. The same review finds high-dose purified EPA does increase bleeding, and that risk scales with grams of EPA (high-dose-purified-epa-increases-bleeding-risk). So "omega-3 does not increase bleeding" is a statement about the doses in the pooled mixture, not about the top of the dose range.

What it explicitly does not settle is whether omega-3 interacts with antiplatelet drugs in an individual person — see omega3-antiplatelet-bleeding-interaction-not-demonstrated-or-excluded, where the authors' own analysis is a between-trial regression too weak to answer the question in either direction.

Funding is disclosed and unusual: an educational grant from Amarin, which manufactures the one product the review finds does raise bleeding risk (sponsor-adverse-findings-and-sponsor-friendly-framing-can-coexist).

No dose is recommended (shared/safety.md).
