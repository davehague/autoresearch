The best-designed observational study in this vault reports the number that says how easily it could be wrong

An E-value is an honesty statistic. It answers a single question: how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be — on both the exposure and the outcome, after adjusting for everything that was measured — to fully explain this association away? A large E-value means the finding is robust: you would need a confounder as strong as smoking-and-lung-cancer, and there isn't one hiding. A small E-value means an ordinary, unremarkable difference between the groups would be enough.

⚑ Here it is 1.42.

That is a low bar. It means a factor 1.42 times more common among people vaccinated in 2017 than 2018, and 1.42-fold associated with heart disease, erases the whole result. For scale: modest differences in smoking prevalence, income, or neighbourhood between two groups of people who happened to present for vaccination a year apart could each plausibly reach that.

⚠ And the authors name precisely which such variables are missing. Verbatim: "Socioeconomic status (education, income, occupation, neighborhood deprivation) is not captured in EHR data, and lifestyle factors (smoking, diet, alcohol use) are only indirectly and imperfectly captured through related clinical variables." Every one of those is associated with cardiovascular risk at well above 1.42. The only question is whether they differ between the cohorts — and there is a named reason to think they might, because the transition followed an updated ACIP recommendation, and guideline changes move who shows up.

This does not make the finding worthless, and the reason is worth stating precisely. The E-value describes what a confounder of that strength could do. The study separately shows that several whole classes of confounder are not operating: the cohorts had no difference in the same endpoint in the year before vaccination (RMTL 0.99, 0.92–1.06), so they were not already diverging; a pre-specified negative control outcome was null, so the 2018 group was not simply being diagnosed with more of everything; a tetanus-vaccine control was null (1.01, 0.99–1.03), so cardiovascular diagnosis rates did not change between the two years; and there was no association with non-cardiovascular death, so the 2018 cohort was not simply healthier overall. A confounder that survives all four of those and still reaches 1.42 in both directions is harder to name than the raw E-value suggests — though the authors are right that it cannot be excluded.

⚑ The generalisable rule this note exists to record. This vault reads observational claims constantly, and the Universal appraisal lens already asks whether an effect is large enough to be out of confounding range. The E-value turns that judgement into a number, and it should be looked for and quoted every time a paper reports one. Its absence is also informative: a paper making a causal-sounding observational claim that does not report an E-value has declined to say how fragile it is.

⚠ The comparison worth holding in mind: this vault's own worked example of a large effect surviving confounding is cardiorespiratory-fitness-all-cause-mortality — a hazard ratio above 5 with no ceiling, where the appraisal reasoned that no plausible confounder is that strong. A 9% reduction in burden is a different animal entirely, and it is filed here as pushing back on the temptation to treat design quality as a substitute for effect size. The design is excellent. The margin is thin. Both are true.

In one sentence: the study reports exactly how easily it could be wrong, the answer is "fairly easily," and the fact that it says so is the reason to trust the rest of it.
