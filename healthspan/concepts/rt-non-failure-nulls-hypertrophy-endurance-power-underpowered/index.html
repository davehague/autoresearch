"No significant difference" on four outcomes is not proof non-failure training is equivalent — three of the four can't even rule out the effect the paper found elsewhere

The meta-analysis behind rt-non-failure-beats-failure-dynamic-strength also tested isometric strength, muscular hypertrophy, muscular endurance, and muscular power for non-failure vs failure resistance training, and found no statistically significant difference on any of the four. The paper's abstract concludes non-failure training "appears to be as effective as training to failure for most neuromuscular adaptations."

The vault does not accept that reading. Look at what the intervals actually contain. Isometric strength: SMD −0.12 (95% CI −0.48 to 0.24) — the upper bound is exactly the pooled effect size (0.24) the same paper calls significant for dynamic strength. Muscular endurance: SMD −0.02 (−0.45 to 0.41) — the widest interval of all five outcomes in the paper, built on only 4 trials and 128 participants, the thinnest base in the study. Muscular power: SMD 0.02 (−0.28 to 0.31) — again an upper bound past the strength finding. None of these three "null" results can statistically distinguish itself from an effect as large as the one the paper is built to advertise. Muscular hypertrophy is the tightest of the four — SMD −0.15 (−0.39 to 0.09), 10 trials, 256 participants — and its upper bound does exclude a benefit that large, but its lower bound (−0.39) does not exclude a real, nontrivial advantage for training to failure on this one outcome.

This is fatal red flag #3 (shared/study-appraisal.md): an underpowered null sold as "no effect." The evidence bases here (4–10 trials, 112–256 participants) are a quarter to just over half the size of the 19-trial, 461-participant pool behind the paper's one significant finding — these outcomes were never given the statistical power to detect an effect the size of the one this study did find, and the abstract generalizes the resulting wide intervals into "as effective as" rather than "inconclusive."

And the paper's own heterogeneity statistics close off the innocent explanation. A wide confidence interval has two possible causes: the studies disagree with each other (heterogeneity), or there simply aren't enough people (low power). The full text reports I² = 0% for isometric strength (p=0.660), hypertrophy (p=0.710), and power (p=0.860) — and I² = 30.96% for endurance. At I² = 0% the trials agree almost perfectly with one another. There is no disagreement inflating these intervals. The only thing making them wide is sample size. That is not an interpretation the vault is imposing on the paper; it is arithmetic the paper itself supplies, and it converts "these nulls look underpowered" into "these nulls are demonstrably underpowered."

The authors concede it, and then their abstract ignores it. From the paper's own Limitations: "most of the included studies had small sample sizes, which may have limited their statistical power and robustness of the estimated effect sizes." That sentence and the abstract's "as effective as training to failure for most neuromuscular adaptations" cannot both be right. The vault records the Limitations section and discards the abstract's generalization.

Why this matters for the map — it has happened here before. resistance-training already carries two concepts built on exactly this pathology from a different source: rt-intensity-does-not-change-bmd (a bone-density "no difference" whose interval reached a moderate effect) and rt-intensity-safety-comparison-underpowered (a fall-risk "similar safety" whose interval reached an elevenfold increase). Wu 2026 is the third instance on this map of a meta-analysis reading a wide, underpowered confidence interval as reassurance. The pattern is now established enough to name: this map's authors keep finding real effects on their best-powered primary outcome and equivalence claims on everything else, and the "equivalence" does not survive a look at the interval.

Bounded honestly (why single-study/open-question, capped below the source's own mixed ceiling). The source's reliability: mixed verdict would normally cap a concept at suggestive; this one is capped further because the fatal-flag lens fires directly on these four outcomes. An underpowered null is closer to no information than to evidence of absence — this concept does not say non-failure training fails to build hypertrophy, endurance, or power, and it does not say it succeeds. It says the question is open, on thin data, and the paper's own conclusion oversells it.

The practical shape of the claim. Do not read this paper as license to assume non-failure training matches failure training on hypertrophy, endurance, or power specifically — only the dynamic-strength finding (rt-non-failure-beats-failure-dynamic-strength) is well-enough powered to act on.
