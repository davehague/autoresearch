Past sixty seconds you gain more range and give back a little force — probably

Binning 23 acute stretching studies at a 60-second-per-muscle threshold produces a consistent directional pattern: hold longer, gain more range of motion, lose a little immediate performance. Long-duration static stretching returned +6.21% flexibility pre-to-post (95% CI 2.68–9.75) with −1.80% performance (−3.97 to 0.37); short-duration returned +0.79% flexibility vs control with no performance cost.

The mechanism is coherent, which is why the pattern is worth recording at all. A prolonged static hold reduces musculotendinous stiffness. Stiffness is what stores and returns elastic energy in the stretch-shortening cycle that jumping and sprinting depend on. Making a tendon more compliant should buy range and cost snap, and that is what the bins show.

The numbers are much weaker than the pattern, and the shrinkage is the tell. The flagship +6.21% is a pre-post figure — the same participants measured before and after, with no control group absorbing warm-up, repeated measurement and effort. Against a control it falls to +1.89%, and the lower bound of that interval is 0.01% — a hair from nothing. The performance side is worse: −1.80% pre-post becomes −0.02% against control. Every controlled interval in this analysis except long-duration flexibility contains zero.

Held open-question and capped single-study, and the source is appraised unreliable. Three reasons, all structural. The review is not a meta-analysis — it computes percentage-weighted mean changes rather than standardised effect sizes, so a 2% change in a sit-and-reach and a 2% change in jump height are averaged as if they were the same quantity (acute-stretching-review-reports-percentage-changes-not-effect-sizes). Fifteen of its 23 studies are low quality by its own instrument (acute-stretching-evidence-base-is-two-thirds-low-quality). And the directional claims are drawn from intervals that cross zero with no power analysis anywhere — shared/study-appraisal.md fatal flag #3.

⚠ No dose is adopted from this. stretching has refused to state a stretching dose across 79 randomised trials (stretching-dose-remains-unspecified-across-79-trials), and it does not adopt one now from a single-session study in athletes averaging 19.6 years old. Per shared/safety.md, the 60-second figure is recorded as the boundary this review chose to bin at, not as a recommendation.

The population gap this widens rather than closes. The map's binding limitation is that its evidence in middle-aged adults is three trials, and in the knee-extensor compartment six (stretching-age-generalisation-rests-on-three-middle-aged-trials, stretching-quadriceps-evidence-thinnest-and-weakest-of-the-lower-limb). Gymnasts and wrestlers in their late teens, measured minutes after a single session, do not speak to it.
