Zinc supplementation raised LDL cholesterol — small, consistent, and better evidenced than most of what the same review recommends

Across seven randomised trials, zinc supplementation increased LDL cholesterol by 3.46 mg/dL (95% CI 1.48 to 5.43), p = 0.0006, I² = 3%, GRADE moderate certainty.

⚑ The interesting property is not the size, it is the consistency. I² = 3% means the seven trials agree almost completely. Compare that with the rest of the same review: serum insulin at I² = 98%, fasting blood glucose at 95%, HDL at 94%, triglycerides at 96%. In a review where nearly every pooled estimate is built on trials that violently disagree, the LDL increase is one of the few findings the trials concur on — and it is in the direction of harm.

⚠ Is 3.46 mg/dL clinically meaningful? On its own, no. The review's own stated frame puts optimal LDL below 100 mg/dL and high at 160+; a 3.46 mg/dL shift moves nobody between categories. The authors call its clinical significance "uncertain," which is fair. It is recorded here not because it is dangerous but because of what it sits next to.

⚑ What it sits next to is a set of null primary endpoints. In the same paper: fasting plasma glucose null, fasting blood glucose null, HbA1c null at +0.04 percentage points and GRADE very low (zinc-glycemic-endpoints-are-all-null-in-a-review-named-for-them). ⚑ So the lipid fraction most directly tied to cardiovascular risk is the most internally consistent thing that moved, and it moved the wrong way, in a review concluding that zinc supplementation is beneficial.

⚠ The apparent contradiction with total cholesterol is not a contradiction. Total cholesterol fell 5.70 mg/dL at I² = 0%, GRADE high — the review's only high-certainty efficacy finding. Total cholesterol is a composite; LDL is one component of it. A total-cholesterol fall alongside an LDL rise means something else in the composite fell further — and HDL was null, triglycerides were null, VLDL was null. ⚠ The components do not reconcile cleanly with the total, which is itself a reason for caution about the pooling. And total cholesterol, like every other positive finding here, loses significance in the longer trials (12–52 weeks: −4.14, −13.82 to 5.54).

⚠ Mechanistically this is not surprising, and the mechanism has its own safety edge. Zinc and copper compete for absorption; sustained supplemental zinc can induce copper depletion, and copper status influences lipid metabolism. ⚠ The review did not measure copper and this note is not asserting that mechanism — it is recording that a plausible one exists and that zinc at supplemental doses has an established upper limit for exactly this reason. That is a clinician question, and this vault sets no dose (shared/safety.md).

⚠ And as with every result in this review, nothing isolates zinc — trials co-administered magnesium, vitamin D, calcium, curcumin or vitamin A (zinc-trials-cannot-isolate-zinc-and-the-magnesium-result-shows-why). The LDL rise is attributed to "the intervention," and the intervention was not always zinc alone.
