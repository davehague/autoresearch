Sleeping under 7 hours tracks with ~14% higher all-cause mortality (79-cohort meta-analysis)

Pooling 46 cohorts within a 79-cohort meta-analysis, habitual short sleep (<7 h/night) versus a 7–8 h reference was associated with all-cause mortality HR 1.14 (95% CI 1.10–1.18), with moderate heterogeneity (I²=53%). Sex-stratified: men HR 1.16, women HR 1.14.

Why this matters for the vault. It puts a magnitude on the number everyone actually tracks. Fourteen percent is real but modest — and it is worth holding against the vault's stronger sleep claim: sleep-regularity-predicts-mortality found, using objective wrist accelerometry in ~61,000 adults, that the Sleep Regularity Index out-predicted sleep duration for mortality, with the most-regular sleepers showing 20–48% lower all-cause mortality. Same outcome, better instrument, bigger effect, on the consistency axis rather than the quantity axis. Duration is the weaker lever, measured worse.

Bounded honestly (why suggestive). The exposure is self-reported in nearly every cohort — a measurement with known large validation error against actigraphy. The effect (HR 1.14) is small enough to sit inside the range residual confounding can move: short sleepers differ from 7–8 h sleepers on shift work, income, stress, alcohol, and undiagnosed illness. Retrospective cohorts are included alongside prospective ones. Directionally stable across dozens of cohorts, quantitatively soft.

The practical shape of the claim. "At least ~7 hours" is a reasonable floor, and the evidence-consistent way to reach it is not to chase a duration number but to anchor consistent sleep and wake times (sleep-regularity) and to protect the window from late caffeine (caffeine-evening-cutoff-for-sleep) — both of which raise duration as a side effect of raising regularity.

Rebutted by: objective-short-sleep-without-insomnia-carried-no-mortality-excess — in the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort, adults whose sleep was measured at under 6 hours by polysomnography showed no mortality excess unless they also reported insomnia symptoms, which suggests the pooled hazard here may be carried by the insomnia subgroup rather than distributed across everyone sleeping a little under seven hours. Also self-reported-short-sleep-does-not-identify-the-high-risk-insomnia-group — the same analysis run on self-reported duration, the instrument behind this claim, found nothing at all. Neither refutes the pooled association (767 people cannot overturn 79 cohorts, and an underpowered null is not a demonstration of absence); both narrow what it is probably about.
