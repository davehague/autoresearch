Metformin redirected the training adaptation rather than removing it — more fat burned during exercise, uncoupled from fitness

The most statistically robust effect in this trial is not the fitness attenuation it is usually cited for. It is a large, internally consistent shift in which fuel the body burns while exercising.

Maximal fat oxidation — the highest rate at which a person oxidises fat during a graded exercise test, measured by comparing oxygen consumed with carbon dioxide produced — rose by 0.127 g/min (95% CI 0.093 to 0.161) in the metformin arm against 0.040 g/min (0.006 to 0.074) in placebo. The interaction is p = 0.001 with η²p = 0.413, the largest effect size in the paper. This is also the only contrast in the entire trial where the two arms differ directly at the end (difference 0.067 g/min, 0.007 to 0.127, p = 0.031) — every fitness comparison was null between groups (metformin-exercise-attenuation-rests-on-an-interaction-not-a-group-difference).

The pattern across intensities is what makes it coherent rather than a lucky p-value. Fat oxidation rose in the metformin arm at 20%, 30%, 40%, 50% and 60% of maximal aerobic power — every submaximal intensity tested, though not at rest — and in the placebo arm at none of them. Energy expenditure at matched relative intensity likewise rose in the metformin arm only. Doing the same work, the drug arm ended up burning more fat and spending more energy to do it — which is a plausible mechanism for the same session feeling harder (metformin-raises-perceived-effort-at-the-same-exercise-intensity).

The uncoupling is the interesting part. In the placebo arm, gains in fat oxidation tracked gains in fitness (r = 0.612, p = 0.045) — the textbook relationship, where getting fitter and getting better at using fat are two faces of the same mitochondrial adaptation. In the metformin arm that link was absent (r = 0.442, p = 0.173), and the regression line sat visibly above placebo. So the drug arm improved its fat handling out of proportion to, and independently of, its aerobic capacity. The authors call this a "dissociated adaptation profile."

⚠ The vault records this as open-question, not as a benefit, and it has a specific reason rather than general caution. intermittent-fasting already holds fat-oxidation-gain-is-dwarfed-by-compensatory-intake — a trial in which a measured, real increase in fat oxidised during exercise was swamped by how much more the same people subsequently ate, leaving nothing at the level of 24-hour energy balance. This vault has therefore already watched this exact surrogate fail to translate, in a different literature. And this trial is in a weaker position than that one: it recorded no food intake, no body composition, no fat mass and no weight change over its five weeks. There is no route from these numbers to a claim about anyone getting leaner or healthier.

What "burns more fat during exercise" does and does not mean. It means the body is meeting the same energy demand with a different mix of fuels at that moment. It does not mean net fat loss, which is set by total energy balance across the whole day, not by fuel selection inside a one-hour cycling test. That distinction is the entire content of the intermittent-fasting result above.

Graded suggestive and held as a surrogate. Eleven per arm, five weeks, per-protocol analysis, an outcome that is a mechanistic intermediate rather than anything a person experiences. It is recorded because it is real, well measured and internally consistent — and because it changes the shape of the map's interaction flag: the honest reading of this trial is that metformin appears to redirect the training adaptation, not to block it.
