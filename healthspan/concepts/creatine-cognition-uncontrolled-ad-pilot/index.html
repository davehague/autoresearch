Cognition improved in an uncontrolled 20-person creatine pilot — which tells us almost nothing

In the single-arm, open-label CABA pilot (n=20 Alzheimer's-dementia patients, 20 g/day creatine monohydrate, 8 weeks), NIH Toolbox scores rose from baseline to week 8:

MeasurepCohen's dz
Total cognition composite0.020.58
Fluid cognition composite0.0040.74
List Sorting (working memory)0.0010.87
Oral Reading Recognition<0.0011.03
Flanker inhibitory control0.050.33
MMSE0.33no change

Brain total-creatine change correlated with Oral Reading improvement (r=0.60, p=0.005).

Why the vault records this and refuses to believe it. There was no control group, no blinding, and no randomization. The NIH Toolbox has well-documented practice effects: people score better the second time they take it. That is a complete rival explanation for every row in the table above, and it is the explanation the authors themselves reach for — "single-arm trial, we cannot rule out the possibility that improvements may be the result of artifact (test–retest, placebo, and so on)" and "study design limitations prevent the ability to make conclusions of efficacy." The MMSE, which is less practice-sensitive, did not move.

The evidence that does bear on this question points the other way. A randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 10 g vs 20 g vs placebo in healthy young adults found no cognitive benefit and no dose-response at either dose (creatine-dose-response-cognition) — placebo numerically beat the 10 g arm. When an uncontrolled pre-post in 20 dementia patients disagrees with a randomized trial, the randomized trial wins. Per shared/evidence-model.md, this concept is capped at single-study and cannot move creatine-cognition's Status.

What it is legitimately good for. Hypothesis generation, and one specific inference: taken together with the same trial's objective, within-subject brain-uptake measurement (creatine-20g-raises-brain-creatine), it establishes that a properly randomized ≥20 g trial in this population is now feasible and worth running — which is exactly what the authors set out to show. This was a feasibility pilot. It succeeded as one, and it should be read as one.
