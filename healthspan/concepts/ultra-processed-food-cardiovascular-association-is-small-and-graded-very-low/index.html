Ultra-processed food and cardiovascular disease: a small, consistent, very-low-certainty association

Pooling 28 non-overlapping prospective studies (3.5 million adults, mean follow-up 14.2 years), the highest category of ultra-processed-food (UPF) intake versus the lowest carries:

outcomepooled RR (95% CI)studies
cardiovascular disease1.09 (1.05–1.13)21
coronary heart disease1.16 (1.05–1.36)9
cerebrovascular disease1.08 (1.01–1.16)8
hypertension1.16 (1.08–1.26)10

What a relative risk of 1.09 means, and what it does not. It means people in the top UPF category had 9% more cardiovascular events than people in the bottom — relative to whatever the bottom group's rate was. The review reports no absolute risk anywhere, so the number of extra events per thousand people per year is not recoverable from it. A 9% relative increase on a low baseline is a small absolute number; on a high baseline it is a large one. This is the first lens in shared/study-appraisal.md and this literature fails it.

The certainty attached to all four rows is GRADE very low — the lowest of the four GRADE levels, meaning the true effect is likely to be substantially different from the estimate. Of the 43 cohort studies appraised with ROBINS-E, 35 were high risk of bias, 3 very high, 5 some concerns, and none were low. The confidence intervals above describe sampling precision in 3.5 million people; they say nothing about whether the studies were measuring the right thing.

Consistency is the strongest thing here. The direction holds across 21 studies, every continent, both sexes, FFQ and 24-hour-recall instruments, morbidity and mortality endpoints, and follow-up both under and over ten years, with meta-regression finding no difference by any of those. Publication bias is largely clean for the primary outcome (Egger's p = 0.187, Begg's p = 0.904); trim-and-fill leaves RR 1.07 (1.04–1.11); leave-one-out stays at 1.08–1.09. One wrinkle: dose–response is demonstrable for CVD, coronary heart disease and hypertension but not for cerebrovascular disease, which is nonetheless pooled as significant at 1.08 (1.01–1.16). And hypertension is the one outcome with a publication-bias signal (Egger's p = 0.045).

Rebutted by: methodological-rigour-shrinks-the-ultra-processed-food-association-rather-than-attenuating-it — the same paper's meta-regression and risk-of-bias subgroups show that the more carefully a study is analysed, the smaller this estimate gets, which is the opposite of the review's stated conclusion. Read the two together or read neither.
