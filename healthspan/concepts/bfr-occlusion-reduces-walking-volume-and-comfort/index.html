The cuff costs you steps: occlusion raises discomfort and lowers walking volume — an interaction with the vault's best free lever

In a randomized crossover trial (n = 60) comparing 5-minute walking bouts at 0%, 40%, 60% and 80% limb occlusion pressure, rising occlusion produced significantly more perceived exertion and discomfort (p < 0.05) and significantly fewer steps (p < 0.05).

This is a graded, measured interaction between two maps the vault had been carrying as unrelated. daily-steps is the vault's lowest-burden, knee-safe base lever, recommended on a dose-response mortality curve in which steps are the exposure — ~7,000 vs ~2,000 steps/day → all-cause mortality HR 0.53, with the inflection at ~5,000–7,000 (daily-steps-mortality-dose-response). A technique that measurably reduces step count while making the activity less pleasant is not neutral toward the lever it sits on top of. Per shared/safety.md, safety_flags: [interaction] is set and the interaction is noted in both maps.

Interaction evidence is asymmetric, and this one is unusually well-evidenced for a flag. The safety contract says a credible mechanistic or case-level signal is enough to raise a flag, because a missed interaction is worse than an over-cautious one. This is not mechanistic — it is a randomised, within-subject measurement of the interference itself, in the direction of harm to the underlying exposure.

What it is not. The trial ran 5-minute bouts in sedentary college students; it does not establish that habitual BFR walking reduces daily step totals over weeks, and adaptation could plausibly shrink the discomfort. The effect is reported as significance without published magnitudes. Graded suggestive, appraised mixed.

Novel among the vault's BFR evidence. Four prior syntheses — Xu, Hou, Chen, Ren, spanning 51 RCTs — argued about whether occlusion adds strength (bfr-adds-lower-limb-strength-in-athletes), substitutes for heavy load (bfr-matches-heavy-load-strength-in-knee-rehab), relieves pain (bfr-reduces-patellofemoral-pain-during-loaded-tasks), or is hemodynamically tolerable (bfr-cardiovascular-response-data-older-adults). None asked what the cuff costs the activity underneath it. This is the first source in the vault to measure the burden of wearing it.

For David: the practical rule this generates is specific and easy — if BFR ever enters his knee rehab, it belongs on the strength work, not on the walking. Layering occlusion onto his step count would trade a well-evidenced mortality lever for a weakly-evidenced strength adjunct that is still capped at proposed — review with your doctor/physio. Add it to the lifting; leave the walk alone.
