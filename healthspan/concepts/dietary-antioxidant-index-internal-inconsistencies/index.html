A nutrient effect that flips direction by race, disappears after 65, and disagrees with itself about which nutrient matters

The headline association is large — top-versus-bottom-quintile dietary vitamin E at HR 0.69 for all-cause mortality (dietary-antioxidant-intake-tracks-with-lower-mortality). The internal structure underneath it does not behave the way a nutrient effect behaves, and four separate features say so.

Four of the six nutrients have no dose-response at all. Vitamin A p-trend 0.745, vitamin C 0.573, zinc 0.551, selenium 0.142 against all-cause mortality. And the paper reports significant hazard ratios for them anyway — vitamin A's fourth quintile at 0.81, vitamin C's fourth at 0.85, zinc's second at 0.85. A significant middle quintile with a flat trend across the whole distribution is not a dose-response; it is one cell out of five. Across five quintiles × six nutrients × three outcomes that is roughly ninety comparisons, with no multiplicity correction anywhere and no prespecified analysis plan — this was an unregistered secondary analysis of a public dataset, with every analytic choice made after the data were visible.

The two statistical methods disagree about which nutrient is doing the work. The single-nutrient Cox models make vitamin E the standout — the only nutrient significant across all three outcomes. The weighted quantile sum regression, which apportions a joint effect across the mixture, gives the largest weights to vitamin A or C. Two analyses of one dataset naming different active ingredients is what a composite looks like when it is measuring something correlated with all six components rather than any of them.

The association reverses in a race stratum. p-interaction 0.017: non-Hispanic Black participants HR 1.03 (1.01–1.06) — a small but statistically significant association in the opposite direction — against 0.91 (0.84–0.99) in the other-race group. A biological effect of dietary vitamin E should not change sign by demographic group. A socioeconomic gradient measured through food will, because the relationship between what people eat and how they live differs across populations that face different constraints.

And it vanishes with age, in the group where it should matter most. Cancer mortality, p-interaction 0.008: under 65, HR 0.91 (0.85–0.98); 65 and over, HR 1.00 (0.96–1.05) — nothing at all in the half of the sample doing most of the dying.

Why this is graded pushes-back. It contradicts the headline claim of its own source. The vault records both, because that is how a source with a strong result and a weak structure should be carried — the number is real and reported honestly, and the pattern underneath it points at confounding rather than causation. The appraisal (mixed) turns on exactly this material, and it is the reason multivitamins does not move.

The generalisable check this makes concrete. When an observational nutrient association is offered, look for four things before the effect size: does the trend test agree with the quintile the abstract quotes; do the paper's own two methods name the same active ingredient; does the association hold its sign across subgroups; and does it survive in the age group with the events. Here the answer is no, no, no and no — and the vault holds randomised trials of the same nutrients in which nothing happened (multivitamin-no-cvd-cancer-mortality-benefit-cosmos).
