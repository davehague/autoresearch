Twenty-eight randomised trials, and every single control group was doing nothing

Randomisation and blinding solve different problems, and only one of them is available here. Randomisation decides who goes in each group and removes confounding at baseline. Blinding governs what happens afterwards — whether the difference between groups is the treatment or everything that travels with receiving a treatment.

In all 28 trials, the comparison was structured yoga against usual care or a waiting list. The review's eligibility criteria admitted nothing else — no active control, no attention control, no sham, no alternative structured activity.

⚑ So the contrast being measured is not "yoga versus not yoga." It is "a supervised group programme, several times a week, with an instructor, a schedule, other participants and an expectation of benefit" versus "carry on as you were." Everything in that first list plausibly affects blood sugar, and none of it is yoga.

Three concrete routes by which the comparison inflates, all of them ordinary:

Behavioural spillover. Someone enrolled in a diabetes exercise programme is being reminded several times a week that they have diabetes. Diet, medication adherence, sleep and general activity all plausibly shift. None of this happens in the waiting-list arm.

Medication drift, and the paper documents it. ⚠ Only 6 of the 28 trials clearly documented that medication did not change in either arm. One trial had a marked baseline imbalance in metformin use. Another reported more dose reductions in the yoga group — which either reduced the measured contrast, or reflected genuinely improved control, and the data cannot distinguish the two. The authors state the data were too sparse for a medication subgroup analysis.

Expectation. Participants know which arm they are in. On a blood assay this matters less than on a questionnaire — HbA1c cannot be talked up — but it operates through the behaviour that produces the assay, not through the reporting of it.

⚑ The risk-of-bias tally is the summary statistic for all of this: zero of 28 trials reached a low overall risk of bias, on either outcome. Twenty of 22 HbA1c trials and 23 of 24 fasting-glucose trials sat at "some concerns," the rest at high. This is not a literature with a few weak studies in it; it is a literature with no strong ones.

⚠ What a waiting-list design can still legitimately tell you. It answers a real and useful question: if a health service offers this programme to patients, what happens compared with not offering it? That is a pragmatic effectiveness question and the answer may well be "something good." What it cannot answer is the mechanistic one — is it the yoga? — and every general claim about yoga's physiological effects depends on the second question.

⚑ The generalisable rule. This vault sees waiting-list and usual-care comparators constantly across its behavioural maps, and has flagged the same defect on multidomain-lifestyle, whose load-bearing content is precisely "the comparator gradient" and an "unbroken attribution failure." This is that failure in its purest observed form: 28 trials, one comparator type, zero exceptions. It sits alongside the opposite lesson from the same night — that even a well-designed placebo can fail to blind (placebo-hearing-aids-failed-to-blind-more-than-eighty-percent-of-participants). Together they set the question to ask of any behavioural trial: what was the control group actually doing, and did anyone check whether the participants could tell?

In one sentence: a literature that has never once compared yoga against an equally engaging alternative cannot say what yoga contributes, however sophisticated the statistics applied on top of it.
