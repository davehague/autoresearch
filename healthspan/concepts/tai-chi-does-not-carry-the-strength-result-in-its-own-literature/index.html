Split by practice, Tai Chi's own grip-strength result does not clear zero — and on muscle mass the ordering flips

tai-chi is named for Tai Chi and treats Baduanjin, Qigong, Yijinjing and Wuqinxi as the umbrella around it. This synthesis is the first in the vault's corpus to split the umbrella by practice on a quantitative outcome. It comes apart.

Handgrip strength.

PracticeEffect (95% CI)p
Tai Chi+0.25 kg/m² (0.12 to 0.38)<0.01 — the only clearly positive practice

Baduanjin+0.14 (0.00 to 0.28)0.05

⚑ Two outcomes, two different winners, and the practice with the pooled strength benefit is not the one with the pooled mass benefit.

The honest statement is not "Yi Jin Jing is better." Between-subgroup tests were non-significant on both outcomes (p = 0.48 for grip, p = 0.50 for mass), so these estimates are not statistically distinguishable from one another. What the pattern shows is that the literature cannot tell these practices apart, and that the umbrella term is doing more work than the evidence supports. A subgroup of 132 people producing a point estimate more than double the pooled effect, with an interval running from 0.22 to 4.32, is a sparse-network artefact rather than a ranking.

⚑ The consequence for the map is specific. tai-chi already records that 37.3% of the evidence in its founding umbrella review does not state which practice it studied. This adds the harder version of the same problem: when the practice IS specified, the answer moves around, and the named practice is the one that fails on strength. Neither observation is a mark against Tai Chi — it is a statement about how thin this evidence is per-practice.

⚠ Two structural reasons not to over-read either direction. All subgroups inherit the pool's inactive comparators — health education, usual care or nothing in 16 of 17 trials (the-sarcopenia-tce-literature-has-one-trial-against-real-exercise) — so every one of these numbers is "versus not exercising." And the Baduanjin grip row as printed reads "MD = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.93–1.13", an interval whose upper bound equals its point estimate; the paper contains two further arithmetic errors including an impossible confidence interval in its own abstract (TUG "−2.80, 95% CI −2.55 to −2.05"). Subgroup numbers from a paper with three printed arithmetic errors are read as directional, not as measurements.

Nothing here changes what the map recommends or why. The map's case for Tai Chi is knee-joint function, balance, bone loading and zero cost in a joint-sparing format — not strength. What this closes off is a claim nobody should have been making: that these practices build strength or muscle competitively with lifting.

Capped suggestive on a mixed appraisal.
