"The benefit did not differ significantly between groups" turned out to be a test with a 5% chance of noticing if it did

For roughly a decade, the argument for extending the GLP-1 cardiovascular benefit from people with established heart disease to people without it has been an interaction test: the trials checked whether the treatment effect differs between the two groups, found no significant difference, and concluded the overall result applies to both.

This review ran the test properly across all eleven trials — interaction estimate 0.99 (95% CI 0.81 to 1.22), comfortably non-significant, apparently reassuring — and then did the thing that is almost never done. It computed how much power that test had to detect a real difference. Five percent.

⚑ What 5% power means, in plain terms. Statistical power is the probability of finding an effect given that the effect is real. The conventional target is 80%. At 5%, a test is barely distinguishable from a coin that almost always lands on "no difference" — it was going to return a reassuring answer whether or not the groups actually differ. The reassurance carried essentially no information, and it has been load-bearing for prescribing guidance.

⚑ Why interaction tests are so weak, and why this generalises far beyond one drug. Detecting a difference between two effects needs roughly four times the sample of detecting one effect — the two subgroup estimates each carry their own uncertainty, and the comparison inherits both. So a trial powered exactly for its main outcome is, by construction, badly underpowered for any subgroup comparison inside it. A non-significant interaction test is the expected result of an adequately powered trial, whether or not the subgroups differ.

⚑ The practical rule this yields, and the vault should apply it in both directions. When a paper reports "the effect was consistent across subgroups" or "there was no significant interaction," the honest translation is usually "we could not tell," not "they are the same." This cuts both ways: it undermines confident extrapolation of a benefit to an untested group, and it equally undermines confident claims that a subgroup is spared a harm. The authors put it exactly: "A non-significant interaction test does not necessarily mean that the overall treatment effect applies to all patients."

⚠ What it does not establish. A powerless interaction test is not evidence that the groups do differ. It removes an argument; it supplies none. The primary-prevention question stays open on its own merits (glp1-cardiovascular-benefit-is-not-established-without-prior-heart-disease). Capped suggestive; the interaction estimates come from aggregated trial data, and the authors note that individual-participant data would test this properly.
