A vaccine given for a rash tracks with 9% less time lived with heart disease

Over 7 years and 36,460 matched pairs of US adults aged 60 and over, people who got the recombinant shingles vaccine (Shingrix) rather than the older live attenuated one had a restricted mean time lost ratio of 0.91 (95% CI 0.88–0.95) on a composite of ischaemic heart disease, heart failure and ischaemic stroke.

What "RMTL ratio" means, since it is not a hazard ratio. Restricted mean time lost is the average share of the 7-year window a person spends having already had the event. A ratio of 0.91 means the recombinant group spent 9% less of those seven years living with the diagnosis — equivalently, 9% more time lived diagnosis-free. The authors chose this over a hazard ratio because they expected the proportional-hazards assumption to fail across seven years, and it did: the effect appeared early and faded later, which a single hazard ratio would have averaged into mush.

The interval 0.88–0.95 is narrow and entirely below 1. It rules out no effect, and it also rules out anything dramatic: the most this data supports is a 12% reduction in burden, the least is 5%. The authors give the absolute scale themselves — a 0.8% difference in cumulative incidence of ischaemic heart disease and heart failure. Small for any individual; large across a population of tens of millions.

The components disagree in an informative way. Ischaemic heart disease 0.90 (0.87–0.94) and heart failure 0.88 (0.83–0.93) both clear comfortably. Atrial fibrillation 0.93 (0.88–0.98) — which the authors read as a validation, since atrial fibrillation and ischaemic heart disease are bidirectionally linked. ⚑ Ischaemic stroke did not clear in the pooled analysis and reached significance only in males (0.88, 0.78–0.98). The authors offer two readings and refuse to choose: a genuinely cardiac-specific mechanism, or simple lack of power, since ischaemic strokes occur 2–4 times less often than heart failure or ischaemic heart disease at these ages — and consistently, the signal appears in males, whose stroke rate was 15% higher.

Four outcomes were null: myocarditis, peripheral arterial disease, haemorrhagic stroke and transient ischaemic attack. The myocarditis null does real work — myocarditis can follow vaccination and is misdiagnosable as ischaemic heart disease, so a myocarditis signal would have suggested the main finding was a coding artefact. It is absent, and the ST-elevation myocardial infarction estimate (a diagnosis much harder to confuse with myocarditis) is of similar magnitude to the main result, though not significant on its own.

⚠ The effect is small enough that the design has to carry it, and the design's own robustness statistic is modest. The E-value is 1.42 (an-e-value-of-1-4-is-a-low-bar-for-an-unmeasured-confounder) — an unmeasured factor only 1.42 times more common in one cohort and 1.42-fold linked to heart disease would explain the whole thing. Socioeconomic status is not in electronic health records at all.

⚑ What makes it worth recording despite that: the comparison is vaccinated people against other vaccinated people (comparing-vaccinated-people-to-other-vaccinated-people-removes-the-healthy-vaccinee-bias), the two cohorts showed no difference in the same endpoint in the year before vaccination (0.99, 0.92–1.06), a pre-specified negative control outcome was null, a tetanus-vaccine secular-trend control was null (1.01, 0.99–1.03), there was no association with non-cardiovascular death, and the finding survived four sensitivity analyses including truncation to the pre-COVID period.

In one sentence: the best non-randomised evidence yet that an adult vaccine might have a cardiovascular benefit, at an effect size small enough that only the design's unusual rigour keeps it interesting — which is exactly why the authors' own conclusion is "run the trial."
