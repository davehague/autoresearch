Walking's effect on perceived sleep quality is unresolved — and the null that says otherwise is unstable

Six of the eight trials reported the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Pooled: MD −1.41 (95% CI −3.18 to 0.36) — not statistically significant — with I² = 92.7%, near-total between-study inconsistency.

This is an open question, not a negative result, and the distinction is the whole point of the note. The vault has spent the past week catching papers that sell an underpowered null as equivalence (rt-intensity-safety-comparison-underpowered, where falls RR 2.68 with a CI reaching 11.11 was called "similar safety"; rt-non-failure-nulls-hypertrophy-endurance-power-underpowered, where I²=0% proved the trials agreed and only sample size widened the intervals). The same lens must be applied when the result is inconvenient in the other direction — to a null on an intervention the vault likes.

Apply it: the confidence interval runs from −3.18 to +0.36. A 3-point PSQI improvement is a clinically meaningful improvement in perceived sleep, and it sits comfortably inside this interval. So does zero. The honest statement is that six small, extremely heterogeneous trials could not resolve whether walking makes people feel they slept better — not that it doesn't.

The authors deserve credit for showing their work. Their leave-one-out sensitivity analysis reports that removing any single trial swung the pooled estimate between −0.91 and −2.03, and that in one iteration it crossed into significance (−2.03, −3.78 to −0.27), with heterogeneity never dropping below I² = 87.7%. They conclude the PSQI estimate is "unstable and sensitive to the inclusion of individual trials" and should be read "with considerable caution." That is the opposite of laundering — and it is why this source is graded mixed rather than unreliable despite a headline that could easily have been overclaimed.

How this sits against the objective half of the same paper. The device-measured outcomes did move (+34 min total sleep time; −12 min wake after sleep onset — walking-improves-objective-sleep-duration-and-wakefulness). One reading is that walking changes sleep architecture more than it changes sleep satisfaction. Another is that both results are noise from a literature too small to interrogate. The vault does not choose between them on this evidence; it records that the interesting asymmetry exists and logs the gap.
