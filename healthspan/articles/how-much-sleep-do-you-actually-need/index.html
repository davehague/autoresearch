How much sleep do you actually need? What the evidence supports

The honest answer is a U-shaped curve, and both ends of it are weaker than they look at first glance. Pooling 79 cohort studies, sleeping less than 7 hours a night tracks with modestly higher mortality risk, and sleeping 9 or more hours tracks with a notably larger increase — but the second number is probably telling you about illness that causes long sleep, not the other way around. Newer evidence sharpens both tails further: self-reported sleep gets the shape of the curve right but understates the real effect size, and the danger in short sleep looks like it concentrates in people who also have insomnia symptoms, not in short duration alone. Here's what actually holds up, and what doesn't.

Not medical advice. This summarizes published evidence to inform your own choices and any conversation with your clinician.

The founding claim: a U-shape, with two very different tails

A meta-analysis of 79 cohort studies found habitual short sleep (<7 hours/night) carried 14% higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.10–1.18) and long sleep (≥9 hours/night) carried 34% higher mortality (HR 1.34, 1.26–1.42), both against a 7–8 hour reference. Read as two separate findings, not one:

The short-sleep tail is modest and directionally stable — a 14% relative increase, consistent across dozens of cohorts, though it rests on self-reported sleep and sits within the range ordinary confounding could move. "At least ~7 hours" is a defensible floor, not a hard line.

The long-sleep tail is most likely reverse causation. Sleeping 9+ hours is a well-documented downstream marker of undiagnosed illness, frailty, depression and inflammation — all of which cause both the long sleep and the earlier death. The underlying studies don't exclude early deaths or run lag analyses, so they can't separate "long sleep is harmful" from "dying people sleep more." Nothing here supports deliberately restricting sleep. If you find yourself needing 9+ hours persistently, that's worth raising with a clinician — a diagnostic flag, not a habit to correct on your own.

Self-report gets the shape right and the size wrong

A UK Biobank study measured the same 76,811 adults two ways at once — a week of wrist-accelerometer data and a same-time questionnaire — and compared what each instrument found. Both traced the same curve shape and landed on a similar optimum (~7.2 hours by questionnaire, ~7.7 by device). But the size of the protective effect diverged sharply: at 7 hours versus a 5-hour reference, the device-measured data showed a 30% mortality reduction (HR 0.70, 0.61–0.79); the self-reported version showed an effect so small its upper bound touched "no effect at all" (HR 0.86, 0.74–1.00). This is textbook measurement error, quantified in one population. Nearly every large sleep-duration study — including the one behind the U-shaped curve above — asks people how long they slept rather than measuring it. The practical takeaway: this map's numbers are probably conservative, not inflated.

The short-sleep danger may be about who is short, not how short

A smaller but more precisely measured study helps explain why. Researchers ran 767 adults through overnight laboratory sleep recording — the gold-standard measurement — and split them into four groups: insomnia symptoms or not, crossed with objectively short sleep (<6 hours) or not. Only one group carried excess mortality risk: people with both insomnia symptoms and objectively short sleep (more than double the risk). Short sleep without insomnia symptoms carried no detectable excess risk. Insomnia symptoms with normal sleep duration carried no detectable excess risk either. And when the identical four-way split was run on self-reported (rather than lab-measured) short sleep — the instrument behind almost every large cohort, including the 79-study meta-analysis above — it found nothing at all.

This is one modestly sized study, not a replacement for the larger literature, and it doesn't rule out reverse causation any more than the rest of this evidence does. But it points toward a more useful practical rule than "get 7 hours": persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep is the thing worth taking seriously and treating — with cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia and a clinician, not a supplement — more than the raw hour count is.

A newer cohort tested whether activity buys back short sleep — and the answer is contested

Two independent cohorts have now asked whether being physically active protects you from short sleep's cardiovascular risk. A UK Biobank study that measured both sleep and activity with wrist accelerometers on 87,879 adults found that it does: among people with low activity, poor sleep raised cardiovascular and mortality risk as expected, but among people with high activity, the same poor-sleep categories carried lower risk than well-rested, low-activity people. A second, smaller Chinese cohort asked the same question with self-reported activity in 5,873 adults and found the opposite — activity didn't measurably blunt short sleep's cardiovascular risk. The second study tracked far fewer cardiovascular events, which likely leaves it underpowered to detect the interaction rather than genuinely refuting it, but the honest state of the evidence is: don't count on exercise to fully offset a chronically short-sleep schedule. The two studies agree on the more important point — short sleep raises cardiovascular risk either way.

Sleep duration is the weaker lever, and it's measured worse

This vault's evidence also holds a sleep-timing claim, and it's a better-supported one. How consistently you sleep — not how long — predicted mortality and cardiovascular events more strongly than sleep duration did, in studies that used real wristband measurement rather than a questionnaire. Duration is real, but it's the smaller and more weakly measured of the two signals this vault holds on sleep.

So — what should you do?

Treat 7 hours as a reasonable floor to fall out of a consistent schedule, not a number to chase for its own sake. Don't restrict sleep to hit a "shorter is fine" belief, and don't panic if you occasionally need more — but if you find yourself needing 9+ hours as a persistent pattern, or if you have ongoing trouble falling or staying asleep, those are worth a clinician conversation rather than something to self-manage. Anchoring consistent bed and wake times — the stronger, better-measured lever — does more for you than optimizing the hour count directly, and tends to raise duration as a side effect anyway.
