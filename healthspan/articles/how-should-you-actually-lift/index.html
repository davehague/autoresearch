How should you actually lift? What the evidence supports on load, effort, volume and surface

Short answer: heavier builds more lower-body strength, you do not have to grind to failure, adding sets past a moderate number buys nothing measurable, wobbly surfaces buy confidence rather than capability — and the most reliable finding about this entire literature is that it is too small to answer its own secondary questions, which has not stopped it from answering them anyway.

A companion article on this atlas — how much lifting do you actually need — covers whether and how much: roughly 90–119 minutes a week of resistance training tracks with 13–27% lower mortality in a 147,374-person cohort, and the benefit plateaus above about two hours. That is a different question from this one. This article is about the dials you set once you have decided to lift: how heavy, how hard, how many sets, on what footing.

Read this as an early-stage report. The evidence here is graded emerging — the lowest confidence tier this atlas uses — and the reason is structural: every finding below is measured on a strength or function surrogate. None of it has been connected to living longer. Nothing here is medical advice, and one section carries an explicit safety flag.

These get blended together constantly — "train hard" is used to mean heavy, or to mean to failure, or to mean a lot of sets. They are four separate questions with four separate answers, and two of those answers point away from working harder.

1. Heavy loading builds more lower-body strength

A meta-analysis of 18 randomised trials in 1,283 adults aged 50 and over (Shen 2026, PMID 42366614) compared high-intensity resistance training (≥70% of one-rep max) against low-to-moderate. Heavy won on the trained lifts: leg press SMD 0.95 (95% CI 0.48–1.43) and leg extension SMD 0.63 (0.09–1.17). In plain terms, a standardised mean difference near 1.0 is a large effect — the heavy groups ended up meaningfully stronger on the movements they trained.

One boundary, and it is a real one. A separate network meta-analysis of 19 RCTs in 1,267 older adults with diagnosed sarcopenia (Jin-Hao 2026, sarcopenia network) found moderate intensity ranked first for handgrip strength — MD 2.37 kg (0.76–3.98) — not heavy. That is not a reversal. Handgrip is not a trained lift; no program loads it directly, so a dose–response gradient measured in a leg press has no automatic claim on grip. What it establishes is narrower and worth holding: "heavier builds more strength" has been shown for the legs, in the lifts you actually train, and has not been shown to govern grip and has never been tested in a sarcopenic population.

In the same network, nothing beat routine control on gait speed at all. Every dial in this article is set on strength surrogates; the functional outcome sits unmoved behind them.

2. You do not have to train to failure

Twenty randomised trials in 556 healthy adults (Wu 2026, PMID 42410632) compared stopping short of failure against grinding each set out. Non-failure training won on dynamic strength: SMD 0.24 (0.06–0.42) across 19 trials and 461 people — a small effect, in the convenient direction.

The convenient direction is exactly why the rest of that paper needs reading carefully. Its four other outcomes — isometric strength, hypertrophy, muscular endurance and power — all came back non-significant, and the authors summarised them as non-failure being "as effective… for most neuromuscular adaptations." That is an equivalence claim, and the data do not support one. Three of those four confidence intervals have upper bounds that meet or exceed the paper's own significant 0.24, and heterogeneity was reported at I² = 0% — meaning the trials agree almost perfectly and nothing but sample size is keeping those intervals wide. The paper's own limitations section concedes the studies "may have limited their statistical power."

The honest version: stopping a rep or two short is at least as good for dynamic strength, and possibly as good for everything else — but "possibly" is doing real work in that sentence. For most people that is enough to act on, because the cheaper option is also the one with evidence behind it.

3. More sets stopped buying anything, and this is the best-designed study in the whole area

Every complaint above is about statistical power. One study fixed it.

Camargo 2026 (doi 10.1152/japplphysiol.00284.2026) used a within-subject unilateral design: 25 resistance-trained adults trained both legs twice weekly, with one leg progressed to a much higher weekly set count than the other. Each person was their own control group, which removes almost all the between-person noise that makes training studies underpowered. The achieved separation was verified and reported: 32.8 ± 5.9 versus 18.0 ± 3.3 sets per week (P < 0.001).

Result: muscle cross-sectional area rose in both legs over eight weeks with no difference between them (no protocol × time interaction, P = 0.197). Fascicle length, pennation angle, fibre cross-sectional area, satellite cell number and the whole anabolic signalling panel were null as well.

Why this null counts when the others do not. It carries a prospective power analysis — 10,000 simulations, 80% power for a deliberately conservative effect size, minimum n = 24 — and it recruited 25. Add pre-registration, a CONSORT participant flow, 98.5% adherence, blinded ultrasound assessors, standardised post-exercise protein, public funding and no conflicts. This atlas grades it reliable, the only source on this topic to earn that.

⚠ Two caveats, one of which is the most decision-relevant fact in this article. First, 80% power for a moderate difference does not exclude a small one — "33 sets is no better than 18" is well supported; "no amount of extra volume ever helps anyone" is not. Second, and buried in the participant flow rather than the discussion: three of 28 participants withdrew "due to persistent muscle or joint pain." These were healthy, resistance-trained, injury-screened 18-to-35-year-olds doing eight weeks of supervised leg press and leg extension. Roughly 11% dropped out hurt. The within-subject design makes it impossible to blame the high-volume or low-volume leg — each withdrawal removes both at once — but it establishes that musculoskeletal dropout from a supervised quadriceps program is not confined to the elderly or the frail.

And note what the title said. The paper is titled to say large volume increases "do not impair muscle hypertrophy," while its own summary block says there is "no evidence… under the present experimental conditions." Even the good study wrote its null as equivalence in the line that gets quoted.

4. Unstable surfaces buy confidence, not capability

Seven RCTs in older adults (Doan 2026, doi 10.1097/PHM.0000000000002966) compared training on unstable surfaces — wobble boards, foam pads, stability balls — against the same work on the floor. No significant advantage on lower-limb strength or short-term mobility, with the five-times sit-to-stand test actually trending toward stable training.

What did improve was fear of falling and a 24-week Timed Up and Go. ⚠ Fear of falling is a self-reported confidence questionnaire in an intervention nobody can be blinded to — close to the most expectation-prone result this design can produce. And no fall count, injury count or adverse event appears anywhere in the paper.

This is a substitution worth naming, because it recurs. A separate review of exergaming — screen-delivered balance games, 9 RCTs, 1,385 adults aged 60+ — found the same shape one rung up: fall rate dropped (IRR 0.53, 0.41–0.68), but the number of people who fell mostly did not, and injurious falls were measured in exactly one trial and came back non-significant. "Frequent fallers fell less often," "fewer people fell," and "fewer people were hurt" are three different claims, and the evidence gets thinner at every step toward the one that matters.

That review also did something rare and instructive: its authors split their results by how strong the comparison group was. Against a leaflet or low-intensity activity, exergaming looked good — IRR 0.58 (0.44–0.77), the only moderate-certainty rating in the paper. Against actual strength-and-balance training, every estimate was low certainty and the number-of-fallers result pointed the wrong way. The impressive headline was partly borrowed from the weakest comparison.

Across four separate literatures — unstable surfaces, exergames, cognitive training, and standard programming — the training is the active ingredient and the packaging is not.

5. Bone: train at all, and stop shopping for a load

For a year this atlas held that bone density does not respond to how heavy you lift. That was true and it was answering a narrower question than it sounded like.

The nulls all compared two things people do: heavy versus light loading (lumbar spine SMD 0.28, −0.02 to 0.58), creatine-plus-training versus training, a potassium salt versus placebo. None of them compared training against not training.

Zhang 2026 (bone density network, doi 10.1007/s00223-026-01578-8) did — 34 RCTs, 1,746 middle-aged and older adults — and found moderate-intensity resistance training three times a week beat control at the femoral neck (SMD 0.26, 0.06–0.46) and the trochanter (SMD 0.34, 0.10–0.58). A quarter to a third of a standard deviation, with lower bounds at 0.06 and 0.10: an effect whose existence is better established than its size.

The revised position: train, at ordinary intensity, and expect a small effect on hip bone density. Do not choose a load — or a supplement — for your skeleton. Bone mineral density is still a surrogate; no fracture outcome appears anywhere in this evidence. And the winning category was moderate, not heavy, so nothing here is an argument for lifting heavier.

⚠ The safety flag, stated plainly

This topic carries a high-dose-risk flag in the atlas, and the reason is that nobody has shown heavy lower-limb loading is safe — not that anybody has shown it is dangerous.

The same meta-analysis that established heavy loading's strength advantage also reported fall incidence RR 2.68 (95% CI 0.65–11.11) and adverse events RR 2.42 (0.66–8.88) between intensities — both point estimates above 2, both intervals stretching past an elevenfold increase — and concluded the intensities had "similar safety profiles."

That is an underpowered null read as a clean bill of health, and it is the single clearest example of the pattern running through this whole literature. Heavy lifting builds more strength is supported. Heavy lifting is safe for a knee that has been injured is not a claim this evidence can make in either direction.

If you have a joint history — a previous knee injury, osteoarthritis, anything that changes how a loaded squat feels — heavy lower-limb loading is a conversation with a physio or a doctor, not something to read off an effect size. That is the atlas's standing position and this article does not soften it.

The pattern worth taking away

Seven times now, across this literature, a study has run an analysis it was never powered to complete, found nothing, and written the result up as equivalence. Every time, the null flattered what the authors were already recommending.

Two of those seven flattered the low-effort conclusion that would have been convenient for this atlas too — and were refused on the same grounds, which is the only condition under which the criticism means anything.

The sharpest version came from the best study in the set. Camargo 2026 was registered, powered, conflict-free and public-funded, and it still titled its null as equivalence. So the problem is not statistical incompetence. It is a writing-and-incentives problem, and a good trial committed it anyway.

Practically, what this means for reading anything in this area: when a training study tells you two approaches are "equally effective," check whether it found a difference or merely failed to find one. Those are not the same sentence, and only one of them is evidence.

What would change these answers

  • A trial powered on falls and injuries, not fear of falling or balance scores, comparing training intensities in adults over 50. This is the biggest hole and it has been open for a month.
  • Any of these dials tested against a functional or hard outcome rather than a strength surrogate.
  • A volume study powered for a small difference rather than a moderate one.
  • Load and grip strength tested in the same population, since the current answers come from different people.
