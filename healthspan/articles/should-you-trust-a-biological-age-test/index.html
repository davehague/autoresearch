Should you trust a biological age test? What the evidence supports

Short answer: no commercial biological-age panel has been shown to tell a healthy person anything actionable. The best-validated single measure is a research algorithm, not a product you can buy standalone. Most of the field's other biomarkers don't even reliably predict death, and none of them — including the best one — has been shown to respond to anything in a healthy person in a way connected to a real outcome.

This is not an intervention article. There is no dose, no protocol, no "should you do this." It is about whether to trust a number — a "biological age," an "epigenetic age," an organ-age score — that a test kit or a lab panel hands you, and what that number can and cannot tell you.

Four findings carry the whole case:

  • A head-to-head test of 14 widely-used aging biomarkers found 9 of them did not predict death at all, even though several are routine primary endpoints in aging trials.
  • The entire panel, pooled, classified who would die only slightly better than a coin flip (C-index 0.65 — and three-quarters of that performance came from just three measures).
  • Only about a third of the biomarkers that did respond to an intervention did so in healthy people — the responsiveness lives almost entirely in people who are already sick.
  • No aging clock — not one, in any study this map holds — has a defined minimum change that means anything clinically. A lab can tell you your number moved. No one can tell you what that movement is worth.

What a "biological age clock" actually is

Chronological age is just how long you've been alive. A biological-age clock is an algorithm — usually built from DNA methylation patterns (an "epigenetic clock"), sometimes from blood proteins ("proteomic organ clocks"), sometimes from a panel of ordinary clinical measures — trained to output a number that is supposed to track how fast or how far your body has aged, independent of the calendar.

The promise is straightforward: if the number is lower than your age, you're aging well; if an intervention lowers the number, the intervention is working on aging itself, not just on one disease. The problem is that almost nothing in that chain has actually been tested. This article is about what has.

The first grading: most of a "consensus" biomarker panel predicts nothing

A panel of aging-research experts selected 14 biomarkers — blood markers, physical-performance tests, and epigenetic clocks — as the field's best candidates for measuring biological aging in trials. One study put all 14 head-to-head against real mortality in the same people (BASE-II, a German longitudinal cohort, 1,083 adults, 316 deaths), with one analytic pipeline so the comparison would be fair (Vetter 2026, PMID 41792861).

Five predicted death. Nine did not — including gait speed, the frailty phenotype, muscle mass, blood pressure, CRP, and IGF-1. These are not fringe measures. Gait speed and the frailty phenotype are routine primary endpoints in geroscience trials today. A trial powered on either would have been powered on something that, in this cohort, did not track the outcome anyone actually cares about.

And the whole 14-marker panel, used together, discriminated who would die only weakly. Its C-index — a measure of how well a score ranks people by risk, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect — reached 0.65. Three markers alone got to 0.63. The other eleven, combined, bought an extra 0.02. The field's instinct is to build bigger panels; this result says that, past a point, a bigger panel buys almost nothing.

One biomarker in the fourteen stood apart: DunedinPACE, an algorithm that estimates your pace of aging (how fast you're aging right now) rather than your attained biological age. It was the single strongest, most consistent predictor of death in this cohort, and it keeps winning every time this literature re-runs the comparison in a new population — more on that below.

The second grading: does moving the number do anything?

Predicting death in people you never touched is one question. Whether an intervention that moves a clock actually changes anything is a completely different question, and it is the one that matters if you're buying a test to track progress.

A systematic test across 51 published human intervention studies (3,128 pre/post blood samples, 16 clocks) found that 19 interventions significantly lowered epigenetic age (13 after correcting for the number of comparisons), 5 raised it, and 26 — more than half — did nothing measurable (Sehgal 2026, PMID 42629466).

The responsiveness is not evenly spread, and where it concentrates is the part worth remembering. Restricting to the 41 studies done in people without a diagnosed disease, no biomarker response survived correction for multiple comparisons at all. The actual signal lived almost entirely in the 10 studies done in sick populations. The commercial market for these tests is healthy people. The measurable response is in sick ones.

DunedinPACE again stood out — the most responsive of sixteen clocks tested, and the only one that moved in both healthy and diseased populations rather than only one. That is a genuinely interesting property. It does not change the next problem, which applies to every clock including this one.

The gap nobody has closed: a moving number with no known meaning

Even where a clock does move, this literature is explicit that nobody has defined what amount of movement matters. There is no established minimum clinically important difference for any DNA-methylation clock — no number a lab can hand you with "and this much change is worth something to your health" attached. Responsiveness is a prerequisite for a clock to mean something as a health outcome. It is not the same as meaning something, and the authors who built this responsiveness catalogue say so themselves.

This map's name for the resulting rule is predictor ≠ responder: a biomarker that reliably forecasts death in an untouched population has not thereby been shown to be a valid trial endpoint, because an intervention can shift the number without shifting the thing the number is supposed to stand in for. The field keeps citing clock changes as if they closed this gap. They don't.

Newest and most expensive isn't winning either

The newest entrant to this market is the plasma-proteomic organ clock — a blood-protein-based estimate of how fast a specific organ (liver, heart, immune system, and so on) is aging, sold as more targeted and more informative than a single whole-body number.

Benchmarked head-to-head against the measures above, in the same people, with the same statistical pipeline (Lothian Birth Cohort 1936, 861 adults, 444 deaths over 16 years), the best-performing organ clocks — liver, immune, and heart — predicted mortality at a hazard ratio of 1.38 to 1.43 per standard deviation. Every one of them was beaten by DNA-methylation age, brain volume on an MRI, a basic breathing test, and a cognitive test score — ordinary measures, run in the same cohort, scoring 1.44 to 1.62 (Pyrgioti 2026, PMID 42823846). The newest, most expensive technology in this space did not outperform a spirometer and a memory test.

The same study separately scanned 9,703 individual blood proteins for a mortality signal and found 368 worth watching, led by three (GDF15, WFDC2, TIMP1) with associations in the same range as the best organ clocks. That is a useful list for future research. It is not, on its own, a reason to buy anything — it is a prediction exercise, in people nobody intervened on, exactly like everything above it.

It gets worse the more carefully you look

Three unrelated literatures converge on the same shape, and it's worth naming as a pattern rather than three separate findings.

Residential greenness and epigenetic age is a popular "nature is good for your cells" claim. A review reported a "consistent" 1.0-to-1.6-year deceleration — but that headline came from three studies totalling roughly 755 people, while the one properly pooled analysis in the field (seven European cohorts, 5,975 adults) found essentially nothing (β = −0.01, 95% CI −0.08 to 0.06). The signal that survives review-level narrative collapses at the scale where it would actually need to hold.

A separate biological-pathway attack on the same question — perturbing the molecular hub genes thought to drive aging, across methylation data from more than 15,000 samples spanning 17 tissues — found that most computational perturbations made the aging profile worse, not better, and concluded that most aging co-methylation networks are "fragile and resistant to beneficial modulation." Two completely different methods, one epidemiological and one mechanistic, land on the same discouraging answer.

And a sex-specific re-analysis of 38 organ-aging clocks found that a clock's predictive validity itself — not just its value — can differ by sex and by organ. Every ranking in this article was estimated on sex-pooled or single-sex cohorts that never tested for that interaction. Nothing here overturns DunedinPACE's standing, but it is one more axis of uncertainty nobody had been checking.

What this means if you are deciding whether to spend money

Don't buy a biological-age test expecting it to tell you something actionable about your own healthspan. Across every grading this map holds — a 14-biomarker panel, a 51-intervention responsiveness catalogue, a 15-clock longevity comparison, a sex-specific re-analysis, and now a head-to-head against the newest proteomic technology — the pattern repeats: most candidate biomarkers don't predict death reliably; the ones that do respond to anything mostly respond in sick people, not healthy ones; and not one of them has an established threshold for what a change is worth.

If you want the one number this literature actually respects, it has a name — DunedinPACE — and it is a research algorithm, not a retail product you can simply order. It is the best-validated predictor in every cohort this map has checked, including this run's addition, and the most broadly responsive clock tested. It has still never been shown, in anyone, that moving it changes a hard outcome. Predictor, not proven responder — and definitely not a validated surrogate.

The honestly useful numbers are the ones you already have access to, cheaply, from measures this map's own benchmarking keeps ranking at or above the newest clocks: grip strength, a basic cognitive test, respiratory function, and — if you want a hard number — actual cardiorespiratory fitness. None of them need a subscription.
