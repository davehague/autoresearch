Does brain training actually work? What the evidence supports — and the one thing it has never shown

Cognitive training — the puzzle apps, the computerised memory drills, the "brain games" category — reliably improves performance on the specific task you practice. Across every design this vault has checked, including the one that removes every excuse, it has never been shown to improve anything you didn't directly train. That's not a hedge; it's the single finding eight independent sources, across four different literatures, keep converging on.

Not medical advice. This summarizes published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician; it doesn't prescribe a dose, duration, or product.

The founding evidence, and the crack in its headline

The best network meta-analysis on digital cognitive training (20 RCTs, n=1,358, adults 65+, pre-registered on OSF) ranked one regimen first for verbal fluency: computerised cognitive training combined with social and language activities, SMD 0.47 (95% CI 0.02 to 0.93) against a passive control (Hu 2026, International Psychogeriatrics, PMID 42538168).

Read that interval again: it clears zero by two hundredths. And the winning arm never isolated the software from the people — no comparison arm tested the computer task without the added social and language contact. A result this close to null, on a comparator this weak (passive control, no blinding possible), confounded with the one variable — social contact — that this vault's dementia-prevention evidence rates as more credible on its own than anything in this training literature, is thin ground to build a recommendation on.

The design that removes every excuse — and finds the same thing

Every prior source had an escape hatch: maybe the transfer failure is really a weak-comparator artifact. One study closed that hatch completely. A meta-analysis of 23 sham-controlled trials (135 effect sizes, healthy adults only) had both arms do the identical cognitive training — the only difference was whether a weak brain-stimulation current (tDCS) was real or sham, and participants were genuinely blinded (Wang 2026, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, PMID 42609444).

The trained task improved: Hedges' g = 0.424 (95% CI 0.180–0.667). Untrained working memory — actual transfer — did not: g = 0.130 (−0.040 to 0.301), not significant. With the comparator problem designed out entirely, the pattern held exactly where the weaker studies predicted it would.

A theory paper explains why, and narrows the claim usefully

A preregistered meta-analysis of 55 studies (208 effect sizes) on working-memory training found the transfer failure isn't symmetric — it has a direction. Training on demanding "updating" tasks transferred to simpler "span" tasks (g=0.176, p<.001); training on the simpler tasks did not transfer back the other way (g=0.048, p=.453) (Fu 2026, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, PMID 42711482). That's a narrower, more precise claim than "transfer never happens" — it's near-transfer within working memory, not far transfer to daily function — and it's compatible with, not a contradiction of, the broader pattern.

Three more independent checks, same answer

A trial in people with prodromal Lewy body disease found nine hours of cognitive training moved the one domain it drilled (executive function, d=0.50 at 6 weeks) and left eleven other pre-specified outcomes — memory, language, mood, motor symptoms, subjective complaints — flat (Ophey 2026, Alzheimer's & Dementia, PMID 42555286). A gait-and-balance meta-analysis found cognitive training improved only dual-task walking speed, with even that signal vanishing once you restrict to the highest-quality trials (Li 2026, PMID 42525971). And in clinically diagnosed Alzheimer's patients, cognitive training combined with exercise ranked highest for executive function (SUCRA 99.9%) on the one outcome in the network with no detectable publication bias — but the winning arm again bundles two interventions, so it can't say what the cognitive-training component contributed on its own (Zhou 2026, Frontiers in Neurology, PMID 42812254).

The closest thing to a counter-example — and why it doesn't change the answer

The one source that gets closest to this map's own bar for promotion — a randomised trial, a 20-year horizon, a hard clinical endpoint rather than a test score — is a Medicare-claims follow-up of the decades-old ACTIVE trial. It reported that speed-of-processing training, if a participant completed a voluntary booster session, associated with lower diagnosed-dementia risk two decades later (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.59–0.95); the same training without the booster showed nothing (HR 1.01) (Coe 2026, Alzheimer's & Dementia: Translational Research, PMID 41669119). It's a tempting headline — until you notice six hazard ratios were tested (three training arms × booster status) with no stated correction for multiple comparisons, and the paper leads with the one that happened to clear significance. A published companion critique in the same journal applied the missing correction and found the interval widens to include no-effect; its own re-analysis instead points to a small, roughly uniform effect across all three training types, concentrated in the youngest participants — a different, less flattering story than "speed training plus a booster prevents dementia." Booster-session completion was also self-selected, not randomised, which reopens the exact healthy-adherer bias a 20-year randomised trial is supposed to close.

What this is actually worth to you

If you want to get better at a specific cognitive task — faster at a particular memory game, quicker on a particular attention drill — the evidence says that will happen reliably, because that's what practice does for any skill. What the evidence does not support, in eight independent checks across four literatures and every study design that could have shown it, is that this translates into a measurably sharper memory, better daily function, or lower dementia risk. The field's best-controlled study — the one that eliminated the comparator problem entirely — found exactly the same ceiling everyone else found.

The commercial brain-training market is large, and this vault has graded none of its specific products — nothing here names or recommends one. If something in your life already delivers social contact, physical movement, or structured learning, the evidence in this vault rates those levers higher on their own merits (see the social-connection and resistance-training maps) than anything measured here. Brain-training apps are not harmful on this evidence; they're also not shown to do anything beyond make you better at themselves.

The bottom line

Cognitive training moves the trained task. It has never been shown, in any design this vault has checked — including the sham-controlled one built specifically to test it fairly — to move anything else. If the goal is genuinely better memory or lower dementia risk rather than a higher score on a specific app, the evidence doesn't support spending time or money here over better-evidenced levers already in this vault. No dose, session count, or product is recommended, because none has earned one.
