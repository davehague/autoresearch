Should you stop taking a statin after 75? What the evidence supports — and why its two best trials disagree

If you're 75 or older, have no history of heart attack or stroke, and have been taking a statin for years just to prevent one, two randomized trials have now tested whether you can stop. The first, smaller one found stopping looked safe. The second, larger one — the trial everyone was waiting for — found a result whose own confidence interval leans toward staying on it, even though it technically passed the bar its designers set for calling it "fine to stop." This isn't settled. Read both numbers before you or your clinician decide anything. Nothing here is medical advice, no dose or drug is recommended, and nothing here applies to anyone with existing heart disease, anyone under 75, or the separate question of whether to start a statin.

Why this question had no answer at all until recently

Statins are one of the most-studied drug classes in medicine — but almost entirely in people who started them in middle age. Guidelines have long suggested caution about continuing them into advanced old age, but that caution was built on subgroup analyses of trials that mostly excluded people over 75, not on a trial that actually randomized the decision. As of mid-2025, a literature search found no randomized trial anywhere that tested stopping versus continuing a statin in an adult 75 or older with no known cardiovascular disease. Two trials have now tried to close that gap, and they don't agree.

Trial one: stopping looked safe, with a big asterisk

SAGA/SITE randomized 1,160 French adults 75 and older (median age 80), all on a statin for at least a year (most for over a decade), to keep taking it or stop. Over three years, 7.9% of the continuing group died versus 7.2% of the group that stopped — a difference of -0.68 percentage points, with a confidence interval (-3.95 to +2.60) that comfortably includes zero. Read plainly: this trial could not detect a difference either way. The trial's own designers called this "non-inferior" against a pre-specified 5-percentage-point margin — but they also wrote, in their own limitations section, that the margin they chose "is not acceptable relative to the observed mortality" once you see how low the actual death rate turned out to be. A 5-point margin against a 7.6% observed rate would tolerate stopping causing roughly a two-thirds relative increase in deaths and still call it "non-inferior." Every secondary cardiovascular outcome — heart attack, stroke, major events — ran numerically worse for the group that stopped, though none reached statistical significance either. LDL cholesterol rose by about 56 mg/dL within three months of stopping and stayed there, with no corresponding rise in deaths over three years — a real biomarker change that didn't translate into a detected mortality signal, at least not yet and not at this sample size.

Trial two: the one everyone was waiting for, and its numbers point the other way

STREAM, an international trial, randomized 1,880 adults 70 and older — nearly double SAGA/SITE's size, and requiring at least two chronic conditions rather than just age — to discontinue (938 people) or continue (942 people) statin therapy. Its primary outcome combined heart attack, stroke, and death from any cause, analyzed with a statistical method called restricted mean survival time: essentially, how much extra healthy, event-free time each group averaged over two years.

The discontinuation group averaged 2.0 fewer weeks of event-free time, with a confidence interval of -3.7 to -0.3 weeks. The trial's designers had pre-specified that anything smaller than a 5.2-week gap would count as "non-inferior" — and 2.0 weeks is comfortably inside that bound, so the trial is reported as a non-inferiority success.

Here's the part that matters more than the word "non-inferior." Unlike SAGA/SITE's interval, STREAM's confidence interval for this difference does not include zero — it's a statistically real difference, and it favors staying on the statin. The raw numbers behind it: over a median two years, 12.7% of the group that stopped had a heart attack, stroke, or died, versus 9.2% of the group that continued — about 3.5 more events per 100 people. Serious adverse events were also numerically more common after stopping (43.0% vs. 39.8%). None of this is hidden in the paper; the authors report the raw numbers alongside the "non-inferior" framing. But a real, direction-consistent difference that happens to be smaller than a pre-chosen acceptability line is a different, less reassuring result than SAGA/SITE's "we couldn't tell either way."

Why this vault calls the question open, not closed

Put the two trials side by side: the trial that first tested this question found a null. The larger, more diverse trial that tested it next found a real, if modest, signal pointing toward staying on the drug — big enough to show up as statistically significant, small enough to clear its own non-inferiority bar. That is precisely the kind of tension that keeps a question open rather than resolved. Three larger trials were named from the start as the ones that would move this: STREAM has now reported; STAREE and PREVENTABLE remain the other two, and STAREE (which tests starting a statin in people not already on one — a different question) found a clear cardiovascular benefit from starting, with no change in disability-free survival.

What the observational literature said, and why it doesn't settle this either. Three large cohort studies, covering over 200,000 people, had reported roughly 30% higher cardiovascular risk after stopping a statin. Neither randomized trial reproduced an effect anywhere near that size. The likely explanation is that these cohorts never recorded why someone stopped — and people often stop a medication because they're declining, not the other way around. Randomization removes that bias; the honest reading is that the observational signal was probably inflated by it.

Does stopping actually make you feel better?

One stated reason to stop a statin in old age is to reduce pill burden and side effects. SAGA/SITE tested this directly and found it didn't deliver: adverse events were essentially identical whether people stopped or continued (73.1% vs. 74.0%), muscle-related symptoms were nearly identical (5.7% vs. 5.1%), and quality of life didn't differ between groups over three years. In a population that had already tolerated the drug for a decade on average, there wasn't much burden left to remove.

Which statin, and how much, is a separate (and clearer) question

Apart from the stop-or-continue question, a large network analysis of 29 trials (218,657 people) asked a different question: among people who haven't had a cardiovascular event, which lipid-lowering approach works best? The honest answer is that benefit tracks how much you lower LDL, not which brand does it. A high-intensity statin beat a moderate one; adding a PCSK9-inhibiting drug (evolocumab) to a statin added further benefit; but adding fish-oil-derived EPA or a fibrate to a statin did not show added benefit in this analysis. Bempedoic acid, an alternative for people who can't tolerate statins, performed well here too, in a source with no disclosed funding. None of this bears on whether to stop an already-established statin — it's a drug-choice question, not the one this article opened with.

So what's the honest read?

If you're 75 or older, have no heart disease, and have been on a statin for years: the evidence does not currently give a clean answer. The first trial to test stopping found no detectable difference. The second, larger, more recent trial found a small but real signal favoring staying on it, even while officially clearing its own "acceptable" bar. Both the National Lipid Association and the American Geriatrics Society point toward a conversation with your clinician rather than a blanket rule, and that is still the right instinct — now with a second data point that leans, gently, toward not stopping without that conversation. Nothing here is a recommendation to start, stop, or change a dose or a drug.
