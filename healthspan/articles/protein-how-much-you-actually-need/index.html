How much protein do you actually need? What the evidence supports

If you lift and you're trying to build or keep muscle, the honest number the research points to is a target of roughly 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day — and the more important half of that sentence is that it's a ceiling worth hitting, not a floor to keep climbing above. Past about 1.6 g/kg/day, the trials show no further muscle payoff. So the goal is to reach a sensible target, not to chase ever-higher intake. Here's what the evidence says, at what strength, and the two ways people usually get this wrong.

Not medical advice. This summarizes published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician or dietitian; it doesn't prescribe a personal target.

What the evidence shows

The anchoring study is a meta-analysis with meta-regression of 49 randomized controlled trials (1,863 participants) of protein supplementation during resistance training (PMID: 28698222). Two findings matter:

  • A real but modest benefit. Adding protein to a resistance-training program improved strength by +2.49 kg on one-rep-max and added +0.30 kg of fat-free mass beyond training alone. Evidence tier: meta-analysis of RCTs — top of the hierarchy — with a consistent direction.
  • A clear plateau. The benefit flattened at about 1.62 g/kg/day of total daily protein. Above that, more protein produced no additional muscle or strength. That's where the "~1.6 g/kg/day" target comes from — it's the point of diminishing returns, drawn directly from the data.

To make it concrete: for a 165 lb (75 kg) adult, ~1.6 g/kg/day is about 120 g of protein a day. That's a total-diet figure — food first, with a shake only if it's the easy way to close the gap, not a requirement.

The two ways people get this wrong

  • Undershooting. Plenty of people who train — especially those eating in a compressed window or cutting calories — quietly land well under the target and leave the strength/lean-mass benefit on the table. The fix is deliberate, not dramatic: distribute protein across meals until you reliably reach the target.
  • Overshooting. The gym-culture default of "1 gram per pound" is about 2.2 g/kg/day — well past the plateau. There's no evidence it builds more muscle than 1.6, so the extra is effort and money for no added return. (Very high intakes are a separate clinician conversation for anyone with kidney concerns.)

Does when and how often matter? Mostly no.

A popular follow-on rule says protein only "counts" if you spread it out — roughly 0.4 g/kg per meal across four or more meals — because muscle can supposedly only use ~20–25 g in one sitting and wastes the rest. That per-meal ceiling is a reasonable-sounding idea (Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018, PMID: 29497353), but it's a mechanistic proposal whose own authors called the practical threshold "speculative," and the newer data don't support the strong version.

A tightly-controlled tracer study fed 100 g of protein in a single sitting after training and found it produced a larger and longer-lasting (>12 hour) muscle-building response than 25 g, with almost none of the excess burned off — so there's no hard per-meal ceiling; a big dose is used, not wasted (Trommelen et al. 2023). The honest catch: that study measured muscle protein synthesis, a short-term lab marker, not muscle actually built over months. And on the outcome that matters, a 2025 meta-analysis of RCTs found protein timing and distribution didn't change lean mass or strength (essentially zero effect — SMD −0.08 for lean mass), though from a small pool of five trials (Casuso & Goossens 2025). Evidence tier: meta-analysis of RCTs — the top of the hierarchy.

So hit your daily total and split it however is easiest — that's a preference, not a lever. If it's simpler to get the ~120 g above into two big meals than four small ones, the evidence says that's fine. (The one unsettled thread: a lone, unreplicated hint that protein before training may slightly help leg-press strength — worth watching, not worth reorganizing your day around.)

Two 2026 studies have since tested this harder, from opposite ends, and both landed in the same place.

The first pooled 7 trials of 305 adults aged 60 and over, asking whether front-loading protein at breakfast helps (Ikeda et al. 2026). Every muscle outcome came back flat — lean mass, grip strength, leg strength. This is the population where timing should matter most, because older muscle responds less readily to a given dose of protein, so a null here is worth more than a null in twenty-year-olds. Its more interesting result wasn't the headline: the extra breakfast protein displaced other food, cutting total daily energy intake — significantly so in the trials with no exercise component. Protein is the most filling macronutrient, and this is a reminder that it cuts both ways. Add 20 g at breakfast and people eat less of everything afterward, plausibly including protein.

The second is the cleanest test yet, because of one detail in its diet records. Twenty-nine resistance-training men were randomised, double-blind, to drink either carbohydrate plus protein or matched carbohydrate alone during eight weeks of supervised training, with muscle biopsies before and after (Svensson et al. 2026). Nothing separated the groups — not fibre size, not lean mass, not strength. And here is why that null is informative rather than empty: weighed food records showed both groups were already eating 1.65 and 1.81 g/kg/day. Both were sitting on the flat part of the curve above, where the evidence says there is no more benefit to buy. There was nothing left to detect.

That cuts the other way too, and the authors say so: this tells you nothing about someone whose intake is inadequate, which is the one case where a workout protein drink might genuinely matter. The null applies to people already hitting the target — which, if you're reading this article, is the plan.

Does the type of protein matter? Mostly no — and be wary of anything that says otherwise

If timing is the first place people over-optimize, the protein source is the second — and the marketing is louder here. The honest answer is that once you're hitting your daily total from reasonable-quality protein, the specific supplement is close to noise, and the more emphatic a study is that its favorite powder is special, the harder you should look at how it got there.

A 2026 network meta-analysis pooled 78 randomized trials (4,755 resistance-training adults) comparing 13 protein supplements (Drummond et al. 2026). Read carefully, it says two things. First, the credible effects are small: whey — the most-studied option — beat placebo by about +0.16 standard deviations for fat-free mass and +0.15 for strength, and no protein source reliably beat another on direct head-to-head evidence. That's the same "hit the total, the details are minor" message as the timing data. Second, its headline — that collagen ranked above whey, with an implied ~3.2 kg of fat-free mass in 8–16 weeks — is almost certainly an artifact, not a discovery. Exactly one trial directly compared collagen with whey; the rest of collagen's top ranking came from indirect statistical inference, which the analysis's own confidence ratings mark down. And the number is biologically implausible: collagen is low in the branched-chain amino acids (especially leucine) that trigger muscle building, so a supplement worse suited to the job topping the chart is a red flag about the chart, not a breakthrough about collagen. The honest verdict: collagen is unproven for building muscle, not a proven upgrade — and mechanistically unlikely to beat whey.

The same caution applies to the probiotic now added to many protein powders, and this one has now been checked three separate ways. A 2026 Bayesian meta-analysis of 20 trials (Chang et al. 2026) found that when you isolate the probiotic's added value over protein alone, it disappears — nine of ten comparisons showed no benefit. A closer read of that literature traced its apparent wins to what they were compared against rather than to the bacteria.

And in 2026 someone finally ran the trial that settles the design question, if not the biology. Fifty-one men aged 65–79 were randomised, triple-blind, into three groups (Bastos et al. 2026). Every group did the same twelve weeks of supervised lifting, and every group was coached by dietitians to the same protein intake. One group then got 20 g of whey after each session; one got the whey plus a four-strain synbiotic capsule daily; one got flavoured water. The synbiotic group gained nothing the whey group did not. No extra strength, no extra function, no change in blood sugar. The authors' own conclusion is that routine synbiotic use "cannot be recommended" on these findings. Evidence tier: randomized controlled trial, and a well-run one — the people analysing the data didn't know which group was which.

Two honest limits. Only 14 people were in the synbiotic group, so a modest benefit could have been missed; this is a failure to find a benefit, not proof there isn't one. And nobody sampled anyone's gut, so we don't know whether the bacteria arrived. But three different study designs have now looked for the add-on's value and none of them found it. The probiotic in your protein powder is, on current evidence, selling you the protein.

Collagen resurfaced again in 2026, this time on a bone claim — a meta-analysis of 20 studies in mostly postmenopausal women with osteopenia or osteoporosis reported gains in bone mineral density (Sun et al. 2025). It's worth naming why we don't bank it, because the reasoning transfers. The reported density effects run up past 2 standard deviations with 80% heterogeneity between studies — and an enormous average effect that studies wildly disagree about is the statistical signature of a few small trials pulling the number around, not of a real change in bone. The population is also women with diagnosed bone disease, and the best-looking subgroup also took calcium and vitamin D, so you can't tell which ingredient did anything. That's the second time collagen has topped a chart on evidence that doesn't hold up on inspection.

There's a broader pattern here worth learning, because it will save you money on the next supplement ranking you read. A 2026 network meta-analysis of 35 trials in trained athletes (Wang et al. 2026) produced a suspiciously neat result: protein won endurance, creatine won strength, omega-3 won recovery, and nothing placed second anywhere. Thirty-five trials split across three supplements and three outcomes is about four trials per combination — and the tidy pattern is mostly a map of what got studied, not of what works. Creatine trials tend to measure strength; protein trials tend to measure endurance. To its credit, the paper's own certainty table says as much: the head-to-head rankings are rated low confidence, while the plain comparisons against placebo are rated moderate. The useful rule, in that paper and generally: trust what a study says versus a placebo, distrust the leaderboard it builds on top.

None of this changes the plan: pick a third-party-tested protein you'll actually use, hit your daily total, and ignore the source-of-the-month.

The question underneath all of this: what does the extra actually add?

If you read the last two sections and felt like the answer kept being "not much," there's a reason, and it's worth naming because it will help you read the next protein study you see.

Almost every trial that finds a benefit from adding protein — or from a source, or a supplement, or a schedule — also changed something else at the same time. A 2026 review of 15 trials in 1,161 women set out to compare animal against plant protein and ended up publishing the problem in its own conclusion: "when analyzed independently of energy restriction and physical exercise, the effects of consuming both proteins on body composition were less consistent, making it impossible to confirm benefits from an isolated increase in protein intake" (Fialho et al. 2026). The trials that worked also cut calories or added training. You can't tell which ingredient did the work when three things changed at once.

Two 2026 trials did it properly, in opposite directions, and between them they draw a fairly clear line.

The first found something. A hundred postmenopausal women were randomised into three groups (Yang et al. 2026): Nordic walking plus a protein target plus a 10-hour eating window, the identical walking and eating window without the protein target, or their usual routine. Because both active groups did the same walking inside the same window, the protein prescription is the only thing that differs. Both beat the control group on grip strength, walking speed and getting out of a chair — and the protein group beat the walking-only group on all three, separating most clearly on lean mass. That's a real, cleanly-designed result, and it's the best evidence in this article that hitting a target does something you wouldn't get from the training alone.

The catch worth knowing: the researchers collected everyone's food diaries and never compared total calories between the groups. So "coached to eat more protein" and "ate more, full stop" aren't separated, and lean mass was measured by a bioelectrical-impedance device — which infers muscle from body water — in a trial that had two of its three groups fasting fourteen hours a day. Take the direction; don't bank the size of it.

The second found nothing, and it's the more useful design. In the Bastos trial above, the control group wasn't untrained and underfed — it was lifting and eating to a protein target with a dietitian checking. Against that comparator, adding 20 g of whey improved strength on the leg machine that tested the muscles they'd been training, and not on grip strength, which didn't move in anybody. And all three groups — including flavoured water — improved the same amount on a standard physical-function test. That is what a modest, local training effect looks like, and it's a good deal smaller than "protein makes you stronger" usually sounds.

One caveat that cuts in the other direction: that trial's protein target was about 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day, the standard recommendation for adults over 65 — noticeably below the ~1.6 g/kg/day figure this article is built on. So the whey was being added on top of a lower floor than the target above, not on top of it.

The practical version: the daily total is the lever, the training is the engine, and nearly everything sold as an upgrade to either has failed the test that isolates it. That's not a reason to skip protein. It's a reason to stop paying for the things stacked on top of it.

Two honest caveats

  • It's additive to training, not a substitute for it. Every trial paired protein with resistance training. Protein without the training stimulus is not what this evidence shows, and the benefit shrinks with age — older adults tend to need to be more deliberate about hitting the target, not less. A 2026 network meta-analysis put a sharper edge on this. In 24 trials of 1,298 older adults with both low muscle and excess fat, researchers ranked nine strategies across body composition and strength (Yu et al. 2026). Resistance training came out on top for grip strength and for fat loss. No nutritional strategy — supplements or high-protein intake — beat usual care on any of the five outcomes, including in the arms where nutrition was added on top of training. Two honest caveats keep that from being a verdict on protein: those trials ran only 3–9 months, which is likely too short for muscle to respond in older adults, and the nutrition arms were thinly studied. So read it as a boundary rather than a reversal — the daily-total target above is about supporting training in people who train, and supplementation has not been shown to work as a standalone treatment in its own right. A second 2026 meta-analysis, of 41 trials in 3,141 adults over 60, pointed the same way on a different measure: strength per unit of muscle (Ohta et al. 2026). Across seven kinds of intervention, only resistance exercise improved it (a moderate effect); nutrition alone, and nutrition added on top of exercise, were both flat. The honest caveat is that the paper published no confidence intervals or trial counts for any of its null categories, so "didn't work" and "wasn't measured well enough to tell" can't be distinguished — the authors themselves say the nutrition results "remain inconclusive."
  • Losing weight changes the question, and the target above was not derived for that. A 2026 randomised trial followed 114 adults aged 65–85 with obesity through six months of a supervised programme: a 500–750 calorie daily deficit, protein prescribed at about 1 g/kg/day, and thirty minutes of progressive resistance training three times a week, with calcium and vitamin D supplied to everyone (Nava et al. 2026). The programme worked — 19% more aerobic fitness, 50 more metres of walking distance, 12% better physical function. But lean mass still fell about 2 kg, thigh muscle volume measured by MRI fell about 4%, and hip bone density fell measurably too — roughly a fifth to a quarter of everything lost was lean tissue, with the lifting and with the protein. Read this as a boundary, not a dosing instruction: the trial didn't randomise protein intake and doesn't tell you what number would have been enough. What it does say is that a sustained calorie deficit taxes muscle and bone even when you're doing the two things that are supposed to protect them, and that the ~1.6 g/kg/day figure above came from studies of people who were not dieting. If you're planning a deficit — by diet or by drug — that's a conversation worth having with your clinician before you start, not after.
  • The vault leans on one anchoring meta-analysis, and it carries dairy-industry funding. The direction (protein helps, with a plateau) is robust across the wider literature, but honesty means capping certainty on the exact magnitude and plateau point rather than treating 1.62 as a precise law.

Where it fits

Protein is one leg of a single muscle-building stack, not a standalone lever. It amplifies the same resistance-training adaptation that creatine does — so think of resistance training as the foundation, and protein and creatine together as its two amplifiers, not three independent wins. (The companion note here: creatine's benefit also plateaus — around 5 g/day — so both of these levers are "hit the target, then stop," not "more is better.")

A couple of interactions worth knowing: an intermittent-fasting eating window can crowd out the target, which is manageable by placing protein inside the window; and during GLP-1 (e.g. semaglutide) weight loss, where appetite suppression and rapid loss threaten muscle, hitting protein alongside resistance training is the evidence-based way to preserve lean mass — a clinician conversation, since GLP-1s are prescription.

So — what should you do?

If you train, aim to reach roughly 1.6 g/kg/day of total protein and stop worrying past that; the muscle benefit is real, modest, and capped. If you don't train, protein alone isn't a muscle strategy. This article deliberately doesn't set your personal number — bodyweight, kidney health, and goals make that a conversation for you and your clinician or dietitian.
