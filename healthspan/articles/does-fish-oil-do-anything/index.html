Does fish oil do anything? What the evidence supports on cognition, heart disease, muscle and bleeding

Short answer: for a healthy adult, mostly no — and the one place it clearly does something, you probably do not qualify for.

That is a blunt opening for a supplement taken by tens of millions of people, so here is the honest split before any of the detail:

  • Memory and thinking: no. This is now settled at the highest tier available. Pooled randomised trials say nothing, a trial designed to prove the fish oil was reaching the brain proved it and still found nothing, and a three-year placebo-controlled prevention trial in exactly the people who should have benefited found a difference of one hundredth of a standard deviation.
  • Heart attacks and cardiovascular death: yes, but only if you already have heart disease or are at high risk — and even then it comes with a real arrhythmia harm attached.
  • Muscle, strength and recovery: no. The training does the work.
  • Bleeding: reassuringly ordinary at normal doses, genuinely dose-dependent at high ones.
  • "Biological age" clocks: the needle moves a little, and nobody has shown that moving it matters.

Everything below names its evidence tier, because on this topic the tier is doing most of the work.

The cognition question is closed, and it took three different kinds of trial to close it

If you take fish oil because you have heard it is good for your brain, this is the section that matters.

Three independent designs now agree, and — this is the part that makes it convincing — they each fail in a different way, so they cannot all be wrong for the same reason.

1. Pooled randomised trials. A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 placebo-controlled randomised trials in cognitively unimpaired older adults found a standardised mean difference on global cognition of −0.02, 95% confidence interval −0.07 to 0.04. A standardised mean difference expresses an effect in units of the spread of the data; −0.02 is two hundredths of that spread, and the interval is narrow enough to rule out anything a person would notice.

2. The excuse got tested. For years the standing defence of that null was a delivery story: the dose was too low, the participants already had enough, or people carrying the APOE ε4 gene variant cannot transport DHA into the brain. Yassine 2026 was built to check. A registered, double-blind, 24-month randomised trial gave 2 g/day of DHA to 365 non-demented adults aged 55–80 who were specifically recruited for low dietary DHA intake, and measured cerebrospinal fluid — the fluid around the brain and spinal cord — to see whether the fat actually arrived. It arrived, decisively, and it arrived regardless of ε4 status (difference in the CSF DHA/AA ratio of 0.19, 0.16 to 0.21, p < 0.0001). Two years later there were no differences in cognition and no differences in brain volumes.

3. The long prevention trial. The last shape missing was a big, long, placebo-controlled trial in the population that should benefit most. That trial exists, and this vault only read it properly this week. MAPT — the Multidomain Alzheimer Preventive Trial (Andrieu 2017, Lancet Neurology, PMID 28359749) — randomised 1,680 adults aged 70 or older at 13 memory centres in France and Monaco, all of whom had either reported a memory complaint to their doctor, lost a daily-living function, or walked slowly. It ran for three years. The fish-oil arm got 800 mg DHA + 225 mg EPA daily. The result, on a pre-specified composite of four cognitive tests, in the 1,525 people analysed:

0.011 standard deviations better than placebo, 95% confidence interval −0.081 to 0.103, p = 0.812.

Read the interval rather than the p value. A p value of 0.812 only tells you the result is compatible with chance. The interval tells you something more useful: whatever fish oil does to cognition over three years, this trial has bounded it between "slightly worse" and "slightly better," and both ends are too small to matter. That is a demonstration of absence, not a failure to look. The distinction is worth insisting on, because most nulls in nutrition research are the second kind — a small study that simply could not have found anything — and this one is not.

One more detail from MAPT is worth the space, because it teaches you how to read every lifestyle trial you will ever see. MAPT had four arms: fish oil, a lifestyle program, both, and placebo. Participants were blinded to the capsule — they did not know whether they were getting fish oil or placebo — but obviously could not be blinded to the lifestyle program, which involved 43 group sessions. Blinding matters because people who know they are being treated tend to report and perform slightly better; that is the whole reason placebos exist. Now line up the three results:

ArmCould the participant tell?Result vs placebo
Lifestyle program + fish oilYes (program visible)+0.093
Lifestyle program + placeboYes (program visible)+0.079

The two arms people could see themselves receiving drifted slightly positive. The one arm nobody could see did nothing at all. None of the three reached significance. But the ordering tracks visibility, not the amount of treatment — which is a good reason to discount small positive results from any trial where participants know what they are getting, and a good reason to trust the fish-oil number here more than the other two.

Heart disease: a real benefit, in people who already have it

This is the one place fish oil has earned something, and the boundary is sharp.

A 2025 meta-analysis of 42 randomised trials covering 176,253 participants found that omega-3 significantly reduced cardiovascular mortality, cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, fatal myocardial infarction and revascularisation — in people with established cardiovascular disease or at high cardiovascular risk. That is secondary prevention: reducing the next event in someone who has already had one, or is close to it.

Two things travel with that benefit and neither is optional reading.

It significantly increased atrial fibrillation — an irregular heart rhythm that itself raises stroke risk — and gastrointestinal side effects. A subgroup signal favoured EPA on its own over EPA+DHA combinations.

And the healthy-adult case is a different question that this evidence does not answer. If you have no cardiovascular disease and no elevated risk, the trials above were not run on you. The honest position is that primary prevention in healthy adults remains unestablished — not disproven, unestablished.

Muscle, strength and recovery: the training does the work

A meta-analysis of omega-3 added to resistance training in older adults came back essentially null on muscle outcomes, with the one apparently positive result (chair-rise time) traceable to a single outlying study. A network meta-analysis in athletes produced a recovery signal that lived entirely on surrogate markers — blood measures of muscle damage and inflammation — rather than on anything an athlete could feel or do.

Plain version: if you lift, the lifting is what changes your muscle. The capsule is not adding to it in any way these trials could detect.

Bleeding: normal doses look fine, high doses do not

This one is more reassuring than its reputation, with one specific exception.

Across 11 randomised trials covering 120,643 patients, omega-3 produced no overall increase in bleeding: rate ratio 1.09, 95% confidence interval 0.91 to 1.31 — 2.6% versus 2.4%, an absolute difference of 0.07 percentage points. Haemorrhagic stroke, intracranial bleeding and gastrointestinal bleeding were each individually non-significant.

The exception is dose, not drug interaction. High-dose purified EPA — icosapent ethyl, a prescription product, in the REDUCE-IT and JELIS trials — raised bleeding rate ratio 1.50 (1.13 to 1.99). In absolute terms that is +0.6 percentage points (0.1 to 1.0), or roughly one extra bleeding event for every 166 people treated. Risk scaled with dose at 0.24 per gram of EPA.

⚠ What is neither shown nor ruled out is the specific interaction most people worry about: fish oil on top of aspirin or another antiplatelet drug. The available analysis is an indirect comparison between trials rather than a test within individuals, and it landed at p = 0.056. That is not evidence of safety. If you take an antiplatelet or anticoagulant, this is a conversation with your doctor, not a question this article can close.

"Biological age" clocks: the least useful section, and the reason it exists

A post-hoc analysis of the DO-HEALTH trial found omega-3 slowing three DNA-methylation "ageing clocks" by roughly 2.9 to 3.8 months over three years. A separate randomised sub-study of the VITAL trial found no effect on telomere length.

Before anyone finds that encouraging, here is what a clock result is worth. An epigenetic clock is a blood test that estimates "biological age" from DNA methylation patterns. For it to be a legitimate stand-in for health, three things must be true: it must predict who gets sick, it must respond when you intervene, and — the one that matters — changing it must change what happens to you. The first two have been tested and hold weakly. The third has never been demonstrated for any epigenetic clock.

There is a cautionary precedent worth knowing, from a completely different biomarker. Elevated homocysteine is a well-replicated predictor of cardiovascular disease, and B vitamins reliably lower it — the first two legs, both passed emphatically. Across 15 randomised trials and 71,422 participants, lowering it prevented neither heart attacks (risk ratio 1.02) nor death (risk ratio 1.01), at the highest evidence grade available, with a formal sequential analysis concluding that further trials would not change the answer (Martí-Carvajal 2017, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews). A marker can predict, respond, and still be worthless as a target. Treat a fish-oil clock result the same way until someone shows otherwise.

So should you take it?

If you are healthy, under 60, and taking fish oil for your brain, your muscles or general longevity: the evidence does not support it. The cognition case is closed at three independent tiers. The muscle case is null. The primary-prevention cardiovascular case has not been made.

If you have established cardiovascular disease or high cardiovascular risk: there is a genuine, replicated, high-tier benefit here — and an atrial-fibrillation harm attached to it. That is a real trade-off with a real magnitude on both sides, and it belongs in a conversation with your clinician, not in a decision made off a supplement label.

If you take an antiplatelet or anticoagulant, or you are considering a high-dose prescription EPA product: the dose-dependent bleeding signal is real and quantified above. Same conversation.

And if you eat oily fish: none of this is an argument against that. Every trial above tested a capsule against a placebo capsule. Whole fish carries a different package, is studied differently, and is not what failed here.
