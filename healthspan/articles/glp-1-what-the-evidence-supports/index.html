GLP-1 drugs: what the evidence supports beyond the weight loss

That semaglutide and tirzepatide drive real weight loss isn't in question. The more useful 2026 question, if you're weighing one, is what else they do. The honest answer now has four parts, and only the first is the one you usually hear. In people who already have heart disease, semaglutide cut major cardiac events by about 20%, which is a genuinely important result. Across 262 trials, no obesity drug convincingly improved quality of life — and a head-to-head trial published since, whose title says the opposite, quietly agrees. The muscle you lose has been measured: about 5 kg on semaglutide. And the appetite suppression has finally been measured too, at roughly 270 calories cut from a single unlimited meal, which is what makes the protein problem real rather than theoretical. A rare eye complication has also joined the file. Here is the evidence, graded, and what follows from it.

Not medical advice, and this one matters more than most. GLP-1 drugs are prescription medications. This summarizes published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician. It never tells you to start, stop, or change a dose, and it sets no dose of its own.

The part that's settled: weight, and the heart

The weight effect is well established and isn't the point of this piece. What lifts these drugs from "weight-loss aid" to "cardiovascular drug" is a hard-outcome randomized trial.

The SELECT trial randomized 17,604 adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease but no diabetes to weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg or placebo, then followed them a mean of 34 months. The primary endpoint, meaning cardiovascular death or non-fatal heart attack or stroke, fell from 8.0% to 6.5%. That is a hazard ratio of 0.80 (95% CI 0.72 to 0.90, P<0.001), or roughly 67 people treated for three years to prevent one event (Lincoff et al. 2023). Evidence tier: a single large randomized controlled trial on a hard endpoint. That is the strongest kind of evidence there is, and it is why this drug class is now taken seriously for the heart and not just the scale.

Two edges keep it from being a miracle headline.

The first is that this was secondary prevention. Everyone in SELECT already had cardiovascular disease. The heart benefit is proven in people who have already had an event, not in a metabolically healthier adult taking the drug mainly for weight. No trial has tested a GLP-1 for first-event prevention in otherwise healthy people. That is a real gap, and a 2026 network meta-analysis of 262 trials did not close it: its mortality estimate comes from the same high-risk trials, so it is a more precise reading of SELECT rather than independent confirmation of it.

The second is that the "lives saved" claim is softer than it looks. SELECT tested its secondary endpoints in a fixed order, and the chain broke at cardiovascular death (HR 0.85, P=0.07). By the trial's own rules, all-cause mortality, numerically lower at 4.3% versus 5.2%, could not be formally declared significant. The randomized truth is a solid reduction in cardiac events with a mortality signal that is encouraging but unconfirmed.

The finding nobody markets: it does not make you feel better

This is new since the last version of this article, and for anyone optimizing for healthspan rather than lifespan, it may be the most decision-relevant number in the whole file.

A BMJ network meta-analysis pooled 262 randomized trials covering 99,791 participants across 19 different obesity drugs. Within it, 43 trials and 45,663 people had quality of life measured. No drug convincingly improved it. Every mean difference came in under 5 points against a minimally important difference of 10, meaning the changes were smaller than the threshold at which a person would be expected to notice (Nong et al. 2026). Evidence tier: a network meta-analysis of randomized trials, independently funded and GRADE-rated, with the pharma-conflicted authors deliberately kept out of data extraction and risk-of-bias scoring. This is about as good as evidence synthesis gets, and the result is a null.

Sit with that for a second, because it is easy to skate past. The drug reliably moves the scale. In people who already have heart disease, it prevents heart attacks. But on how life is actually experienced, measured across forty-five thousand people, it shows nothing.

That is not an argument against taking one. It is an argument for being precise about which promises have actually been tested. If the reason you want the drug is a number on a scale or a cardiovascular risk you already carry, the evidence supports you. If the reason is that you expect to feel better, the trials have looked for that and have not found it.

The honest limits of the null cut both ways. Generic quality-of-life instruments are blunt tools. The trials ran 12 to 172 weeks, which is long enough to say "no measured benefit" and far too short to say anything about decades. And a person whose weight is genuinely disabling may well experience relief that a population average cannot see.

The head-to-head that reads like a rebuttal and isn't

Since the last version of this article, a trial arrived with the title "Improved health-related quality of life with tirzepatide versus semaglutide." It sounds like the null just got overturned. Read the numbers and it does the opposite.

SURMOUNT-5 randomized roughly 750 adults with obesity or overweight and without diabetes to tirzepatide or semaglutide, each at the maximum dose they could tolerate, and measured quality of life at week 72 (Shukla et al. 2026). Evidence tier: a prespecified analysis inside a randomized trial — but a drug-versus-drug one, funded by Eli Lilly, with several authors employed by Lilly and holding Lilly equity.

Two structural facts decide what it can show. There is no placebo arm. Both drugs were active, so every "improved from baseline" result absorbs regression to the mean, seventy-two weeks of trial attention, and the expectations of people who could watch the scale move. The randomization licenses exactly one comparison — the gap between the two drugs — and that gap is 1.25 points of self-rated general health, on a scale calibrated so that a standard deviation is 10 points. Second, the analysis is on-treatment rather than intention-to-treat: it counts only people still taking the drug. Since GLP-1s are stopped mostly because of nausea and gastrointestinal symptoms, and since nausea is quality of life, the people the drug made feel worst are missing from the denominator by construction.

And the most trustworthy result in the paper is the one nobody put in the title. After seventy-two weeks of the most effective weight-loss drugs ever made, the mental-health half of the quality-of-life score did not move in either arm. Sponsor funding, the absent placebo arm and on-treatment selection would all have pushed that number up. A null that survives three biases pointing the other way is worth more than a positive that rides them.

So the picture holds: the scale moves, the heart risk moves in the right people, and how life feels does not.

The mechanism, finally measured: about 270 calories off a single meal

For most of this article's life it carried a warning with nothing behind it: appetite suppression can crowd out the protein you need. Plausible, and unmeasured. It has now been measured.

Pooling placebo-controlled trials that sat people down in front of an unlimited laboratory meal and weighed what they ate rather than asking them to remember it, GLP-1 treatment cut intake at that meal by 1,132 kilojoules (95% CI 815 to 1,449), which is about 270 calories (Quan et al. 2026). Evidence tier: meta-analysis of randomized and crossover trials, and the instrument is unusually good — food on a scale, against a placebo arm.

Two limits stop that number travelling further than it should, and they are severe. The pooled estimate rests on four arms from three trials, 209 people, out of sixteen studies the authors reviewed. And it is one meal, in a laboratory, on one day: the authors state plainly that real-world habitual intake was too poorly reported to pool at all. So this measures the drug's mechanism cleanly and says nothing directly about what you eat across a week.

Which is enough for the thing that matters here. The reason to care about appetite suppression is not the calories. It's the protein. A 270-calorie hole punched in a meal comes out of whatever was on the plate, and the countermeasure below depends on hitting a daily protein number that was already the hardest part. Quan's authors reach the same conclusion independently, writing that structured nutritional counseling emphasizing adequate protein should accompany the drug.

The catch that is now a number: you lose muscle

The previous version of this article said the muscle loss was real but that no trial this project had graded had pinned down how much, so it invented no number. Three studies later, the number exists.

A meta-analysis of 7 placebo-controlled randomized trials (821 patients) found GLP-1 treatment cost 1.74 kg of lean mass on average, and 5.44 kg in the semaglutide group specifically (Laverde et al. 2026). Evidence tier: meta-analysis of randomized trials, though with very high heterogeneity (I²=98%), so treat the kilograms as a direction rather than a forecast. The BMJ synthesis adds a gradient worth knowing: tirzepatide takes off the most fat (−25.7%) and also the most lean mass (−8.3%). The harder a drug pulls weight off, the more of what comes off is muscle.

Watch for the reassuring half of that literature. Lean mass rises as a proportion of body weight during treatment, by about 1.81%. That sounds like good news and isn't: it happens because fat falls faster than muscle does. It is a denominator effect, not muscle gained.

There is a tempting next step here, and this project deliberately does not take it. A large meta-analysis of 49 cohorts (1,149,807 people, 83,798 deaths) found that adults with low fat-free mass die sooner, with a relative risk of 1.42, falling to 1.31 after the authors corrected for publication bias they detected in their own data (Haas et al. 2026). It is tempting to chain that to the lean-mass numbers above and conclude that GLP-1 muscle loss will shorten your life. That chain does not hold. Those cohorts measured untreated adults, where low muscle mass is substantially a marker of undiagnosed illness and frailty rather than a cause of death, and the study cannot separate the two. A tell: the association is strongest when measured with the crudest instrument (a tape measure around the calf) and weakest when measured with a real body-composition scan, which is not how a genuine tissue effect behaves. And SELECT, which is randomized, found lower mortality on semaglutide despite the muscle loss.

What the muscle evidence does justify is this: the countermeasure matters more than it used to, and it matters most on the drugs that work best. Nobody has yet run the study that would settle it, because no trial has measured muscle strength, power, or physical function during GLP-1 treatment. Only mass. That gap is real and this article will not fill it with a guess.

The countermeasure itself is well evidenced and entirely in your hands:

  • Lift. Resistance training is the signal that tells your body to keep muscle while it sheds fat. In a cohort of 147,000 adults it tracked with 13 to 27% lower mortality, additive to cardio (resistance-training map). You do not have to train to failure to get the strength benefit, and stopping a rep or two short may even be slightly better for it.
  • Keep protein up as appetite drops. The evidence supports roughly 1.6 g/kg/day paired with training, which for a 165 lb (75 kg) adult is about 120 g/day, and hitting the daily total is what matters rather than how you time it (protein-target map). This is the hardest part on a drug that kills hunger, and the part most worth deliberate effort.
  • Creatine, if you lift. A small, reliable lean-mass and strength benefit at about 5 g/day, and one of the few supplements the evidence actually backs (creatine map).

None of that is exotic. It is the same boring muscle stack the evidence keeps pointing to. On a GLP-1 it stops being optional.

Tirzepatide now has its own heart evidence, and it is not the same evidence

Almost everything above is about semaglutide, because SELECT is the trial that exists. Tirzepatide's cardiovascular case had to be built a different way, and the way it was built is the interesting part.

A trial can only show a drug prevents heart attacks if it has something to compare against. Tirzepatide's own trial, SURPASS-CVOT, compared it against dulaglutide — another drug in the same family that already works — and returned non-inferiority without superiority, which means "not worse" and nothing more. No placebo-controlled heart-outcome trial of tirzepatide exists, and given the evidence for the class, one probably never will: you can no longer ethically give a placebo to people who would benefit.

So researchers used a workaround. They compared 52,971 US adults with type 2 diabetes and established heart disease who started either tirzepatide or sitagliptin — a drug proven in trials to be cardiovascular-neutral, meaning it neither helps nor hurts the heart. Sitagliptin is a placebo stand-in that everyone is nonetheless taking. Over one year, major cardiac events occurred in 2.9% on tirzepatide versus 4.4% on sitagliptin — a hazard ratio of 0.68 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.80), about 70 people treated for one year to prevent one event (Krüger et al. 2026). Heart attacks specifically fell (0.67, 0.52 to 0.87). Strokes did not clearly move (0.91, 0.64 to 1.28) — and that interval is wide enough to be uninformative rather than negative.

Evidence tier: an observational study, pre-registered with a posted protocol, using a careful design. That is a third independent line pointing the same way, on a second molecule, by a third method — after SELECT's randomized trial and a large database emulation. It is not a randomized result and should not be quoted as one.

One caution about that same paper, because it cuts against the drug's own case. It also reported all-cause death at a hazard ratio of 0.55 and infection-related death at 0.40 — that is, roughly halving death from infection in a single year. No plausible mechanism explains that, and it is larger than what SELECT achieved randomized over four years. The most likely explanation is that people prescribed a newer, more expensive drug are healthier in ways the statistics could not fully balance. When a study's incidental numbers are too good, that is a reason to discount its headline number too, not to celebrate the extras.

Blood pressure: the two drugs are not the same drug

This is the newest thing on the file, and it is the first place where semaglutide and tirzepatide point in different directions rather than differing by degree.

Pooling 32 randomized trials and 47,332 participants, tirzepatide reduced high-blood-pressure-related adverse events by 60% (risk ratio 0.40, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.60) and raised low-blood-pressure-related events about 2.5-fold (2.45, 1.35 to 4.45) — worse at higher doses (2.58, 1.38 to 4.81). Semaglutide moved neither: 0.81 (0.57 to 1.15) and 1.39 (0.81 to 2.36) (Chen et al. 2026).

Read the semaglutide numbers as "unknown," not as "safe." Those intervals are wide. The upper bound on semaglutide's low-blood-pressure estimate, 2.36, is nearly tirzepatide's whole effect. A non-significant result over an interval that wide means the study could not tell, not that there is nothing there.

Two things make this worth a paragraph anyway. First, the evidence tier is high — these are randomized trials, so the comparison is not distorted by sicker people getting the drug. Nearly every other harm on this file is observational. Second, the outcomes here are events a trial recorded and coded, not blood pressure actually measured with a cuff, so the direction is more trustworthy than the size.

And there is a practical corner worth naming. Exercise lowers blood pressure too, and not trivially: a network of 30 randomized trials in people with hypertension found combined aerobic-plus-resistance training lowering systolic pressure by 9.5 mmHg and even isometric handgrip training by 5.4 mmHg (Lv et al. 2026). A drug that raises low-blood-pressure events and exercise that lowers blood pressure push the same way, and nobody has studied them together — exercise trials do not record drug side effects, and drug trials do not prescribe exercise. The likely form of this is dizziness on standing up during or after a hard session. This is not a reason to train less. It is a specific question to raise with a clinician — especially if the drug in question is tirzepatide, and especially if you are getting back to a court sport in the summer. A rare eye risk, newly on the file, and not shown to be caused by the drug

This is new since the last version, and it needs stating carefully in both directions.

A study using US insurance claims emulated a trial: adults aged 18 to 65 with type 2 diabetes starting a GLP-1 were compared against people starting one of two other diabetes drug classes, with more than eighty characteristics balanced statistically between the groups. Over eighteen months, ischemic optic neuropathy — a sudden loss of blood supply to the optic nerve, which can cause permanent vision loss in one eye — occurred in 8.5 per 10,000 GLP-1 users versus 5.5 per 10,000 on an SGLT2 inhibitor, a difference of 3.0 per 10,000 (95% CI 0.4 to 5.7). Against a DPP-4 inhibitor the difference was 3.6 per 10,000 (1.1 to 6.1) (Reynolds et al. 2026). Evidence tier: an observational target-trial emulation, NIH-funded with no manufacturer involvement — the strongest form of observational design, and still observational.

Translate the size before reacting to it. Roughly 3,000 people would need to take the drug for eighteen months for one extra case. The events concentrated sharply: 85% of the 81 cases were in people over 50, and 70% were in men, with very little signal in women or in the under-50s. And the authors decline the causal claim themselves, naming the confounder that would explain it — body mass index, which is also the reason people are prescribed the drug in the first place. The outcome was identified from billing codes rather than eye exams, because specific codes for this condition did not exist.

Since that study, the signal has been pooled — and the pooling sharpens it in a way that matters. Eight observational cohorts covering 5.9 million people put the risk at roughly double: relative effect 1.93 (95% CI 1.22 to 3.08) (Khani et al. 2026). Two caveats travel with that number and both are large. It is a ratio with no baseline attached — this condition affects something like 2 to 10 people per 100,000 per year, and doubling a number that small leaves it small, which is why the per-10,000 figures above remain the useful ones. And the eight studies disagree with each other enormously (a heterogeneity statistic of 91%, meaning almost all the variation between them is more than chance).

The subgroup split is the part worth carrying into an appointment. The pooled risk is driven entirely by people with type 2 diabetes (1.84, 1.21 to 2.80). Among people who are overweight or obese without diabetes, it did not reach significance: 1.68, with an interval running from 0.72 to 3.91. That interval is compatible with a real reduction and with a near-quadrupling. It is not reassurance; it is an admission that nobody knows yet — and the obesity indication is exactly where the drug is expanding.

Both studies point the same way from different angles: the eye signal concentrates in people whose blood vessels were already damaged — older, male, diabetic, with existing eye or heart disease. That could be biology, or it could be that the disease is doing the damage and the drug is along for the ride. Nothing available can separate those two, and the authors of both papers say so.

So: a real signal, a small absolute risk, honest papers, and no demonstration that the drug caused it. It does not outweigh a randomized trial showing 67 people treated for three years prevents one heart attack or stroke. It is a thing to mention to a clinician, particularly if you are a man over 50, or diabetic, or have existing eye or cardiovascular disease. One thing does make it different from the tolerability side effects below: if it happens, it is permanent, and there is no treatment that restores the vision. A rare irreversible harm and a common reversible one do not trade off on the same scale.

Tolerability: real, and a common reason people quit

The drugs are not free of side effects, and SELECT quantifies it cleanly. About 1 in 6 participants stopped semaglutide for an adverse event (17% versus 8% on placebo), and gastrointestinal problems drove most of it, with 10% discontinuing for GI reasons versus 2% on placebo (Lincoff et al. 2023). Notably, serious adverse events were lower on the drug (33% versus 36%), because it prevented so many cardiac events. So this is a tolerability story rather than an organ-damage one. Across the wider drug class, discontinuation for adverse events runs at a relative risk of 1.9 to 4.2, and semaglutide sits at the milder end of that range.

So what's the honest read?

For someone with established cardiovascular disease and excess weight, semaglutide is one of the better evidenced things in this whole project. A randomized trial shows it prevents heart attacks and strokes on top of the weight loss, at a tolerability cost that is real but usually manageable.

If you are metabolically healthier and considering a GLP-1 mainly for weight or for "longevity," be honest about which claims travel with you. The weight loss travels. The proven heart benefit was measured in sicker people. The mortality piece is not formally nailed down even there. And the quality-of-life benefit, the thing that sounds like the whole point, has now been looked for in forty-five thousand people, then looked for again in a head-to-head trial that put the opposite claim in its title, and it was not found either time.

Whatever your reason, the muscle you will lose without lifting and eating enough protein is the one part of this the trials cannot do for you — and the appetite suppression that makes the protein hard is now a measured quantity rather than a caution.

Because this is a prescription drug, this article hands you no dose and no start-or-stop advice. That is a conversation for you and your clinician, including whether the cardiovascular evidence applies to your situation.
