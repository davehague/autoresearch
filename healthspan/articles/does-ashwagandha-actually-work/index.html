Does ashwagandha actually work? What the evidence supports — and the one pattern every thread repeats

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) has real evidence behind it on five separate outcomes — stress, exercise performance, mood, blood sugar, and general wellbeing in older adults. Every single one of those five threads lands at the same place: the direction is believable, and the size of the effect isn't. That's not a hedge — it's the one finding that keeps repeating across nine independent studies, five outcome families, and three different manufacturers' branded extracts.

Not medical advice. This summarizes published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician; it doesn't prescribe a dose, duration, or product.

The best evidence: a real hormone moves, but the symptom scores don't hold up as well

The strongest case for ashwagandha rests on a genuinely objective measurement — cortisol, the stress hormone you can draw from blood rather than ask someone to rate on a scale. A meta-analysis of 15 placebo-controlled trials (873 adults, roughly 125–600 mg/day for 30–90 days) found cortisol fell by 2.36 units on average, alongside drops in perceived stress and anxiety questionnaire scores — but no improvement in quality of life (Bachour 2025, BJPsych Open).

That finding has now been independently reproduced. A 2026 systematic review covering a different set of 14 ashwagandha trials pooled a cortisol reduction of 3.27 micrograms per deciliter — same direction, similar size, from researchers who weren't looking at the same data (Alyami 2026, JoVE). Two independently-run syntheses landing in the same place is about as good as corroboration gets without a single enormous trial — the honest read is that ashwagandha probably does lower cortisol by a real, modest amount.

The stress and anxiety symptom scores are a shakier story. The largest single mental-health dataset assembled (22 trials, roughly 1,391 adults) reported stress, anxiety, and depression all improving with a dose-response pattern — but its reported effect sizes are mathematically impossible: a six-standard- deviation separation between groups doesn't exist in questionnaire data (Alsanie 2026, Complementary Therapies in Medicine, PMID 41644067). That doesn't mean the direction is wrong — it confirms the same direction every other study found — but it means you can't trust the magnitude this particular paper reports. Risk-of-bias assessment across the broader saffron-and-ashwagandha literature backs up the caution: only 10 of 31 relevant trials scored low risk of bias, and the certainty of the evidence topped out at moderate (Alyami 2026).

A factorial trial just showed something new: stacking it with a sleep blend can erase the benefit

A 2026 trial randomized 103 healthy, physically active adults into four groups: a morning adaptogen blend (600 mg ashwagandha + 300 mg rhodiola rosea), an evening sleep blend (magnesium L-threonate + L-theanine + a small dose of apigenin), both together, or placebo (Mastrofini 2026, Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition). The morning blend alone lowered weekly perceived stress and improved both self-reported and wearable-measured sleep. The group taking both blends together did not show that same benefit — adding the evening supplement to a working morning one made the morning one's effect disappear.

This is worth taking seriously for anyone tempted to stack a "morning calm" supplement with an "evening sleep" one on the theory that more support can't hurt: in this trial, it did hurt, or at least erased the gain. Two caveats keep this from being the final word. First, ashwagandha was co-dosed with rhodiola in the same capsule, so the trial can't tell you which ingredient (or their combination) produced the morning-alone benefit. Second, the groups were small — about 25 people each — over just two weeks, so this is one data point, not a settled finding about stacking adaptogens in general.

Exercise performance: a real average, with an interval wide enough to include harm

A separate line of evidence looked at ashwagandha's effect on physical performance rather than mood. A three-level meta-analysis of 13 trials (599 people, mostly young adults and athletes) found an overall performance benefit (Hedges' g = 0.47) concentrated in aerobic endurance (g = 0.54) — with no declared conflicts of interest, a genuine strength given how much of this literature is industry-funded (Li 2026, Nutrients, PMID 42356302). But the paper's own 95% prediction interval — the range a new trial's result would be expected to fall in — runs from −0.40 to 1.33. That spans "makes you slightly worse" to "large benefit." The authors report this themselves, which is to their credit and rare in this literature.

One industry-funded trial of a low-dose branded extract (30 mg/day, roughly a tenth of the usual dose) is worth a special mention for a different reason: its headline VO₂max improvement turns out to be, arithmetically, almost exactly the rise in the maximum heart rate researchers measured on the same participants — not a new oxygen measurement at all, since VO₂max here was computed from heart rate numbers rather than measured directly (Prajapati 2026, Phytotherapy Research, PMID 41846233). A genuine fitness effect might be hiding inside that trial's data, but the way its headline number was built means the vault can't currently tell.

Blood sugar and general wellbeing: the same verdict, two more times

An 11-trial dose-response meta-analysis found ashwagandha modestly lowers fasting blood glucose (about 3 mg/dL) at the lowest GRADE certainty rating ("very low") — a real but small, uncertain signal, with the longer-term marker (HbA1c) an uninformative null on only three trials (Farrin 2026, Diabetes Research and Clinical Practice, PMID 42641800). Small as it is, this is real enough to flag: if you take a prescription that also lowers blood sugar — metformin or a GLP-1 drug — stacking ashwagandha on top adds a plausible, if unmeasured, hypoglycemia risk. Separately, a small trial in adults 60–85 found every quality-of-life subscale improved alongside better self-reported daytime alertness and physical-fitness test scores — but the paper reports only p-values, with no actual size of effect anywhere in the accessible text (Honnutagi 2026, Phytotherapy Research, PMID 41725116), funded by the manufacturer of the tested product.

And neither saffron nor ashwagandha — two of the most-studied calming botanicals — has ever been tested in a randomized trial for burnout or PTSD, despite a plausible biological rationale for both (Alyami 2026). That's not evidence they don't work for those conditions; it's an honest gap nobody has filled yet.

What this is actually worth to you

Five independent outcome families — stress hormones, mood symptoms, exercise performance, blood sugar, and general wellbeing in older adults — all point the same direction and all hit the same wall: real effect, size not nailed down. The clearest exception is cortisol, now backed by two independent analyses landing close together, which is the one claim here you could reasonably call more than a maybe. Almost every trial in this literature uses a different manufacturer's standardized extract at a different dose, funded (directly or through a research-grant relationship) by that manufacturer more often than not — two of the sources above are industry-funded, and a third branded extract's trial showed a real behavioral effect sitting behind a measurement that doesn't hold up to scrutiny.

For GOAL.md's standing rule — supplements must carry independent third-party testing (NSF, USP, Informed Sport) to enter a protocol — none of the manufacturer-run studies above qualify, regardless of whether their results hold up. Ashwagandha also sits in a crowded "wind-down" category alongside L-theanine and chamomile on this vault, with genuinely overlapping sedative mechanisms; stacking several of them is redundancy, not four independent wins, and the new factorial trial above is a concrete demonstration that combining supplements doesn't just not-help sometimes — it can actively erase a real benefit.

The bottom line

If you're deciding whether ashwagandha does anything: probably yes, modestly, on cortisol and likely on stress and mood symptoms too — but no study here lets you bank on a specific size of benefit, and the one trial that tested stacking it with a sleep supplement found the combination wiped out the gain. No dose is recommended here, because no independently-verified product has been tested at a dose this vault can stand behind. If you're already taking a glucose-lowering medication, that's a real conversation to have with your clinician before adding this on top.
