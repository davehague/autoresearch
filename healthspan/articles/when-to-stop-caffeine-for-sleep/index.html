When should you stop drinking caffeine? What the evidence supports

If you want caffeine without wrecking your sleep, the evidence points to a simple rule: stop drinking coffee about 8–9 hours before bed, and push a caffeinated pre-workout even earlier — roughly 13 hours out. The catch most people miss is that "I fall asleep fine after an evening coffee" isn't the win it feels like: caffeine quietly cuts your deep sleep and total sleep time even when you don't notice trouble dozing off. This isn't a "quit caffeine" article — it's a timing one. Here's what the trials show.

Not medical advice. This summarizes published evidence to inform your own choices and any conversation with your clinician.

What the evidence shows

The anchoring study is a 2023 meta-analysis of 24 randomized controlled trials of caffeine and sleep (PMID: 36870101). Pooled across the trials, caffeine measurably degraded subsequent sleep:

  • −45 minutes of total sleep time,
  • −7% sleep efficiency (more of your time in bed spent awake), and
  • less deep (slow-wave) sleep — the physically restorative stage.

Crucially, the effect persists for hours, which is what makes timing the lever. From the same analysis, the evidence-based cutoffs are: stop coffee at least ~8.8 hours before bed, and stop a typical caffeinated pre-workout (~217 mg) ~13.2 hours before bed (pre-workouts are dosed higher, so they need a longer runway). Evidence tier: meta-analysis of RCTs, the top of the hierarchy.

A separate crossover RCT sharpens the point: a 400 mg dose taken even 6 hours before bed still significantly disrupted sleep versus placebo (PMID: 24235903) — "6 hours is fine" is not far enough for a larger dose.

The practical version

Work backward from your bedtime:

  • Bed at 10:30 pm → last coffee by early-to-mid afternoon (~1:30–2:00 pm).
  • Caffeinated pre-workout → keep it to the morning (roughly before ~9:30 am for that same bedtime).

These are population averages, and caffeine metabolism varies a lot between people (genetics, habitual intake, medications). So treat the cutoff as a default to start from, not a stopwatch — if you sleep poorly, move it earlier before you conclude caffeine isn't the problem.

Two things worth knowing

  • This is about sleep, not alertness. Caffeine's daytime benefit is real and separate; nothing here says stop drinking it. The claim is narrow and well-supported: caffeine late in the day costs you sleep. The fix is when, not whether.
  • L-theanine doesn't buy you a later cutoff. The popular L-theanine + caffeine combo is a daytime focus pairing; it doesn't neutralize caffeine's effect on that night's sleep. The timing rule stands regardless.

Where it fits

The most important interaction is with sleep regularity — keeping consistent sleep-wake timing, which the vault treats as the single strongest foundation lever. Late caffeine directly undermines it: an evening stimulant is working against the exact thing that does the most for your sleep and long-term health. If you're going to invest in one sleep habit, protecting it from late caffeine is close to free.

So — what should you do?

Drink your coffee; just draw a line in the early afternoon and move caffeinated pre-workout to the morning. The benefit is a real, measured improvement in sleep quantity and depth, at zero cost beyond a little planning.
