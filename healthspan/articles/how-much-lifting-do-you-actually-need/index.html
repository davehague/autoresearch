How much lifting do you actually need? What the evidence supports

Short answer: about 90 to 120 minutes a week, and there is no measured benefit to going past that. The largest study we have on this — three prospective cohorts following 147,374 adults for up to 30 years, with 35,798 deaths — found that people doing 90–119 minutes a week of resistance training had roughly 13% lower all-cause mortality, 19% lower cardiovascular mortality, and 27% lower neurological-disease mortality than people doing none. Above about 120 minutes a week the curve goes flat (Zhang 2026, PMID 42230125).

Two hours a week. Two or three sessions. That is the whole dose, and the ceiling arrives early.

But read the next paragraph before you treat that as settled, because the tier matters more than the number.

The honest grade: this is a cohort study, not a trial

Nobody has randomised anyone to thirty years of lifting or not lifting, and nobody ever will. The mortality evidence here is observational: it compares people who chose to lift against people who chose not to, with statistical adjustment for the differences the researchers could measure.

That leaves a real alternative explanation on the table — the healthy-exerciser problem. People who lift weights for decades also tend to sleep better, drink less, see doctors more, and have the kind of life that makes a gym habit possible. Adjustment reduces that confounding. It does not eliminate it.

Two further limits worth knowing:

  • The cohorts are narrow. These are largely US health professionals, predominantly white. Whether the same numbers hold elsewhere is untested.
  • Training volume was self-reported. People are not accurate historians of their own exercise.

So the correct summary is: a large, long, consistent association on a hard endpoint, held below "proven" by a design ceiling rather than by weak evidence. That is roughly as strong as observational evidence gets. It is also the reason this article does not tell you lifting causes a 13% mortality reduction — it says that is what the association looks like, and that the association is the best thing available.

One thing that ceiling costs you nothing on: the cohort measured minutes, not intensity. So the dose recommendation does not depend on lifting heavy — see the safety section below, where that distinction does a lot of work.

What lifting demonstrably does, at randomised tier

The mortality signal is observational. Several other effects have been tested in actual randomised trials, and they are worth having:

Blood pressure comes down, and by a clinically meaningful amount. Across 51 randomised trials (n = 2,025, adults 60+), resistance training lowered systolic blood pressure by 6.11 mmHg and diastolic by 2.53 mmHg (both p < 0.001), with larger effects in women (Ghasemi 2025, PMID 40578159). A 6 mmHg systolic drop is the size of effect guidelines care about. It also offers a plausible mechanism for part of the cardiovascular-mortality association above — though it is a surrogate (an office blood-pressure reading, not a heart attack), so it adds a thread rather than upgrading the evidence.

Depressive symptoms fall substantially — in people who have clinical depression. Across 29 randomised trials (n = 2,036 adults with diagnosed depression), resistance training reduced depressive symptoms at SMD −0.94 (95% CI −1.16 to −0.72) (Chang 2025, PMC12745427). Discount that magnitude three ways before carrying it anywhere: heterogeneity was very high (I² = 80.1%), the trials were open-label with a self-reported outcome (people who know they got the exercise report feeling better), and the population was diagnosed patients, whose scores have the most room to fall. The direction is well-established. The number is not yours if you are not depressed.

How to lift: four variables, and three of them matter less than you think

This is where most gym argument happens, and where the evidence is most consistently deflating.

VariableQuestionWhat the trials found
Loadhow heavy?Heavier wins for strength — ≥70% of one-rep max beat lighter loads on leg press (SMD 0.95) and leg extension (0.63)
Efforthow close to failure?You don't need to grind — stopping short of failure matched or slightly beat training to failure on dynamic strength (SMD 0.24)
Volumehow many sets?33 sets a week grew no more muscle than 18
Stabilitywobbly surface?No advantage over stable ground on strength or mobility

Load is the one variable that clearly pays. Across 18 randomised trials (n = 1,283, adults 50+), training at ≥70% of one-rep max produced meaningfully more lower-limb strength than lighter training (Shen 2026, PMID 42366614).

Effort is not. Across 19 trials (n = 461), sets taken short of failure produced slightly better dynamic strength than sets taken to failure — SMD 0.24 (0.06–0.42) (Wu 2026, PMID 42410632). Small, and pointing the convenient direction, but it is the largest and best-supported result in that paper.

Volume is the newest answer, and the best-designed study in this whole set. Twenty-five trained lifters had one leg's weekly volume raised 120% above their habit and the other leg's raised 20% — reaching 32.8 versus 18.0 sets per week — for eight weeks, every set to failure. Both legs grew. Neither grew more (Camargo 2026, doi 10.1152/japplphysiol.00284.2026).

Why that design deserves attention: each person was their own control. Diet, sleep, genetics, stress and adherence were identical across the two conditions because they belonged to the same body. That removes the single largest source of noise in this research, which is why 25 people were enough — the researchers calculated in advance that they needed 24, and said so.

⚠ Read the boundary, though. The trial was powered to detect a moderate difference. It can say "no moderate-or-larger difference between 18 and 33 sets." It cannot rule out a small one, and a small advantage compounded over years is exactly what a committed lifter would care about. Eight weeks is also short.

Unstable surfaces buy confidence, not capability. Across 7 trials in older adults, training on wobble boards and similar showed no advantage for lower-limb strength or short-term mobility; what improved was self-reported fear of falling, which is close to the most expectation-prone thing you can measure in a study nobody can be blinded to (Doan 2026, doi 10.1097/PHM.0000000000002966).

A warning about how this literature is written

Three of those four answers are "the easier option is not measurably worse," and that should make you suspicious rather than relieved — including if, like most people, you were hoping to hear it.

Across seven separate findings in this literature, our reading has been the same: a study fails to detect a difference, and then writes that failure up as though it had demonstrated equivalence. The direction of the error is not random. Every time, the null flattered the recommendation the authors were already making.

The clearest case is a safety comparison. Shen 2026 recommends heavy lower-limb loading in adults over 50 while reporting fall incidence at RR 2.68 with a confidence interval running from 0.65 all the way to 11.11, and describes that as "similar safety profiles." An interval reaching an elevenfold increase is not evidence of similarity. It is evidence that nobody looked hard enough.

The volume trial is the interesting exception and the interesting confirmation at once. It is the first study here with a real power calculation that it met — and its title still reads "Large increases in resistance training volume do not impair muscle hypertrophy," while the paper's own summary block correctly says "no evidence… under the present experimental conditions." A registered, properly powered, conflict-free trial made the same rhetorical move as the underpowered ones. Which suggests this is a writing-and-incentives problem, not a statistics problem.

Practical translation: "X was not shown to be better" and "X and Y are the same" are different sentences, and this field routinely prints the second when it has earned the first.

What lifting has NOT been shown to do

It has not been shown to move bone density by lifting heavier. No detectable difference in bone mineral density between training intensities (lumbar spine SMD 0.28, −0.02 to 0.58 — a near-miss that is underpowered, not equivalent), and creatine adds nothing to bone beyond the training itself (Chilibeck 2017, PMID 29138605). Whatever bone benefit exists appears to come from training at all, not from training heavy and not from a supplement. Do not add plates, or buy a powder, for your skeleton.

And the muscle you build is not itself the proven mechanism. Low fat-free mass tracks with dying sooner — RR 1.42, falling to 1.31 once the study's own authors correct for the publication bias they detected (Haas 2026, PMID 42430205). But the effect shrinks as the measurement improves: calf circumference gives RR 2.19, DXA gives 1.52, bioimpedance gives 1.23. A real tissue effect should not get weaker as you measure it better. That gradient is what a frailty-and-illness marker looks like, not a causal lever. Nothing in that literature shows that adding muscle lowers mortality. The case for lifting rests on the training cohorts, not on the muscle.

Safety — the part that is usually skipped

Resistance training is not risk-free, and this literature is unusually bad at counting the risk.

Almost every trial above measures performance and reports nothing about injury. The volume study is the exception, and only by accident: buried in its participant flow is the fact that three of 28 participants withdrew "due to persistent muscle or joint pain." That is roughly 11% dropout for musculoskeletal pain, from a supervised programme of leg press and leg extension taken to failure, in healthy trained 18-to-35-year-olds who were screened to exclude existing injury (Camargo 2026).

And because each participant trained one leg at each volume, the study cannot tell you which volume caused it.

If you are returning from a knee, hip, shoulder or back injury, the heavy-load recommendation is the one piece of advice here that should go through a clinician or physiotherapist before it goes into your week. The reassuring phrase "similar safety profiles" has not been earned. This applies to unstable-surface training too — an unpredictable footing is precisely the condition a healing joint is least ready for, and no trial enrolled anyone in orthopaedic recovery.

One scheduling note that costs nothing: if you are lifting for muscle, don't take an ice bath right after. Post-workout cold-water immersion measurably blunts the hypertrophy adaptation (Piñero 2024, PMC11235606). Move the cold away from the session, or skip it on lifting days.

Putting it together

For a 165 lb (75 kg) healthy adult with no injury history, everything above collapses into something fairly small:

  1. Two to three sessions a week, totalling 90–120 minutes. That is the dose the mortality curve flattens at. More is not shown to buy more.
  2. Pair it with aerobic work. The lowest mortality in the cohort belonged to people doing both — roughly HR 0.55 for the combination, against neither (Zhang 2026). They are complementary, not competing.
  3. You do not need to train to failure. The evidence mildly favours stopping short.
  4. You do not need to pile on sets. Eighteen hard sets a week for a muscle group did what thirty-three did, over eight weeks.
  5. Heavier loads do build more strength — and that is the one recommendation here with a safety caveat attached. Discuss it with a clinician if you have a joint that is healing.
  6. Eat enough protein. The training is the lever; protein is what lets it work, plateauing around 1.6 g/kg/day — for a 165 lb adult, roughly 120 g (Morton 2018, PMID 28698222).
  7. Skip the wobble board, and skip the supplements bought for your bones.

The most useful thing in this entire article is the ceiling. The literature's default posture is that more is better — more sets, heavier loads, closer to failure. On the outcomes that actually matter, the evidence does not support any of that. Two hours a week, not taken to the edge, appears to capture essentially all of the measured benefit. The rest is preference.

Not medical advice. This article organises published evidence to inform a conversation with a clinician. It prescribes nothing and sets no doses. Anything above involving heavy lower-limb loading, an existing injury, or a medication should go through your doctor or physiotherapist first.
