Does balance training actually prevent falls? What the evidence supports — and the one question nobody can answer yet

Yes, on the outcome that matters: structured balance training cuts real falls, not just a score on a balance test, and the best evidence for that is now high-certainty. What's unresolved is which specific method is "best" — the rankings you'll see quoted don't survive their own confidence intervals — and the one domain that actually decides whether a stumble becomes a fracture has barely been studied at all. Here's what the evidence actually supports, evidence-graded, and what it means for whether this belongs in a routine.

Not medical advice. This summarizes published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician; it doesn't prescribe a dose, duration, or specific program.

The headline: two independent, high-tier sources agree balance training cuts real falls

The strongest claim this literature can make rests on two separate pieces of evidence, each a different design, each converging on the same conclusion.

First, a cluster-randomized trial across 128 rural Chinese villages gave older adults with cardiovascular disease a 12-month, village-doctor-led program combining balance and functional exercise with health education. Against usual care, any fall dropped from 42.7% to 31.6% (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.43–0.84), the falls rate from 1.6 to 0.9 per person-year (RR 0.62, 0.43–0.89), and fall-related injury from 24.9% to 15.9% (OR 0.57, 0.42–0.78) (Tang 2026, Age and Ageing, PMID 42580695). The injury estimate is at least as large as the any-fall estimate — which rules out the specific failure mode you'd otherwise suspect: a program that looks good on paper because it only prevents harmless stumbles.

Second, an independent, prospectively registered umbrella review — pooling 41 systematic reviews and roughly 4,475 primary studies, with overlap between reviews checked and found minimal — found balance and multicomponent exercise reduced fall rates by 23–34% (rate ratio 0.66–0.77), rated GRADE HIGH certainty (Dharmansyah 2026, Journal of Nutrition, Health & Aging, PMID 42208412).

Two methodologically distinct, independently conducted syntheses landing on the same hard endpoint — not a balance-test score, but whether someone actually fell — with no credible source currently pushing back, is what earns this map's consolidating rating.

The catch: the trial that proved it used a bundle, and nothing isolates the balance training

The village-doctor trial's 12-month program wasn't balance training alone — it was balance exercise plus functional exercise plus health education plus a year of regular contact with a doctor, compared against a usual-care group that got none of that attention. Nothing in the design separates "the balance exercises worked" from "having a doctor check in on you monthly for a year worked." Falls were also self-reported in a trial nobody could blind, which biases in the direction that flatters the intervention.

This is a pattern that shows up across multidomain interventions generally: bundles resist being taken apart. Against usual care, the bundle works. What specifically inside the bundle is doing the work remains genuinely unanswered.

Almost any structured balance practice beats doing nothing — which is informative, and also a little suspicious

The largest single piece of evidence here is a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 423 randomized trials (33,901 participants, 19 distinct exercise modalities) in older adults recruited without any disease criterion (Wang 2026, Ageing Research Reviews, PMID 42600321). Eighteen of the nineteen modalities tested improved balance versus control. Only one didn't: stretching.

That's a real and useful finding on its own, but "18 of 19 wins" is also a yellow flag. Exercise trials can't blind participants, balance tests are mostly effort-dependent and clinician-scored, and most control groups in this literature are simply told to keep doing nothing. A network where almost every arm beats the control group is partly measuring the gap between doing something and doing nothing — which is exactly what makes stretching's failure to clear even that low bar the more informative half of the result.

The modality rankings you'll see quoted don't actually hold up

The same 423-trial network ranked the modalities against each other: perturbation training led (effect size 0.66, 95% credible interval 0.49–0.82), followed by sensory training (0.55), dance (0.54), and agility stepping (0.53). This is the kind of ranking that gets quoted as "science says X is the best exercise for balance" — and it's the part of the evidence that supports that claim the least. Perturbation's own lower bound (0.49) sits below the point estimates of dance and agility stepping. The top four intervals overlap across most of their range. On the numbers actually published, the top four methods are not statistically distinguishable from one another.

A smaller, independently conducted network (12 randomized trials, 491 participants) makes the same point more sharply. Its published headline put Pilates at an effect of 0.64 versus control. But when the authors split that estimate into its direct and indirect components — a standard network meta-analysis diagnostic most syntheses in this space don't bother reporting — the direct randomized evidence for Pilates was 0.07 (95% CI −0.65 to 0.78), a null, while the indirect evidence (routed through other comparisons) was 1.01 (0.43–1.60). The two disagreed at a statistically detectable level (P = 0.045). The randomized trials that actually tested Pilates against control found nothing; the published number comes from routing around them (Zhao 2026, Frontiers in Public Health, PMID 42630909). The same paper also found that restricting to tests performed in an actual laboratory with force-plate instrumentation — rather than simpler clinical or field balance tests — caused three of its four active treatment arms to stop clearing statistical significance entirely.

The practical takeaway from both networks: don't pick a method based on a ranking. Pick one you'll actually do. The review's own subgroup analysis found perturbation training performs best when supervised and in higher-fall-risk groups, while sensory training performs best delivered at home — the single most actionable distinction in this literature, because it's the one that answers "what works without a trainer."

The domain that actually prevents fractures is the one nobody has adequately studied

There are two different kinds of "balance." Static balance — standing still, holding a position — is what almost every instrument in this literature measures, and it's where the strongest evidence sits. Reactive balance — what your body does in the half-second after you've already started to fall, which determines whether a trip becomes a catch or a hip fracture — is the domain that actually matters most for injury prevention. The 423-trial network could only call this domain "tentative," with no point estimate and no confidence interval for any modality at all.

A dedicated trial has since tried to fill exactly that gap: a tether-release reactive-stepping program in 88 older adults (mean age 72), layered on top of strength training both groups received (Sharma 2026, Age and Ageing, PMID 42696641). The surrogate measures it was designed to move — maximum step length, choice stepping reaction time — improved significantly. Actual falls, both in a laboratory simulation and tracked over 12 months of daily life, did not (p=.19). Adherence was 90%, which rules out "people didn't show up" as the explanation. This is now the third time this literature has produced a case where the surrogate moved and the real-world outcome didn't — a pattern worth taking seriously whenever a balance study reports only a test score and not an actual fall count.

What doesn't move the needle: fear of falling

A separate, well-designed sham-controlled trial (n=160, assessor- and statistician-blinded) tested a 4-month exercise-plus-cognitive-behavioral-therapy bundle specifically targeting fear of falling — psychological concern, not fall rate itself. At 8-month follow-up it found no advantage over sham on fear of falling, function, or a panel of stress and inflammatory markers (Britting 2026, Aging Clinical and Experimental Research, PMID 42773257). The trial fell short of its planned enrollment (160 of a planned 200), and the authors themselves caution the long-term null might reflect that shortfall rather than a true absence of effect. Either way: this is a different outcome than fall rate, and a null here neither confirms nor undermines the fall-rate evidence above.

A genuine mortality signal, with a genuine "which came first" problem

Separately from falls, balance and agility tests turn out to predict death better than you'd expect. A cohort of 13,423 Taiwanese adults 65+ given seven standardized fitness tests and followed for a median 7 years found the two balance-and-agility tests — the 8-foot up-and-go and one-leg stance — predicted mortality better than a cardiorespiratory fitness test, in the same people (Wu 2026, JAMA Network Open, PMID 42574013).

The honest caveat: the 8-foot up-and-go is also the most physically integrative test in the battery — it needs heart, lungs, legs, joints, vision, and inner-ear function all working together — which makes it exactly the kind of test that would predict death well if what it's actually detecting is undiagnosed illness quietly degrading someone's body, rather than a trainable capacity that, if improved, would change their risk. The study excluded people with dementia, stroke, or catastrophic illness, which blunts that concern somewhat, but doesn't eliminate it. Being the best predictor and being the best lever to pull are different claims — this study can only support the first.

The bottom line

Structured balance training, delivered as part of a real-world program, cuts actual falls and fall-related injury — that's now backed by two independent, high-tier sources agreeing on a hard endpoint, not just a test score. Almost any structured method beats doing nothing. What the evidence can't yet support: a confident ranking of which specific method is "best" (the published rankings don't survive their own uncertainty), a verdict on whether balance training specifically — versus the attention and education that came bundled with it — is what drove the village-trial result, or any claim about reactive balance, the domain most directly tied to whether a stumble becomes a fracture, where the evidence is thinnest of all. If you're choosing a method, the most defensible answer in this literature is the least exciting one: pick something that trains balance and that you'll actually keep doing, and don't put much weight on a league table that claims to know which one is "best."
