Does exercise help an arthritic joint? What the evidence supports — and which kind actually works

Short answer: exercise produces a real but small benefit for a hip or knee with osteoarthritis — too small for most people to feel on its own — it doesn't add anything on top of care you're already getting, and it doesn't appear to hurt you. If you're going to exercise an arthritic joint, which kind of exercise you do matters more than whether you do it at all: mind-body movement (tai chi, yoga-type practice) ranks best for pain and stiffness, a mixed programme ranks best for daily function, and strength training ranks best — unsurprisingly — for muscle strength. No single modality wins everything.

Here is the honest split, up front:

  • Versus doing nothing, exercise helps — a little. A high-quality Cochrane review found real, statistically significant improvements in pain and function for hip osteoarthritis, at a magnitude the review's own authors call "unlikely to be clinically meaningful."
  • Added on top of care you're already receiving, it does nothing measurable — in the review's best-powered comparison.
  • It doesn't appear to make things worse. The only randomised adverse-event data on loading a painful joint found slightly fewer adverse events on exercise, not more.
  • Which exercise you choose matters. A 51-trial network comparing exercise modalities head-to-head for knee osteoarthritis found no single winner — the best choice depends on whether you're trying to reduce pain, improve function, or build strength.
  • A drug can reach the same symptom through a completely different door. GLP-1-class weight-loss drugs reduce knee-OA pain too — not by training the joint, but by removing load from it. Different mechanism, same destination.

What exercise is sold as doing for a bad joint

The pitch, roughly: move the joint, strengthen the muscles around it, and the pain and stiffness of osteoarthritis improve — maybe even enough to replace or delay other care. This map tests that claim directly, joint by joint and modality by modality, rather than assuming it.

The founding evidence: real, small, and honest about it

This map's foundation is a Cochrane systematic review (18 randomised trials, 1,368 people with hip osteoarthritis, no funding, GRADE certainty on every estimate) — about as high a tier of evidence as this vault ever sees. It tested exercise against three different comparators, and got three different answers.

(1) Versus doing nothing, usual care, or limited education — land-based exercise probably reduces pain (MD −7.19 points on a 0–100 scale, 95% CI −10.70 to −3.68, moderate certainty) and probably improves physical function (MD −8.79, −12.00 to −5.41, moderate certainty). Quality of life didn't move. The review's own words: these improvements are "unlikely to be clinically meaningful." A seven-point shift on a hundred-point pain scale is real, and small enough that most people wouldn't notice it (Hall 2026, PMID 42486497).

(2) Added on top of care a person is already receiving — nothing measurable, and this is the best-powered comparison in the whole review: pain, function, quality of life, and withdrawals were all null, at moderate certainty, across 751 people. If you're already getting some form of care for your joint, adding exercise to it didn't show up as helping further in this evidence.

(3) Versus an attention control (a comparison group that gets equal time and attention but not the exercise itself, isolating how much of the benefit is really just "someone paid attention to me") — the pain benefit vanished, but on only 2 studies and 123 people, too few to call that a real null rather than an underpowered one.

The one adverse-event number this vault has for loading a painful weight-bearing joint: exercise showed a lower rate of adverse events than comparators (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.58–0.97, n=731) — read as an absence of harm, not evidence of protection, since the upper bound sits just under 1. Still, it's a reassuring result for a joint many people are afraid to load.

Does it transfer from the hip to the knee?

Mostly yes, with the same modest magnitude. A 139-RCT network meta-analysis (n=9,644 knee osteoarthritis patients) ranking twelve non-drug therapies against each other found exercise placed 2nd–3rd overall, behind a knee brace and near hydrotherapy, with a statistically significant advantage over ultrasound and placebo on pain. No GRADE certainty accompanies this ranking — a step down from the hip evidence's rigor — but the direction agrees: exercise helps a knee with osteoarthritis too, modestly, and holds its own against other non-drug options (Chen X 2025).

A separate, independent network found the same shape from a different angle: added to existing care, a bundle of manual therapy plus exercise modestly reduced knee-OA pain and improved function in the short-to-medium term — but that benefit didn't extend to long-term follow-up (da Silva 2026, PMID 42885413). Exercise helps, exercise plus manual therapy helps a bit more for a while, and nothing in this literature has shown a durable fix.

Which exercise actually works — the question this map couldn't answer until now

Every result above treats "exercise" as one thing. It isn't. A PROSPERO-registered network meta-analysis of 51 randomised trials (n=3,045 knee-osteoarthritis patients) directly compared five exercise modalities against each other — strength training, mind-body exercise (tai chi/yoga-type movement), aerobic exercise, blood-flow-restriction training, and mixed programmes — and found no single modality wins on every outcome:

  • Pain and stiffness: mind-body exercise ranks best. Pain, SMD −1.13 (95% CI −1.47 to −0.79, p<0.001); stiffness, SMD −1.15 (−1.59 to −0.70, p<0.001).
  • Knee function: a mixed programme ranks best, mind-body exercise second. Mixed exercise, SMD −1.43 (−2.31 to −0.55, p<0.001); mind-body, SMD −1.28 (−1.64 to −0.93, p<0.001).
  • Muscle strength: strength training ranks best — and only for muscle strength. SMD 0.79 (0.47 to 1.12, p<0.001) (Diao 2026).

Certainty for these rankings is graded moderate-to-low for pain and strength, and low-to-very-low for function and stiffness — read them as directional, not settled. But this is the first time this map has had a number attached to the mind-body/tai-chi effect at all: an earlier high-certainty umbrella review named tai chi's benefit for knee-OA function as one of only 23 associations in its entire literature to reach high GRADE certainty, but published no effect size whatsoever for it. This network finally supplies one.

The practical read: there is no single "best exercise for a bad knee." What you should do depends on what you're trying to fix — pain and stiffness point toward gentler, mind-body movement; day-to-day function points toward a mixed programme; and if you specifically want the muscle stronger, you still have to do strength training for that.

A completely different door to the same room: what a weight-loss drug does to the same pain

This map tracks exercise. A separate map in this vault tracks GLP-1-class drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide, and newer agents like retatrutide) — and one of those drugs' trials just answered almost the same question exercise is trying to answer, through an entirely different mechanism.

In a phase 3 trial of retatrutide (a weight-loss drug combining three hormone-receptor targets) in 2,339 adults with obesity, a prespecified substudy in 574 participants with knee osteoarthritis found the drug reduced WOMAC pain scores by 1.6 to 1.8 points more than placebo (p<0.001 at every tested dose) — alongside roughly a quarter of body weight lost over 80 weeks (Jastreboff 2026). The trial doesn't separate a joint-specific drug effect from the generic effect of losing that much weight — this reads as pain relief via weight loss, not a new mechanism for the joint itself.

Two levers, same destination, no comparison between them. Nothing in this vault has tested exercise against drug-driven weight loss head-to-head for joint pain, so this isn't a verdict on which is "better" — it's a reminder that a painful joint has more than one route to relief, and they aren't mutually exclusive. (Retatrutide is unapproved and prescription-only where approved drugs in its class exist; nothing here is a recommendation to pursue either path instead of a clinician's advice.)

What else this map has ruled out or left open

  • Vitamin D3 and vitamin E don't add anything over exercise alone for knee osteoarthritis, in a small, underpowered pilot — recorded as inconclusive rather than a confident null.
  • A pharmacological comparison network (celecoxib, glucosamine/chondroitin, krill oil, probiotics, metformin) didn't include an exercise arm at all — so there's still no head-to-head evidence placing exercise against the drugs and supplements people also reach for.
  • Osteoarthritis carries a large but imprecisely measured sleep-disorder burden — roughly 42% of people with knee, hip, or hand osteoarthritis also report insomnia — but this is a comorbidity-prevalence finding, not a test of whether exercise helps the joint pain or the sleep problem.
  • A patellar contusion is not knee osteoarthritis. They share a location and nothing else — nothing in this literature transfers between a healing bruise and progressive cartilage degeneration.

So does exercise help?

Yes — modestly, honestly, and not as a substitute for whatever else you're already doing about a painful joint. It is not a dramatic fix: the effect sizes against doing nothing are real but small enough that the review's own authors call them unlikely to be clinically meaningful, and adding exercise on top of existing care didn't move the needle further in the best-powered test available. It also doesn't appear to hurt — the one adverse-event comparison favored exercise, slightly.

What's new is the "which kind" answer. If pain and stiffness are the problem, mind-body movement (tai chi or a similar practice) has the best-ranked evidence. If day-to-day function is the problem, a mixed programme ranks best. If you specifically want a stronger joint, strength training is the one modality that delivers that — but it won't be your best bet for the pain itself.

Not medical advice. This summarises published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician or physical therapist — it prescribes no specific programme, dose, or duration, and anyone with an acute joint injury (as opposed to osteoarthritis) should get individual guidance before starting or changing an exercise routine.
