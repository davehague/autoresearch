Is Zone 2 cardio worth it? What the evidence supports

Short answer: the aerobic base is worth building, and "Zone 2" is one reasonable way to build it — but it has never been shown to be the best way, and most of the confident claims made for it specifically are not supported.

Here is the honest split, up front:

  • The foundation is strong and it is about fitness, not about Zone 2. Higher cardiorespiratory fitness tracks with dramatically lower mortality, with no observed ceiling.
  • The dose is known, and it is smaller than you think. Most of the mortality benefit lands in the first 75–150 minutes a week.
  • Zone 2's specific selling points — that it is the optimal intensity, that it uniquely builds mitochondria, that it is the fat-burning zone — do not hold up. Two separate randomised comparisons now put moderate steady-state work at or near the bottom of the intensity rankings for fitness gains.
  • The single best-evidenced instruction in this entire literature is: keep going for more weeks. Not harder, not longer. More weeks.

No randomised trial has ever tested a Zone-2 protocol against an alternative and measured whether people live longer. Everything below is graded accordingly.

First, what "Zone 2" means

Zone 2 is low-intensity, steady-state aerobic work — roughly a conversational pace you could hold for an hour. It sits above walking and well below intervals. The popular case for it is that this specific intensity band drives mitochondrial and fat-oxidation adaptations better than harder training does.

That last claim is the one that fails. The rest of the case survives, in a weaker form than usual.

The foundation: fitness predicts mortality, and the effect is enormous

A retrospective cohort of 122,007 adults who underwent treadmill testing found all-cause mortality falling steadily with measured cardiorespiratory fitness. Comparing the lowest-fitness group to elite performers gave a hazard ratio of 5.04 — and, unusually, no upper limit of benefit appeared. The relationship held in adults over 70 and in people with hypertension (Mandsager 2018, PMID 30646252).

The tier matters, and it caps everything. This is observational. It measures fitness people already had, using a treadmill test — not fitness produced by an assigned training programme. Fit people differ from unfit people in many ways, and the cohort was clinic-referred, which selects further. Reverse causation is live: early illness lowers fitness before it kills anyone.

So: a very large, dose-responsive association on a hard endpoint, held below "proven" by a design ceiling rather than by weak evidence. It is the reason to build an aerobic base at all. It is not evidence for any particular way of building one.

The dose: most of the benefit is in the first 75 to 150 minutes a week

This is the most practically useful number in the article, and two independent lines agree on it.

A dose-response meta-analysis of 94 prospective cohorts covering more than 30 million adults and 811,616 deaths found the curve is steeply non-linear and front-loaded. About 150 minutes a week of moderate-to-vigorous activity was associated with all-cause mortality RR 0.69; half that — around 75 minutes a week — already captured roughly a 23% reduction; and benefit largely plateaued beyond a much higher threshold (Garcia 2023, PMID 36854652).

An independent prospective cohort of 109,407 Chinese adults (4,571 deaths) landed the floor in the same place from a different population and a different unit: a minimum protective dose around 75 minutes a week (Wang 2025, PMID 41360828).

Both are observational and both used self-reported activity, which people report badly. And both measure aerobic volume in general, not Zone 2 specifically.

The practical read: the first 75 to 150 minutes a week is where the return lives. That is two or three easy sessions. Going far beyond it is not obviously wrong — it is just not where the evidence says the payoff is.

Where Zone 2's specific claims fall down

It is not the optimal intensity

A meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials in healthy adults aged 60–80 found high-intensity interval training raised VO₂max more than moderate-intensity continuous training — a head-to-head difference of +1.17 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ — and did it in less session time (roughly 21–39 minutes versus 60 or more) (Chu 2026, PMID 41517714).

A second, larger comparison now points the same way — and finished moderate continuous training dead last. A Bayesian network meta-analysis of 61 randomised trials ranked 11 ways of adding training on VO₂max. Five beat the control condition: blood flow restriction +3.93 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, intermittent hypoxic training +2.74, HIIT +2.71, small-sided games +2.56, sprint intervals +2.49. Moderate-intensity continuous training — the closest thing here to Zone 2 — placed 11th of 11, at +0.16 (Li 2026, PMID 42597392).

Now read the interval, because it is the whole story. That +0.16 comes with a range of −2.66 to +2.91. The top of that range would put moderate training among the best modalities in the table. This is a failure to demonstrate an effect, not a demonstration that there is none — a distinction worth holding onto, because they get reported identically and mean opposite things.

And the population matters more than the ranking does. Every participant was a trained athlete, and the control group kept doing their normal sport training. So the question asked was: if you already train seriously, does adding easy steady-state volume on top do anything measurable? Apparently not. That is a genuinely different question from whether easy aerobic work does anything for a sedentary or detrained adult, and this study cannot answer the second one.

Note what none of this does. The outcome is VO₂max — a fitness measurement, not a health event. Nobody in these trials had a heart attack counted. So this refines the efficiency question and leaves the mortality foundation untouched.

How much to trust a modality ranking

Not much, and the authors of the study above say so themselves.

They rated their own evidence "predominantly low to very low" certainty — despite doing nearly everything right: registering the protocol in advance, requiring direct gas analysis for VO₂max (studies that merely estimated it were excluded), formally testing whether the network's indirect comparisons agreed with its direct ones, and running two pre-planned robustness checks. Excellent method applied to a thin literature does not manufacture certainty. The 61 trials averaged roughly 23 participants each, and only about 6% of participants came from women-only study arms.

The ranking itself is the least reliable part of the output. Blood flow restriction took first place — on the widest uncertainty range in the table (0.60 to 7.27), a range whose bottom sits below the best guess for four modalities ranked beneath it. The authors put it plainly: a top rank "coexists with its widest credible interval." On these numbers the top five are not distinguishable from one another.

This is the third time this pattern has shown up in the evidence base behind these articles — a network study producing a confident-looking leaderboard that its own uncertainty ranges do not support. The leaderboard is the part that gets quoted, and it is the part to ignore.

The mitochondrial story is weaker than advertised

A review in Sports Medicine argues that the "Zone 2 is optimal for mitochondrial and fat-oxidation capacity" claim traces largely to observation of elite endurance athletes, and that higher intensities drive those same adaptations more time-efficiently, especially at low training volumes (Storoschuk 2025, PMID 40560504).

This is a narrative review — opinion-tier, the weakest thing cited here — but it is now corroborated in direction by the randomised evidence above.

"Burning fat" during the session is not the point

The intracellular fat-oxidation story keeps failing to translate into outcomes. In a randomised crossover trial, exercising in a fasted state raised fat oxidation by about 23 kcal across a 30-minute bout — while the same condition was followed by roughly 547 extra kcal eaten over the next 24 hours. That is about 1 to 24 in the wrong direction.

What your muscle does with fuel during the hour is not the same thing as what your body does over the day.

The one instruction the evidence actually supports: keep going

This is the strongest randomised finding in the whole area, and it is not about intensity or duration.

A Bayesian meta-regression across 93 trials (78 studies, n = 1,188 untrained adults), pre-registered, conflict-free, with published analysis code, tested ten possible moderators of the VO₂max gain from interval training. Nine were flat.

  • Number of sprints per session: no effect (−0.04% per sprint, credible interval −0.32 to 0.24).
  • Total weekly sprint time, across a 16-fold range: no effect (−0.01% per extra 120 s/week).
  • Weeks of training: +0.7% per week (0.3 to 1.0) — about +2.69% (1.25 to 4.19) over four extra weeks. The only interval excluding zero (Hutchinson 2026, PMID 42477971).

This is a rare thing: a null that is actually well-powered, so "we found nothing" means something here rather than "our sample was too small." The returns are in the unbroken weeks, not in the session design.

Two honest caveats. The outcome is again VO₂max, a surrogate. And the authors explicitly decline to name a minimum effective dose — their model estimates change around a corpus average of about six sprints, so this does not license "two sprints is enough."

What about the biomarkers — telomeres, epigenetic age?

You will see aerobic exercise credited with slowing cellular ageing. There is real randomised evidence, and it is worth stating precisely.

A meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (n = 1,908) found exercise raised telomerase activity (SMD 0.35, 95% CI 0.20–0.51), carried by the aerobic subgroup, and maintained telomere length on a much shakier estimate (SMD 0.59, 0.14–1.06, with I² = 92% — meaning the trials disagree wildly). At least 16 weeks were needed before anything moved (Sun 2025, PMID 40642293).

This is the most interesting comparison in the article, and it is not a reason to change anything. On these markers, omega-3 was null and vitamin D moved them only in a post-hoc sub-study of a trial that failed all its hard endpoints. The free intervention outperforms the supplements on the supplements' own favourite biomarker.

But they are still biomarkers. Telomerase activity is not a health outcome. Nothing here upgrades the case for cardio; it just removes a reason to buy pills.

Does the kind of cardio matter? Apparently not much

A network meta-analysis of 47 randomised trials (n = 1,995, adults 60+) compared seven exercise modalities on machine-measured vascular outcomes. Aerobic training topped arterial stiffness (SMD −0.81) and several modalities beat control — but the authors state their central result twice: "no statistically significant differences were identified across any head-to-head comparisons," and "the statistical evidence does not support the prescription of a single 'most effective' modality" (Wen 2026, PMID 42516957).

Read that as no evidence of difference, not evidence of no difference — 1,995 people spread across seven modalities is thin. But combined with everything above, the pattern is consistent: supervised aerobic exercise of essentially any kind helps these markers, and the evidence does not support picking one for the purpose.

What about supplements that claim to raise your fitness?

One is worth a paragraph, because the evidence is better than usual and the conclusion is still modest.

A meta-analysis of 38 placebo-controlled trials (n = 703) found beetroot juice raised VO₂max by SMD 0.24 (0.11–0.37) — falling to 0.19 (0.07–0.31) once the analysis corrected for detected publication bias. The effect was largest in recreationally active people (0.38) and not demonstrable in highly trained athletes (0.07, interval crossing zero) (Qin & Kim 2026, PMID 42523624).

Unusually for a supplement literature, the comparison is clean: the standard placebo is nitrate-depleted beetroot juice — the identical drink with the active compound stripped out — used in 34 of the 38 trials, and restricting to those trials does not change the answer.

So the effect is real, small, and measured on a treadmill test on a supplement day. Nothing was measured after the supplement stopped. Set against another four weeks of consistent training being worth +2.69%, the ranking is clear: the training is better evidenced and free. Beetroot juice is a food; there is no reason not to drink it, and no reason to expect it to change your health.

So should you do Zone 2?

Yes, if it is what you will actually keep doing — and that is the real argument for it, not physiology.

The defensible case for low-intensity steady-state work is:

  1. The aerobic base is worth having (large observational mortality association, no ceiling).
  2. The dose is modest (75–150 minutes a week captures most of it).
  3. It is low-impact, which matters if you have a knee, hip or back that intervals would aggravate. This is a property of the modality, not a study finding — no trial cited here randomised people by joint health.
  4. It is sustainable, and sustained weeks are the one variable randomised evidence says moves the needle.

The case you should not make for it: that it is the optimal intensity, that it uniquely builds mitochondria, that it burns fat in any way that survives contact with 24-hour energy intake, or that it has been shown to extend life. None of those are supported.

If you can tolerate higher intensity and want the same fitness in less time, the randomised evidence mildly favours intervals — at a higher joint cost you may or may not want to pay.

One assumption in that trade deserves checking, because it is usually stated without evidence: that people stick with easy training and quit hard training. In a Bayesian meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials of interval training, adherence averaged 85.7% — and none of the prescription variables predicted it. How long the intervals were, how many there were, how hard they were: none of it explained who kept showing up (Zhang 2026, PMID 42472833). A separate trial of home-based, remotely supervised interval training in older adults reported adherence around 96% (Lapierre 2026, PMID 42561157).

Both were supervised trials with motivated volunteers, so neither transfers cleanly to a Tuesday in February. But they do undercut the confident version of the adherence argument. "Zone 2 because I will actually do it" is a claim about you, not a finding about intensity — and it is still a perfectly good reason. It is just not one the studies establish on your behalf.

The honest bottom line

Build the aerobic base. Two or three easy sessions a week gets most of what is on offer. Pick the intensity you will still be doing in three months, because persistence is the only variable with well-powered randomised evidence behind it.

And keep the tiers straight: the mortality case is observational, the intensity comparisons are randomised but measured on VO₂max, the cellular-ageing findings are biomarkers, and no trial has tested a Zone-2 protocol against anything and counted deaths. That gap is the largest open question in this area, and it is unlikely ever to be closed.

Not medical advice. This summarises published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician — it prescribes no exercise dose and no supplement, and anyone with cardiac, joint or metabolic conditions should get individual guidance before changing what they do.
