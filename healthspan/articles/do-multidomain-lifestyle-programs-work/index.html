Do multidomain lifestyle programs work? What the evidence supports

Short answer: they reliably move test scores, they have not yet moved anything that happens to a person, and the strongest single predictor of how big the benefit looks is how little the comparison group was given.

A "multidomain lifestyle program" is a supervised package: a diet plan, an exercise prescription, some form of brain training, blood-pressure and cholesterol monitoring, and a coach who keeps you attending. It is one of the most heavily funded ideas in ageing research, and over five weeks this atlas has taken in ten trials and syntheses of it. The picture that emerges is genuinely mixed, and the mixture is the finding.

Read this article as an early-stage report. The evidence here is graded emerging — the lowest confidence tier this atlas uses — and the reason is spelled out below. Nothing in it is a recommendation to join or avoid such a program.

The three wins, stated at full strength

Three randomised trials, on three continents, run by different investigators, all found a benefit.

LatAm-FINGERS randomised 1,065 adults aged 60–77 across 11 Latin American countries to two years of a structured supervised program or to flexible health advice. Global cognition improved in both arms; the structured arm improved faster, by 0.11 standard deviations per year (95% CI 0.06 to 0.15, p < 0.0001) (Crivelli 2026, The Lancet).

SUPERBRAIN-MEET randomised 300 South Korean adults aged 60–85 with mild cognitive impairment to 24 weeks of a similar program or usual care. Their score on a standard neuropsychological battery improved 4.17 points more than control (95% CI 1.92 to 6.43, p < 0.001) — that battery is scaled to a mean of 100 with a standard deviation of 15, so the gap is about 0.28 standard deviations (Kim 2026, Neurology).

DEMFOS randomised 114 US veterans aged 65–85 with obesity and mild frailty to six months of supervised training inside a calorie deficit. Physical performance rose 2.5 points on a 0–36 scale (1.2 to 3.8, p = 0.0002), peak oxygen uptake rose about 19%, and six-minute walking distance rose 44.5 metres (Nava 2026, Lancet Healthy Longevity).

Those are real results from real randomised trials, and this article is not going to explain them away. What it is going to do is say precisely what they measured.

What "0.11 standard deviations per year" actually is

Every one of the cognitive results above is a test score. Not a diagnosis, not a case of dementia prevented, not an event of any kind. No participant in either cognition trial was recorded as developing mild cognitive impairment or dementia, because neither trial measured that.

A standard deviation is a unit of spread: 0.11 of one is the difference between someone at the exact middle of the distribution and someone slightly above the middle. Neither trial states a clinical anchor — a statement of how much test-score movement corresponds to a difference a person or their family would notice. Without one, the number is a real measurement of an uncertain thing.

And in an unblinded trial where the participants know which arm they are in and the same test battery is administered up to four times, two boring explanations are available and neither was excluded: people get better at tests they take repeatedly, and people who are being attended to try harder.

The two disappointments, which are the ones with hard outcomes

Look AHEAD is the reason this whole topic sits at emerging. It randomised 3,655 adults with type 2 diabetes to a ten-year intensive lifestyle intervention and followed them for 12 to 14 years afterwards — the longest and largest test of this idea ever run. Its actual primary endpoint, a cardiovascular composite, was null (hazard ratio 0.95, 95% CI 0.83 to 1.09) and the trial was stopped early for futility in 2012. A 2026 re-analysis found the effect on incident cognitive impairment varied by starting BMI: benefit in the overweight (HR 0.64, 0.46–0.89), nothing in the moderately obese (0.98, 0.85–1.16), and a harm signal above BMI 40 (1.40, 1.01–1.93) whose lower bound barely clears no-effect (Espeland 2026, Alzheimer's & Dementia).

US POINTER is the other. The largest and best-funded American trial of this design took MRI scans of 959 adults aged 60–79 at baseline, 12 months and 24 months, and looked at six markers of small-vessel brain disease. Five of the six did not move. White-matter hyperintensity volume — the decades-old validated standard measure — came in at P = .90 (Maillard 2026, JAMA Network Open).

And a new one, which is why this article exists now. The PRAMEV trial randomised 200 Brazilian adults with obesity to 20 weeks of a hybrid lifestyle program or usual medical care. Its prespecified primary endpoint — the proportion losing 5% or more of body weight — came back 20.6% versus 15.5%, odds ratio 1.34 (95% CI 0.61 to 2.92), P = .46 (Coelho 2026, JAMA Network Open).

That is the first randomised test in this atlas of an outcome that cannot be moved by attention, expectation or practice: bodyweight on an instrument. It did not move.

Read that interval carefully, because it does not say what it looks like it says

An odds ratio of 1.34 with bounds at 0.61 and 2.92 is not a demonstration that the program does not work. It is compatible with the program cutting your odds of success by 39%, and it is equally compatible with the program nearly tripling them. The honest reading is "200 people were not enough to tell," not "lifestyle programs fail."

This distinction gets collapsed constantly, usually in whichever direction suits the writer. It is worth holding onto: a non-significant result rules out only what its interval rules out, and this one rules out almost nothing.

The pattern that explains more than any ingredient does

The less the control arm was given, the larger the program looked — across three cognitive trials, monotonically. That is what unmatched contact time, structure and expectation buy in a study nobody can be blinded to.

PRAMEV is the useful exception that keeps this from becoming too tidy. Its comparator was the weakest of the four and it still produced a null — because it was weighing people rather than testing them. A weak comparator inflates an effect that exists; it cannot manufacture one out of nothing.

Nobody can say which part of the program does the work

Ten sources in, this remains unresolved, and it is not a technicality. Every trial delivers diet, exercise, brain training and monitoring as a single arm. None isolates a component, and the authors say so.

Two recent findings sharpen it rather than settling it:

  • A British cohort of 3,028 adults measured diet quality and accelerometer-recorded activity at age 46 and cognition at 51, and tested for an interaction between them. There wasn't one — diet tracked with better cognition regardless of activity level (Atkinson 2026, J Epidemiol Community Health). "Multidomain" is a claim that the levers combine into more than their sum. This found them additive at best.
  • One trial that gave equal billing to "promoting a healthy lifestyle" turned out to have delivered a graphic booklet and a self-monitoring diary against 526 median minutes of adaptive brain training — and the outcomes the lifestyle half should have moved were exactly the ones that stayed flat (Ophey 2026, Alzheimer's & Dementia). Read what a program delivered, not what it was called.

And PRAMEV adds a third version of the problem that has nothing to do with ingredients. Its one positive result — a per-protocol analysis showing 25.4% versus 12.3%, odds ratio 2.52 (1.03 to 6.13) — is a comparison between the 61% of program participants who lasted the full 20 weeks and the 79% of controls who kept showing up. 39.2% of the intervention arm dropped out against 21.4% of controls (P = .008). Restricting to finishers in an unblinded behavioural trial selects for exactly the traits that also produce weight loss. The component most likely to explain that result is not diet, exercise or counselling. It is who stayed.

To the trial's real credit, it published the intention-to-treat null, the per-protocol result and the dropout counts side by side, which is the only reason any of this is visible.

Two costs that rarely make the summaries

Supervised programs can hurt people. LatAm-FINGERS counted adverse events by arm: 412 in the structured arm against 66 in the health-advice arm, with musculoskeletal symptoms in 21% versus 2%. Some of that gap is surveillance — an arm with weekly contact reports a sore knee more readily — but not all of it, and it appears in almost no summary of that trial.

And weight-loss programs cost lean tissue. In DEMFOS, participants lost lean mass, thigh muscle volume and hip bone density despite resistance training three times a week and prescribed protein. A program built around a calorie deficit is not automatically muscle-sparing.

One thing this literature has actually settled

A drug lost to the program. DEMFOS ran both compared arms on metformin and a third arm on the identical program with placebo. The drug changed nothing across seventeen outcomes; the program's effects were the program's. That is a rare, clean, blinded demonstration inside a behavioural trial, and it is worth more than most of the surrogate results above.

So what should a healthy person in their forties do with this?

Almost nothing, and the reason is population rather than pessimism.

Every trial here enrolled people aged 60 to 85, or with type 2 diabetes, or with mild cognitive impairment, or with a BMI of 30 or more, or with frailty. None enrolled healthy middle-aged adults. The single result closest to that group — the British cohort at age 46 — is the one that found the components additive rather than synergistic.

And there is a simpler reason to be unmoved: the components are individually better evidenced than the bundle. Walking, aerobic training, resistance training, protein intake and a Mediterranean-pattern diet each have their own literature, their own effect sizes and their own dose-response curves. A combination result is never evidence for its components — and equally, someone already doing the components gains nothing by paying for them assembled.

What the bundle genuinely appears to add is structure and supervision, which buy adherence. That is a real thing, it is what PRAMEV's 39% dropout rate measures the absence of, and it is worth naming rather than dismissing. If the honest obstacle is "I know what to do and I don't do it," a supervised program is addressing your actual problem. If it isn't, the program is selling you an assembly of things you can do for free.

The one-line version: multidomain lifestyle programs produce small, real improvements in test scores against comparison groups that were given less; the two trials that measured something harder — a decade of cognitive outcomes, and bodyweight on a scale — found nothing; nobody knows which part works; and the healthiest thing you can take from ten trials is that the components have better evidence on their own than the package has together.

Not medical advice. This article organises published evidence to inform a conversation with a clinician. It prescribes nothing, sets no doses and recommends no program. If you are considering a structured lifestyle intervention — particularly one involving a calorie deficit, a new exercise load, or an existing joint problem — that decision belongs with your doctor.
