Creatine monohydrate: what the evidence actually supports

Creatine is having a moment, and the loudest new idea is that you should take more than the old 5 grams a day — 8, 10, even 20 — to get benefits in your brain and bones, not just your muscles. So the real question in 2026 isn't "does creatine work?" (it does) but "how much should I actually take?" The honest answer from the trials: 5 g/day is the dose that delivers, and the case for going higher has not held up — in several instances it was undercut by the very researchers who proposed it. Here's the evidence, graded, and what it means for whether the extra grams are worth it.

Not medical advice. This summarizes published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician; it doesn't prescribe a dose.

The strong claim: strength and lean mass (when you also lift)

This is the well-established one. A meta-analysis of 22 randomized controlled trials (721 participants) found creatine taken during resistance training added +1.37 kg of lean tissue mass (95% CI 0.97–1.76) and measurably improved chest- and leg-press strength beyond training alone (PMID: 29138605). Evidence tier: meta-analysis of RCTs, consistent direction, a real if modest effect.

Two caveats do the important work:

  • It's additive to training, not a substitute. Every one of those trials paired creatine with resistance training. If you're not lifting, this benefit largely doesn't appear.
  • "Modest" is the right word. A little over a kilogram of lean mass across months of training is a worthwhile nudge, not a transformation.

And more creatine doesn't buy more muscle. A 2025 dose-response meta-analysis of 61 RCTs (1,457 participants) found fat-free-mass gains were flat across dose: +1.35 kg at ≤5 g/day versus +1.37 kg above 5 g/day, with no significant dose-response relationship at all (Ashtary-Larky et al. 2025). For the muscle outcome, 10 g does nothing that 5 g doesn't.

A 2026 network meta-analysis reaffirms the strength story — and closes one stacking question. Pooling 52 RCTs in trained young adults, creatine alone beat placebo on sprint (SMD −0.64), repeated-sprint ability (−0.78), upper-body muscular endurance (0.43), and jump (0.33); adding beta-alanine on top added nothing detectable (Bai et al. 2026). Read that honestly in both directions: these are athletic-performance surrogates in young athletes, not healthspan endpoints — a reason to trust that creatine works, not a new reason to take it. And "beta-alanine adds nothing" is no evidence of synergy, not proof against it — the combined arm was estimable in only one of six outcomes, so the fair word is "unproven," not "disproven." The practical takeaway is small: don't bother stacking beta-alanine, and nothing here changes the ~5 g answer.

The weaker claim: cognition (now its own thread — and still not a dose argument)

This thread has grown enough that the living vault now tracks it on a dedicated creatine-for-cognition map. The short version hasn't changed: the benefit is genuine but thin, and it is not a reason to take more.

Chronic use, older adults. A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found a small overall memory improvement (SMD 0.29, 95% CI 0.04–0.53) concentrated in adults 66–76 (SMD 0.88) and near-zero in younger adults, with high heterogeneity (I² = 66%) (PMID: 35984306). A second systematic review in adults 55+ pointed the same way on a weaker base (PMID: 40971619). Suggestive, not settled.

Acute stress. A single large dose (0.35 g/kg — for a 165 lb adult, a one-off ~26 g, not a daily amount) improved processing speed and memory during 21 hours of sleep deprivation, with brain imaging confirming a transient uptake of creatine's energy metabolites — a small (n=15) but mechanistically clean crossover RCT (Gordji-Nejad et al. 2024). This is a situational rescue (a bad night, jet lag), not evidence for a daily habit.

The higher-dose brain argument has been tested since — and it comes out weaker, not stronger. The idea was that ~10 g/day floods a surplus to the brain. A head-to-head RCT gave healthy young adults 10 g/day, 20 g/day, or placebo for six weeks and measured cognition directly: no benefit and no dose-response — placebo numerically beat the 10 g arm (Moriarty et al. 2023). Then two 2026 papers closed the loop from opposite directions. A critical review first-authored by Darren Candow — the researcher most associated with the higher-dose-for-brain argument — concluded that even a detectable brain-creatine rise likely needs ≥20 g/day, not 10, and that the "muscle saturates first, so the surplus floods the brain" mechanism had no direct human evidence (Candow et al. 2026). A pilot study then confirmed that mechanistic half: 20 g/day for eight weeks did raise brain creatine by ~11% (Smith et al. 2025) — but in an uncontrolled, open-label, 20-person trial whose cognitive gains the authors themselves call uninterpretable against ordinary practice effects. So the 20 g dose gets creatine into the brain and still hasn't been shown to do anything there: target engagement is not benefit, and the only randomized test of that exact link in healthy adults was the null one above. When the leading proponent of an idea publishes the paper walking it back, that's worth noticing.

Why this matters for the dose — and for sleep. The safety evidence below tops out around ~10 g/day; 20 g is double that and outside the reassuring envelope, and the brain-loading pilot's mild side-effect list included sleep disturbance. The living map flags the ≥20 g dose for exactly that reason. Cognition is a possible bonus at the dose you'd take anyway, never a reason to climb toward 20 g.

If you're injured and can't train: the claim that doesn't hold

This is the moment most people reach for creatine — a cast, a brace, six weeks off — and it is the setting where the evidence is least supportive. It got its first dedicated review in 2026, and the finding is a clean split by whether you can load the muscle (Stout & Pabian 2026).

During strict immobilization, creatine does not preserve muscle or strength. In the cleanest test — 30 people, seven days in a full-leg cast, on a 20 g/day loading protocol — muscle cross-sectional area and strength fell identically in the creatine and placebo arms. Creatine got into the muscle; it just didn't stop anything from being lost.

Once you can load the limb again, the picture changes. In rehabilitation with progressive loading, the review reports accelerated recovery of quadriceps size and knee-extensor power, with supportive signals in knee osteoarthritis, Parkinson's and inflammatory myopathies — and nulls at ACL reconstruction, total knee replacement, acute stroke, COPD and peripheral arterial disease, all settings where pain or healing blocks high-effort training.

That pattern is the same conditional running through this whole article, arriving in the setting where it's easiest to forget: creatine is additive to a training stimulus, not a substitute for one. "Take creatine while you're injured so you don't lose muscle" is not supported. "Take creatine while you're rebuilding, if you can actually load the limb" is the version the evidence points at.

Two caveats on tier, because they matter here. This is a narrative mini-review — no systematic search, no risk-of-bias assessment, no pooling — so it sits at the bottom of the evidence hierarchy and cannot move a settled claim on its own; it's included because it's the only synthesis of this question that exists. And its senior author discloses thirty years of supplement-industry funding and an unpaid advisory role with a creatine manufacturer. ⚑ Notably, the immobilization null runs against that interest, which is a reason to take it at face value; the reloading benefits run with it and deserve the usual discount. What the disclosure can't fix is that without a systematic search, you can trust the nulls that are reported and not the completeness of the set.

The bone claim: null — and now null four ways

The pitch that ~8 g/day protects bone doesn't survive the highest-tier evidence. A meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (193 older adults) found creatine plus resistance training produced no greater bone mineral density than training alone — at whole-body, hip, femoral-neck, or lumbar-spine sites (all p > 0.25), including in higher-dose arms (Forbes et al. 2018). The "8 g works" claim traces to a single narrative Letter to the Editor that tallies which trials found any positive bone signal, mostly on bone geometry rather than density, written by authors who co-authored the trials they're counting (Forbes et al. 2022). Real but thin, and outweighed by the pooled null.

Since this article was first written, the same null has shown up from two more directions. A 2026 meta-analysis found that doubling training intensity (≥70% vs <70% 1RM) doesn't detectably move BMD either (lumbar SMD 0.28, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.58) (Shen et al. 2026), and a 2026 GRADE-rated meta of potassium citrate — an entirely different, non-mechanical mechanism — found essentially nothing move on bone-turnover markers, with the authors' own trial-sequential analysis calling the evidence too small to be conclusive (AlSejari et al. 2026). Three mechanisms, three failures to shift bone in a way anyone can measure.

And there is now a fourth, which removes the last easy excuse. The defence of the bone claim has always been that the nulls used too small a dose — the Letter to the Editor above argues 0 of 4 trials worked at 5 g while 5 of 6 worked at 7–9 g. So the test that matters is a higher dose, in an older population, run by people who believe in it. That trial now exists: 10 g/day for 12 weeks in healthy adults aged 45–65, with calibrated DXA, from a lab whose senior author chairs the scientific advisory board of the company that supplied the creatine. No significant effect on bone mineral content, area or density, on any analysis (Chun et al. 2026). Above the disputed dose band, in the right decade, with every incentive pointing the other way — nothing moved.

Two honest caveats keep this fair. Those companion nulls are themselves underpowered, and twelve weeks is short for bone, which remodels over months to years — so the right conclusion is "no supplement here has earned a bone claim," not "bone is unmovable." Whatever bone benefit these studies show comes from the lifting, not the powder.

Safety — the actual "what's the downside"

This thread has now grown enough that the living vault tracks it on a dedicated creatine-safety map, the same way cognition split off earlier. The summary below is unchanged; the map is where the detail keeps moving.

Short version: for a healthy adult, there isn't much of one — at least up to ~10 g/day, which is as high as the reassuring trials actually go (why 20 g is a different question is in the cognition section above).

  • Kidneys — and this is the section that changed most in the last update. A double-blind RCT gave ~10 g/day for three months and found no renal dysfunction — kidney-filtration markers actually trended in the healthy direction (PMID: 18188581). A 2025 review spanning 1–80 g/day over durations up to two years reached the same conclusion (Longobardi et al. 2025). Those were 18 people and a narrative review with industry ties. There is now a meta-analysis of 26 randomised trials and 1,036 people, run by kidney specialists with no competing interests, and it does something the earlier work couldn't (de Souza Almeida et al. 2026).

    Here is the problem it solves. Nobody measures kidney filtration directly in normal practice. A blood test measures creatinine, a waste product your muscles produce at a steady rate, and an equation turns that concentration into an estimated filtration rate. The equation assumes creatinine production is constant — and taking creatine breaks that assumption, because roughly 2% of the creatine in your body spontaneously converts to creatinine every day. More creatinine goes into the blood without your kidney doing anything differently, and the equation reads that as reduced filtration.

    So the study measured filtration two ways in the same trials. Estimated the usual way, from creatinine, it fell by about 10.75 mL/min. Measured with a tracer called Cr-EDTA, which doesn't use creatinine as its signal at all, it didn't fall — if anything it drifted the other way. And four other kidney markers that bypass creatinine — urea, and three measures of protein leaking into urine — were all flat. Real kidney damage capable of cutting filtration by that much would be expected to disturb at least one of them.

    What that means for you, concretely. The number is big enough to matter: the bands doctors use to describe kidney function sit at round figures like 90 and 60, so a shift of ~10 can move a perfectly healthy person into a worse-sounding category on a routine blood panel while nothing at all has happened to their kidneys. The old advice was right and is now worth actually acting on: tell your clinician you take creatine before a kidney panel, so the result gets read correctly the first time rather than unwound later.

    The honest limit, because this article doesn't oversell reassurance either. The tracer comparison that clears creatine is itself a non-significant result (p = 0.06) on a range wide enough to include no change and a modest improvement. A non-significant result means "we didn't detect a difference," not "we proved there isn't one." What makes the artifact explanation convincing isn't that one comparison — it's that four independent markers agree with it. Worth knowing too: every measurement here is a blood or urine marker, not a long-term outcome. No study has followed creatine users for years to see who develops kidney disease. This is strong evidence that the scary-looking test result is a measurement artifact. It is not evidence about decades.

  • Hair loss. The DHT/baldness fear traces to one small uncontrolled 2009 study that never measured hair. The first RCT to test it directly (38 men, 12 weeks) found no change in DHT, testosterone, or any of eight hair-follicle measures (Lak et al. 2025). Myth retired.

  • Gut. The main real side effect, and it's dose-dependent — more likely at single doses above 10 g. Splitting into smaller servings avoids it.

So — how much, and is it worth it?

If you lift, creatine is one of the very few supplements the evidence actually backs: a small, reliable strength and lean-mass benefit with almost no downside. The dose that earns that is about 5 g/day. The trendy 10 g is safe — nothing here argues against it if you want a saturation buffer — but across muscle, brain, and bone there is no reliable human evidence it does more than 5 g in a healthy adult, and every update since has made the higher-dose case weaker, not stronger: the muscle benefit is flat with dose, the bone benefit is now null three independent ways, and the brain argument's own originators have walked it back while a 20 g pilot confirmed only that the dose reaches the brain — not that it does anything once it's there. The one genuinely dose-responsive use is the acute sleep-deprivation dose, and that's an occasional tool, not a daily number.

And if you're hurt, the honest answer is "wait until you can load it." Creatine does not preserve muscle through strict immobilization; its recovery benefit appears once progressive loading resumes (Stout & Pabian 2026).

One more test of the dose question arrived and could not answer it — which is itself worth knowing. The obvious criticism of everything above is that the muscle and brain evidence comes from young men and trained athletes, not from middle-aged adults taking the trendy dose. A 2026 trial finally had the right population and the right dose: 10 g/day for 12 weeks in healthy sedentary adults aged 45–65 (Chun et al. 2026). It reports benefits across body composition, strength and some cognitive measures. ⚠ But it compared 10 g against nothing — never against 5 g — so it says nothing about whether the extra grams did any work. Read it carefully and two further things stand out: the abstract's headline claims arrive with no effect size attached anywhere, and the analysis ran more than twenty outcome tables across four groups at three timepoints with no correction for multiple comparisons, which the authors state themselves. That combination is why this article treats it as a hypothesis generator rather than as an update. The dose answer is untouched: ~5 g/day is still what the comparative evidence supports.

One update to the "downside" column since this article was last revised, and it cuts in creatine's favour without changing the dose answer: the kidney worry is now the best-evidenced safety claim here, backed by 26 randomised trials rather than 18 people, with the apparent drop in filtration shown to be an artifact of how filtration is estimated rather than a change in the kidney (de Souza Almeida et al. 2026). The practical consequence isn't "take more" — it's "mention it before a blood test."

This article deliberately doesn't hand you a prescription — dose is a conversation for you and your clinician, especially the kidney-test caveat above.
