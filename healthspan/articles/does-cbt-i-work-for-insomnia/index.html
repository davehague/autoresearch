Does CBT-I work for insomnia? What the evidence supports — and who it's actually for

Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) has the best evidence of any sleep intervention in this vault — randomised, replicated, drug-free, and durable months after you stop doing it. It is also, on the evidence gathered so far, a treatment for a diagnosis, not a tune-up for someone who already sleeps adequately. Here's what a dozen sources — spanning three years of ingests, one accidental false start, and a genuine methodological saga to isolate CBT-I's own contribution — actually found.

Not medical advice. This summarizes published evidence to inform a conversation with your clinician; it doesn't prescribe a dose, duration, or format.

The founding evidence measured the wrong thing — and that turned out to matter

The first source on this map wasn't about sleep at all. A PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis of 53 RCTs (n=13,608 adults with insomnia) found CBT-I's benefit on depressive symptoms persisted three, six and twelve months after treatment ended — d = −0.35, −0.32 and −0.25 respectively, decaying but never vanishing (Fiori 2026, Sleep Medicine Reviews, PMID 42435604). That's a genuinely unusual shape: almost nothing else in this vault shows a benefit that outlasts the intervention by a year. But it left an obvious gap — CBT-I is prescribed for sleep, and this study never measured sleep.

Closing that gap took five more sources and three months, because every early attempt answered a slightly different question than the one that was asked.

The sleep evidence arrived bundled — then unbundled, in pieces

A 31-RCT network meta-analysis (n=2,457) found "CBT" beating control on sleep quality — but every CBT arm in the network was CBT combined with exercise or qigong; there was no CBT-alone comparison to read (Zhou 2026, Frontiers in Public Health, PMID 42395307). The combination itself is a genuinely good finding — PSQI improved by −2.80 points at moderate certainty, roughly four times the effect of the entire wind-down supplement shelf pooled together (tryptophan, L-theanine, GABA, magnesium, zinc, vitamin D, omega-3 and ashwagandha combined manage −0.70, an interval that never clears the threshold considered clinically meaningful — Mei 2025, 28-RCT meta-analysis, PMID 41470897). But it couldn't say what CBT contributed on its own.

A guideline-consensus summary confirmed CBT-I's isolated sleep efficacy is real — roughly 7 to 8 of 10 patients improve, durably, with minimal side effects — but reported no pooled effect size, only interpretive benchmarks (Yan 2026, JBI evidence summary of 28 guidelines/reviews, PMID 41694129). A routine-care effectiveness study then supplied an actual magnitude — 45% remission post-treatment, 51% at a year — but the numbers were within-group, not placebo-controlled (Öst 2025, Cognitive Behaviour Therapy, PMID 41342528). A guided digital-CBT-I trial then supplied a controlled number at last: insomnia severity fell by d = −1.04 at two months and d = −0.98 at eight — 94% of the effect still present six months after the program ended (Reesen 2026, Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, PMID 42574103). That comparator, though, was a sleep diary — close to doing nothing — so the size of that number is partly a function of how weak the zero was.

A network meta-analysis published this week finally supplied what five prior sources each answered half of: a CBT-alone arm, compared against control, with a pooled effect size and a certainty grade. Across 96 RCTs (n=18,419) testing six digital sleep therapies, digital CBT (dCBT) was the only one to clear the bar for a clinically meaningful improvement in insomnia severity — MD −4.24 points, moderate certainty — while digital mindfulness reached statistical but not clinical significance, and neurofeedback, brief behavioural therapy and circadian-rhythm support showed no effect on anything (Tong 2026, BMC Medicine, network meta-analysis, PMID 42806363). That closes the isolation problem this literature has been chasing since July — for digital delivery. It says nothing about whether the in-person protocol most guidelines were built around performs the same way, and the certainty is moderate, not high: risk of bias in the underlying 96 trials skewed toward "some concerns," heterogeneity on the headline number ran 85–90% across every subgroup tested, and the control arm it beats pools together waitlist, active-control, usual-care and sham conditions into one contrast — a comparator-pooling problem this vault has now flagged on eight separate literatures.

When CBT-I is compared against a real rival, not against nothing

One trial tested CBT-I head-to-head against acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) — a different behavioural approach that trains people to stop struggling against wakefulness rather than change sleep behaviour directly. Against a waitlist, ACT looked strong: SMD −0.67. Against CBT-I, the difference vanished: 0.21, not significant (Barroso 2026, Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, PMID 42547685). Read carefully, that's not proof the two therapies are equivalent — no equivalence margin was pre-specified, and the interval's upper bound is compatible with CBT-I still being meaningfully better. But a binary outcome did separate them: CBT-I put significantly more people into full remission than ACT, risk ratio 0.81 (0.67–0.98). The same therapy scored −0.67 against a weak comparator and 0.21 against a strong one — the comparator changed, not the therapy — a pattern worth remembering any time a single number from this literature looks dramatic.

A surprising twist: does it slow biological ageing?

One randomised trial asked whether treating insomnia slows an epigenetic ageing clock — the first trial of its kind anywhere. The headline: CBT-I's group significantly outperformed an active comparator (sleep education) on DunedinPACE, a pace-of-ageing clock. But the honest story, buried below the headline, is that the CBT-I group didn't actually improve — it stayed flat. The comparator group got significantly worse (Carroll 2026, The Lancet Healthy Longevity, PMID 42586102). The defensible claim from this trial is "CBT-I prevents an acceleration seen in untreated insomnia," not "CBT-I slows biological ageing" — a distinction the abstract doesn't make and the data does. It's an interesting, single-trial result (n=92, capped at suggestive strength) worth knowing about, not a reason to expect a measurable anti-ageing effect.

Who this evidence is actually about

Every trial behind every number above enrolled people with diagnosed insomnia. None tested CBT-I in someone who already sleeps adequately, and this vault won't extrapolate a claim it hasn't ingested. That matters because CBT-I isn't free to try in the way a supplement is: its sleep-restriction component characteristically makes sleep feel worse for a week or two before it gets better, and it's a multi-week structured commitment, not something you passively add. If you don't have a sleep problem, there's no evidence here that CBT-I does anything for you — the honest read is "not applicable," not "mildly beneficial."

If sleep does become a problem worth treating, though, the evidence set above adds up to a clear recommendation: CBT-I is the first thing to reach for. It's the only sleep intervention in this vault with genuinely randomised evidence, the only one whose benefit is still measurable after you stop doing it, and — unlike every supplement on the shelf — there's nothing to buy, no dose to get wrong, and no interaction with anything else in a regimen. If exercise is already part of your routine, the evidence suggests adding CBT-I to it rather than starting from zero — the only sources with a controlled sleep-outcome effect size are CBT combined with exercise, or CBT delivered digitally on its own; there is currently no vault-held evidence for in-person CBT-I as a truly standalone protocol with a controlled number attached.

What this doesn't license

Nothing here supports a specific CBT-I program length, module count, or in-person-versus-digital preference — this literature hasn't compared those directly at the quality level this vault requires. Nothing here supports using CBT-I, or any of its components in isolation, for anything other than the sleep and (secondarily) depressive symptoms it's actually been tested on: a related trial found a single CBT-I component — progressive muscle relaxation — posting a larger raw effect size than CBT-I itself, but on a literature failing its own publication-bias test, with no dose-response relationship across a 37-fold range of session counts, and zero trials in healthy adults. That's a red flag about the measurement, not a discount on CBT-I, and it isn't a reason to substitute one component for the whole therapy.

The bottom line

If you have diagnosed or clinically significant insomnia, CBT-I has the strongest evidence base of any intervention this vault has evaluated for the condition — durable, replicated, and free of the comparator and measurement problems that undercut most of the wind-down supplement shelf. If you sleep adequately already, none of this evidence applies to you, and there's nothing to gain by starting it pre-emptively. Either way, there's no dose to invent and no supplement to buy — the honest recommendation is either "do it, guided by a clinician or a structured program" or "you don't need it," never "take this daily just in case."
