Does vitamin D actually do anything? What the evidence supports — and the one question it still doesn't answer

Short answer: if you are already vitamin-D-replete, no — eight separate randomised outcome families have now failed to find a benefit. If you are genuinely deficient, the honest answer is "we still don't know," and the best-designed attempt yet to find out, published this month, came back null on the endpoint that mattered.

That is a blunt opening for a supplement most adults in temperate climates are told to take, so here is the split before the detail:

  • Cancer and cardiovascular disease, in replete adults: no. A 25,871-person randomised trial found flat nulls on both.
  • Fractures, in replete adults: no, even at nearly ten times that trial's size.
  • Testosterone, mood in deficient young people, and body composition: no, across three more independent randomised tests.
  • Surrogate blood markers — telomere length, cardiometabolic risk factors: small movements, sometimes. None of them have shown up as an actual event prevented.
  • Correcting a genuine deficiency: still unresolved, and the newest randomised attempt — built specifically to answer this — was null on its main outcome.

Everything below names its evidence tier and its magnitude, because on this topic the tier is doing almost all of the work.

The trial that changed the story: VITAL

For years, the case for vitamin D rested on observational studies: people with low blood levels of vitamin D had worse outcomes across almost everything measured. That is the kind of evidence that cannot tell you whether low vitamin D causes the problem, or whether whatever is making someone sick also happens to lower their vitamin D.

VITAL (Vitamin D and OmAga-3 triaL) was built to settle it. A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 25,871 adults, average follow-up 5.3 years, testing 2,000 IU/day of vitamin D3 (Manson 2019, NEJM, PMID 30415629):

  • Cancer: no reduction (hazard ratio 0.96 — a 4% difference too small to distinguish from chance).
  • Major cardiovascular events: no reduction (hazard ratio 0.97).

A companion VITAL analysis on fractures (LeBoff 2022, NEJM, PMID 35939577) found the same pattern: no reduction in total, non-vertebral, or hip fractures (hazard ratios roughly 0.98–1.01) — and, notably, no benefit even in people who started with lower vitamin D levels. That last point matters: if there were a "deficient people benefit more" effect hiding in this trial, VITAL's own subgroup analysis should have found it, and did not.

This is the highest tier of evidence nutrition science has, at a scale most nutrition trials never reach, and it is unambiguous: for adults who are not deficient, routine vitamin D supplementation does not prevent cancer, cardiovascular disease, or fractures.

The surrogates moved. The outcomes that matter did not.

Several smaller studies out of VITAL and elsewhere have found vitamin D nudging measurements in the right direction — without ever showing those measurements translate into anything a person experiences.

A VITAL sub-study of 1,031 participants over 4 years found vitamin D slowed telomere attrition — a marker of cellular aging — by about 140 base pairs, roughly 3–4 years' worth of "cellular age" (Zhu 2025, AJCN). That sounds encouraging until you remember: this is the same trial whose hard outcomes — cancer, cardiovascular events — were flat. A blood marker moved in a trial where the actual disease outcomes did not.

The pattern repeated with a 2026 meta-analysis of 45 randomised trials on cardiometabolic risk factors (Abumweis 2026, Asia Pacific Journal of Clinical Nutrition, PMID 42521227): small, real-looking reductions in LDL cholesterol (about 5 mg/dL), systolic blood pressure (about 2.8 mmHg), and fasting glucose — but effects too small to plausibly explain VITAL's null on actual cardiovascular events at 25,871 people, and inconsistent in an odd way (the blood-pressure and cholesterol gains showed up in people over 55; the glucose gain showed up in people under 55 — a split with no obvious biological story). ⚠ This source is worth reading with caution: it was retrieved abstract-only, from an issue that was not yet formally posted, and its heterogeneity and risk-of-bias results could not be checked.

The rule to take from this: a blood test moving is not the same as an outcome changing, and this vault has seen the same disconnect before with other supplements. Treat any vitamin D "biomarker win" as interesting, not as a reason to expect fewer heart attacks or a longer life.

Three more randomised nulls: testosterone, mood, and body composition

Three further randomised tests have each closed off a specific popular claim:

  • Testosterone: a meta-analysis of RCTs in adult men found no effect on total testosterone, free testosterone, or related hormones — the upper bound of the effect was too small to matter clinically even if it were real (Paez-Allendes 2026, Nutrients). If you are supplementing vitamin D hoping it raises testosterone for muscle-building, the evidence says it does not — that route runs through resistance training and adequate protein, not a vitamin.
  • Depression, in genuinely deficient young people: a randomised trial specifically selected participants aged 10–24 who were both depressed and vitamin-D insufficient — the population most likely to show a benefit if one exists. The pre-specified, clinician-rated primary outcome was flat (Lee 2026, European Journal of Nutrition). A secondary, self-reported anxiety measure did move — but self-report in an unblinded-feeling context is exactly the kind of result that should be discounted relative to a clinician-administered primary that didn't move.
  • Body composition, added to exercise: a dose-response meta-analysis of 28 RCTs (n=1,675) found vitamin D added to an exercise program raised blood vitamin D levels reliably, but changed nothing on body weight, BMI, fat mass, or lean mass (Kazeminasab 2025, Nutrition Reviews). A narrow exception — modest knee-strength gains in some subgroups — appeared in only two of four tested measures with no statistical correction for the multiple comparisons, so it is filed as an open question, not a finding.

Fractures, at a much larger scale: still no

If the fracture case seemed like it might survive on a technicality — maybe VITAL just wasn't big enough — a 2026 systematic review closes that door. Pooling 69 trials and 153,902 people (Massé 2026, BMJ), vitamin D alone was a precise null on every fracture outcome tested, at the highest certainty rating (GRADE HIGH) — any fracture, non-vertebral, vertebral, falls, all landing within a whisker of "no effect," with hip fracture actually trending slightly toward more fractures (not statistically significant).

Combined calcium plus vitamin D did nominally reduce any-fracture risk — but the reduction (about 1 fracture prevented per 100 people treated) fell short of the review's own pre-registered bar for what counts as clinically meaningful, and a sensitivity analysis found the result was substantially carried by a single 1992 trial in institutionalised elderly French women. Remove that one trial, and the combined-therapy signal disappears too.

The one thread that has never been properly tested — until this month

Every result above tested people who were, on average, already vitamin-D-replete or close to it. There has always been one legitimate, unanswered question sitting underneath all these nulls: does correcting a genuine deficiency do anything?

An earlier attempt to answer this used sophisticated statistical modelling (a "target-trial emulation") on UK Biobank data rather than a real randomised trial, and suggested that people who were genuinely deficient might see a meaningful reduction in all-cause mortality from correction (Wang 2026, Clinical Nutrition, PMID 41719624) — an intriguing but observational answer to a question that needed a real trial.

This month, the real trial arrived. TARGET-D (May 2026, European Heart Journal, PMID 42702500) randomised 630 heart-attack survivors — 87% of whom were vitamin-D insufficient at baseline — to usual care or an individually titrated dosing strategy, adjusting vitamin D3 over time to reach and hold blood levels of 40–80 ng/mL. This is a materially different, more deliberate design than VITAL's flat 2,000 IU/day: it targeted people who actually needed correcting, and it dosed to a level rather than a fixed amount.

Over an average 4.2 years, the pre-specified primary outcome — a composite of death, another heart attack, heart-failure hospitalisation, or stroke — did not differ: 15.7% versus 18.4%, hazard ratio 0.85, a difference easily explained by chance (95% confidence interval 0.58–1.24). One individual component, heart attacks specifically, was lower in a way that reached conventional statistical significance — but it was one win among four components tested with no stated correction for multiple comparisons, and two of the other three components (heart failure, stroke) trended in the opposite direction. That is not the shape of a real, coherent effect; it is closer to the shape of noise.

So the honest state of the deficiency question, after the best-designed test yet: still null on the outcome that matters, in the population most likely to benefit, with a dosing strategy built specifically for that population. This does not close the question completely — 630 people is not a huge trial for a rare composite outcome, and the trial could still be missing a real, smaller effect — but it removes the best remaining excuse ("nobody tested deficient people with a real dosing strategy") for expecting one.

So should you take it?

If you are a healthy, generally replete adult, the evidence across eight separate randomised outcome families — cancer, cardiovascular disease, fractures, testosterone, mood, body composition, and now a targeted post-heart-attack composite — says the same thing every time: vitamin D supplementation does not prevent the things it is popularly taken to prevent. The blood-marker wins are real but have never been shown to matter.

If your doctor has told you that you are genuinely deficient (a blood test, not a guess), the honest position is that correction remains a reasonable thing to discuss with your clinician on general grounds — deficiency itself is associated with a range of problems in the observational literature — but the randomised evidence that correcting it changes an outcome is still not there, even after this month's best attempt to find it.

Nobody's dose is being recommended here. The milligram and IU figures above are what the cited trials used, not a suggestion — vitamin D supplementation, dosing, and any decision about testing your levels belongs in a conversation with your clinician, especially since high-dose supplementation carries its own (separately documented) risks.
