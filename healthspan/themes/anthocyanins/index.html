Anthocyanins (berry/plant pigment flavonoids) — nutrition

Status: emerging (2026-08-05) — auto-coined this run, flagged for human review. Coined by an AJCN systematic review pooling 18 prospective cohorts and 65 randomised controlled trials, restricted to healthy participants, PROSPERO-registered, GRADE-rated, at a pre-specified, dietarily achievable dose of ≥50 mg/day. That is the largest randomised evidence base this vault holds for any single food compound — and it produced one durable finding: arteries widen slightly better. ⚠ Part-funded by the US Highbush Blueberry Council, with the senior author on the funder's grant committee.

It opens at emerging for a structural reason, not a thin-evidence one. The single source is appraised mixed, which caps every concept at suggestive, and a suggestive-capped source cannot carry a map to consolidating. Sixty-five randomised trials landing on a word that means "early, mechanistic, or single-group" is the same complaint already open on influenza-vaccine-dose and exercise-for-osteoarthritis in docs/backlog.md, and it is logged there again rather than worked around.

What we believe now

Anthocyanins are a real but narrow vascular intervention with a large observational halo, and the two should not be added together.

What randomisation supports. Across 65 RCTs in healthy people, anthocyanins at ≥50 mg/day improved flow-mediated dilation — how much an artery widens after blood flow is briefly cut off and restored, read by ultrasound — by +1.41 percentage points (95% CI 0.90, 1.91) chronically and +1.50 (1.16, 1.84) acutely, plus a small insulin drop of −2.24 pmol/L (anthocyanins-improve-flow-mediated-dilation-at-dietary-doses). GRADE rated acute FMD strong and chronic FMD moderate. Both intervals clear zero with room to spare. It is a machine-measured, replicated effect.

What randomisation does not support. The same 65 trials were null on blood lipids, blood pressure, fasting glucose and insulin resistance (anthocyanin-randomised-evidence-is-null-on-lipids-blood-pressure-and-glucose). So the answer to "do berries improve the numbers on a routine blood panel?" is no, on four of five outcomes, in the largest trial base available — and the fifth is an ultrasound measurement nobody orders at a physical.

The hard endpoints are all observational, and that is the tension the map exists to hold. The quotable figures — 26% lower cardiovascular disease, 18% lower heart attack, 11% lower type 2 diabetes, 9% lower cardiovascular death, 8% lower hypertension, stroke unaffected — come from 18 cohorts comparing highest against lowest habitual intake, reported as bare relative risks with no absolute risks and no confidence intervals (anthocyanin-hard-endpoint-evidence-is-observational-only). People who eat the most berries are not otherwise identical to people who eat none, and the confounder-adjustment sets could not be verified.

⚑ The correct prior comes from the compound next door, and this vault already holds it. cocoa-flavanols tracks a different flavonoid subclass with the same observational halo — and COSMOS randomised it against cardiovascular events, where the pre-specified primary analysis came back null (cosmos-prespecified-primary-cvd-analysis-remained-null). A flavonoid cohort story that did not survive randomisation is on the shelf. The base rate applies.

What this licenses, and what it does not. ⚑ The doses tested are food doses — 50 mg/day, reachable from ordinary portions of ordinary fruit — which distinguishes this from nearly everything on the vault's supplement shelf. Nothing here supports an extract, a capsule or a powder: no trial in this review tested one against food, no supplement dose is stated, and shared/safety.md forbids inventing one. The map's position is that berries are a slightly better-than-neutral thing to eat, on an artery measurement, with the cardiovascular-event story resting on evidence that has failed this exact test before.
