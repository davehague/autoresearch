Status: emerging (2026-07-06) — auto-coined this run, flagged for human review. A large, long prospective cohort puts a direct, hard-endpoint mortality number on resistance training — the base stimulus the vault previously touched only through supplements additive to it. Opened at emerging by the observational ceiling (you can't randomize decades of training), not by weak evidence.

What we believe now

Resistance training carries its own mortality benefit, with an early dose-response plateau. In three prospective cohorts (147,374 adults, up to 30 years, 35,798 deaths), ~90–119 min/week of resistance training tracked with ~13% lower all-cause, ~19% lower cardiovascular, and ~27% lower neurological-disease mortality, with no further benefit above ~120 min/week. The plateau is the decision-relevant point: a modest, sustainable dose captures most of the benefit — which fits GOAL.md's least-work philosophy and David's aspirational-lifting goal without demanding "more is better."

Four framing points the vault should carry: (1) this is the base lever that creatine-monohydrate and protein-target are additive to — their benefit largely appears only when a training stimulus is present, so resistance-training is the foundation, the supplements are amplifiers; (2) resistance and aerobic training are complementary axes — combining ~30–<45 MET-h/week aerobic with 60–119 min/week resistance training reached the lowest mortality in this cohort (HR ~0.55), so it pairs with, rather than competes against, zone-2-cardio.

(3) HOW to lift now has its own map — resistance-training-programming (forked 2026-07-18). Three axes live there, all measured on strength/function surrogates, none touching this map's mortality claim: load (heavy ≥70% 1RM beats light for lower-limb strength, Shen 2026), effort (non-failure beats failure on dynamic strength, SMD 0.24 — you do not have to grind, Wu 2026), and stability (unstable surfaces add nothing over stable except self-reported confidence, Doan 2026). It also carries the vault's sharpest methodological finding: five underpowered nulls on that literature, five conclusions written as equivalence, each one flattering the authors' own recommendation.

⚠ THE KNEE GATE — restated here at full strength, because a fork must not dilute a safety cap. Shen 2026 recommends ≥70% 1RM lower-limb loading in adults ≥50 while reporting fall incidence RR 2.68 (95% CI 0.65–11.11) and adverse events RR 2.42 (0.66–8.88) — both point estimates above 2, both intervals reaching past an elevenfold increase — and calling it "similar safety profiles." Per shared/study-appraisal.md that is an underpowered null sold as "no effect," a fatal reading error (rt-intensity-safety-comparison-underpowered). Against GOAL.md's hard knee/joint-safe constraint and David's March 2026 patellar contusion, this map keeps safety_flags: [high-dose-risk] and any regimen expression of heavy lower-limb loading stays capped at "proposed — review with your doctor / physio" (shared/decision-rule.md, hard overrides). Unstable-surface variants inherit the same cap. Heavy lifting builds more strength is supported. Heavy lifting is safe for this knee is not. And note this map's own mortality claim needs no heavy load to stand — the cohort measured minutes, not intensity, so the gate costs the recommendation nothing.

(4) Bone: two nulls, one conclusion. No intensity effect on BMD (lumbar SMD 0.28, −0.02 to 0.58 — an underpowered near-miss, not equivalence; rt-intensity-does-not-change-bmd) and no creatine effect beyond training (creatine-bone-bmd-null). Twice the vault has failed to find a lever that moves bone density in a training adult. Not "bone is unmovable" — but nobody should buy a supplement or add plates for their skeleton on this evidence. Detail on resistance-training-programming.

(5) The tissue this lever builds is priced on its own map — fat-free-mass (forked 2026-07-18). Low fat-free mass tracks with all-cause mortality (RR 1.42, falling to 1.31 after the authors' own publication-bias correction), but the effect shrinks as the instrument improves (calf 2.19 > DXA 1.52 > BIA 1.23), which is what a frailty marker looks like rather than a tissue effect (fat-free-mass-measurement-method-gradient). It was forked because it is not an intervention at all — an observational marker literature that kept being read as evidence for this lever. Nothing there shows that building fat-free mass lowers mortality; the case for lifting rests on this map's own cohort evidence. That child map also holds the glp-1 non-transfer refusal.

(7) Lifting reduces depressive symptoms — a mental-health thread, well-supported in direction, not David's number (new 2026-07-17). A meta-analysis of 29 RCTs (n=2,036 adults with clinically diagnosed depression) finds resistance training cuts depressive symptoms at SMD −0.94 (−1.16 to −0.72), larger in primary (−1.12) than comorbid (−0.66) depression, conflict-free with clean publication-bias tests (resistance-training-reduces-depression-symptoms). The direction is well-established behavioral medicine; the magnitude is discounted three ways — I²=80.1%, an open-label design on a self-reported outcome (expectation bias runs toward benefit), and a diagnosed-patient population whose symptom scales have the most room to move (resistance-training-depression-effect-heterogeneous-unblindable). So this is a genuine bonus reason to lift, not a reason to change anything or expect −0.94 in a non-depressed adult, and it does not touch the mortality claim — Status held emerging. It also rhymes with the vault's theme: the same run's supplement mood ingest (ashwagandha, Alsanie 2026) reports impossible effect sizes, while this behavioral lever reports a plausible one on a cleaner base.

(8) Lifting lowers blood pressure — a clinically meaningful surrogate, coherent with the mortality signal (new 2026-07-21). A meta-analysis of 51 RCTs (n=2,025, adults ≥60) finds resistance training reduces systolic BP −6.11 mmHg, diastolic −2.53, mean arterial −4.10 (all p < 0.001), larger in women (resistance-training-lowers-blood-pressure-older-adults). A ~6 mmHg systolic drop is guideline-relevant, and it sits beside this map's cardiovascular-mortality cohort signal as a plausible mechanism — RT plausibly lowers CV risk in part by lowering blood pressure, in the same population. But it is a surrogate (office BP, not events), so it adds a thread, not a tier — it does not consolidate the mortality claim — and the vault could verify only the abstract (CIs, I², and risk of bias behind a paywall), so it is carried suggestive. A bonus reason to lift, not a reason to change anything. Status held emerging.

Why emerging, not higher: the map's hard-endpoint evidence is a cohort association. Resistance-training volume is self-reported and cannot be randomized, so residual confounding (the "healthy exerciser") is possible despite extensive adjustment, and the cohorts are narrow (US health professionals, predominantly white). The new RCT-tier evidence is on a strength surrogate, in adults ≥50 broadly (healthy through osteoporotic), and does not touch the mortality claim — a higher-tier study of a different outcome does not consolidate a map (shared/evidence-model.md). Per the evidence model's deterministic tier gating, this is about as strong as observational evidence gets — held below consolidating by the design ceiling, the same honest gate as sleep-regularity.

  • 2026-07-12 — Ingested Xu 2026 (Annals of Medicine, meta-analysis, 10 RCTs, n=181 athletes) as the coining source for blood-flow-restriction — cross-linked here because it is the first candidate answer to this map's own open safety gate, and it does not answer it.   • Base lever for creatine-monohydrate and protein-target. Both are additive to the resistance-training stimulus, not substitutes — creatine and protein amplify the strength/lean-mass adaptation that training drives. At regimen time, treat resistance-training as the foundation and the two supplements as its amplifiers (and count creatine + protein as one muscle-building stack, per the existing regimen note) — do not present the pills as if they work without the training. Bone caveat (added 2026-07-07): creatine adds no BMD benefit beyond resistance training alone (Forbes 2018 meta-analysis, creatine-bone-bmd-null) — whatever bone-loading benefit exists here comes from the training, not the supplement. Creatine amplifies strength only, not bone density.

Do not choose a load, or a pill, for your skeleton.
  • ⚠ Muscle-preservation countermeasure for glp-1 — the cost is now a number, and a gradient (updated 2026-07-10). Across 19 drugs, tirzepatide strips the most fat (−25.7%) and the most lean mass (−8.3%) (tirzepatide-largest-fat-and-lean-mass-loss) — so this countermeasure scales with the potency of the drug. The new load-axis evidence is directly relevant: preserving strength, not merely mass, is the goal, and strength is what responds to load (high-intensity-rt-beats-moderate-for-lower-limb-strength). Both facts land in the clinician conversation together, since heavy loading carries this map's high-dose-risk flag and GLP-1 is a prescription. Prior framing below.
  • ⚠ Muscle-preservation countermeasure for glp-1 — the cost is now a number (added 2026-07-09). A meta-analysis of 7 placebo-controlled RCTs (n=821) puts GLP-1 lean-mass loss at −1.74 kg, and −5.44 kg for semaglutide (glp1-lean-mass-loss-magnitude, safety_flags: [prescription, interaction]). That is precisely the tissue this map exists to build. Resistance training with the protein-target (± creatine-monohydrate) is the evidence-based countermeasure — and the meta-analysis's own authors conclude drug treatment must be accompanied by nutritional and exercise intervention. Flagged on both maps; a clinician-conversation item since GLP-1 is a prescription. Caveat carried honestly: the pooled endpoint is lean mass, never muscle function, and I²=98% makes the kilograms a direction rather than a precise expectation.
  • On cellular-aging biomarkers, the aerobic axis outperforms this one (added 2026-07-09). In a 16-RCT meta-analysis, telomerase activity rose with aerobic exercise and not significantly with resistance exercise (aerobic-exercise-raises-telomerase-activity). The ordering is the reverse of the mortality evidence, where this map holds a direct cohort signal and zone-2-cardio holds only a fitness→mortality association. Different levers are strong in different places — a reason to run both axes, not to rank one. Only 3 resistance trials contributed, so this is thin, and it is a surrogate; it changes no Status.
  • ⚠ mTOR/AMPK interaction with the pharmacologic-longevity drugs (added 2026-07-07). rapamycin (mTOR inhibition) and metformin (AMPK activation) both act on the exact pathways resistance training uses to build muscle, so either could in principle blunt training/hypertrophy adaptations. Both are prescription/clinician-only; flagged in both directions as a clinician-conversation item. Magnitude is a logged open gap — no number invented.
  • ⚠ Interaction with cold-water-immersion — post-workout cold blunts the adaptation (now graded, Piñero 2024). A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs confirms cold-water immersion in the hours right after a strength session attenuates hypertrophy (comparative SMD −0.22, 95.7% probability of blunting), with a parallel strength/power attenuation in the wider literature — cold suppresses the acute mTOR/satellite-cell anabolic signalling and blood flow that drive adaptation. Now a graded concept (cwi-post-workout-blunts-hypertrophy, safety_flags: [interaction]), carried on both maps and the regimen; the rule is a timing one (keep cold plunging off the post-lift window — non-lifting days or well separated). Base is small/young-male → magnitude suggestive, direction well-established (shared/safety.md).
  • ⚠ The cold-water-immersion timing gate matters MORE the harder you train (added 2026-07-10). Post-workout cold blunts the hypertrophy adaptation (cwi-post-workout-blunts-hypertrophy, comparative SMD −0.22). Now that the vault holds evidence that high-intensity loading is what drives the strength adaptation (high-intensity-rt-beats-moderate-for-lower-limb-strength), the cost of mistiming a plunge is larger, not smaller: the harder the session, the more adaptation there is to blunt. Same rule, sharper reason — keep cold plunging off the post-lift window. Carried on both maps.
  • ⚠ The cold-water timing gate now has a SECOND clause, on a second timescale (new 2026-07-22). Zhu 2026 (cold-water-immersion, 22 RCTs, n=375) finds a single cold bout transiently impairs explosive power — CMJ g = −0.68 (95% CI −1.16 to −0.20) immediately post-exercise, resolving by 24–48 h, while maximal strength recovery is a moderate-certainty null (g = 0.08) (cold-water-immersion-transiently-impairs-explosive-power, cold-water-immersion-does-not-speed-strength-recovery). So the rule reads in both directions now: don't plunge in the hours after a session (chronic adaptation cost), and don't plunge in the hours before anything power-dependent (acute performance cost). These are independent findings, not a replication — Piñero measured chronic hypertrophy across training blocks, Zhu measured acute performance within 72 h and explicitly disclaims any statement about long-term adaptation. Two datasets, two timescales, one direction; the vault records the convergence and refuses to count it twice. Base is suggestive (362 of 375 participants were men). Carried on both maps.
  • ⚠ Training is what protects lean tissue during a calorie deficit — and mixed/strength beats endurance (new 2026-07-22). Deller 2026 (fat-free-mass, 34 RCTs, n=1,455): adding exercise to calorie restriction preserves +0.87 kg of fat-free mass, roughly half the loss, without costing weight loss — and meta-regression shows the benefit is not an artefact of losing less weight (R² = 0% across three models) (exercise-preserves-fat-free-mass-during-calorie-restriction). The network ranks mixed > strength > endurance, with endurance alone failing to clear significance (mixed-and-strength-training-outrank-endurance-for-preserving-lean-mass). This is this map's long-standing countermeasure claim, arriving with randomized backing on the dieting population. Hold the magnitude loosely — the authors detected publication bias on that very outcome and left it uncorrected — and note it is fat-free mass, not muscle: only 2 of 34 trials measured skeletal muscle mass. It does not transfer to glp-1; that refusal is unchanged and recorded on fat-free-mass.
  • Complementary with, and NOT a substitute for, sedentary-behavior (new 2026-07-10). An umbrella review of 30 reviews (n=2,778,595) finds sedentary time predicts all-cause mortality even after adjusting for physical activity (sedentary-time-independent-of-exercise-mortality-risk). A structured strength session does not buy back the seated hours around it. Two levers, two slots — not one.
  • ⚠ LOAD × EFFORT — the two flagged axes compose, and the composition is the practical answer (new 2026-07-11). These are different axes and must not be conflated: Shen 2026 is about how heavy (%1RM); Wu 2026 is about how close to failure within a set. Wu tests performance only — it never measures falls or injury, so it neither confirms nor refutes Shen's fall-risk signal, and it does not soften the high-dose-risk flag. But they compose in an obvious and useful way: the reps that most degrade technique are the last ones before failure, and those are precisely the reps Wu's evidence says you can skip at no cost to dynamic strength. Stating this clearly: that composition is this vault's inference, not either paper's finding (shared/safety.md — a plausible mechanism is not a graded finding). It is offered to the physio conversation, not as a safety claim. The flag stands; heavy lower-limb loading remains "proposed — review with your doctor/physio."
  • ⚠ The fat-free-mass ↔ glp-1 boundary — the leap the vault refuses (new 2026-07-11). It is tempting to chain two facts the vault now holds — GLP-1 drugs strip 1.7–5.4 kg of lean mass and low fat-free mass tracks with 42% higher mortality — into "GLP-1 lean-mass loss will kill you." That chain does not hold, and the vault states so explicitly. Haas 2026 measured untreated community-dwelling adults, where low lean mass is substantially a marker of illness and frailty (its own instrument gradient says so); GLP-1 lean-mass loss is pharmacologic and accompanies large fat loss in a treated population — a different mechanism in a different population. And the vault's RCT-tier GLP-1 evidence points the other way: SELECT found lower mortality on the drug despite the lean-mass cost. The honest read is that Haas raises the stakes of the muscle-preservation countermeasure (lift + protein-target + creatine-monohydrate) without grading a harm. No safety_flags are set on this basis, and the muscle-function gap — no trial has measured strength, power, or physical function during GLP-1 treatment — is not narrowed by this source. Logged in docs/open-gaps-log.md. Carried identically on glp-1.
  • ⚠ blood-flow-restriction is the first candidate answer to this map's knee gate — and it is NOT one yet (new 2026-07-12). This map caps heavy lower-limb loading at "proposed — review with your doctor/physio" because Shen's fall-risk comparison was an underpowered null (rt-intensity-safety-comparison-underpowered). The obvious workaround is low-load training with occlusion, and Xu 2026 is the vault's first BFR source: adding BFR raised lower-limb strength SMD 1.09 (0.52–1.66) (bfr-adds-lower-limb-strength-in-athletes). Three reasons it does not lift the gate. (1) The trials added BFR to training in healthy young athletes (n=181 total) — none tested it as a substitute for heavy load in a knee-compromised adult, which is the only question that matters here. (2) The functional endpoint was null (bfr-sport-performance-null). (3) No adverse events were reported in any of the 10 trials of a technique that restricts arterial inflow (bfr-safety-not-established-in-trials, safety_flags: [high-dose-risk]). Cross-linked on both maps; no regimen change.

    ⚠ Updated 2026-07-13 — the gate has now been properly tested, and it STILL stands, for a better reason. Hou 2026 supplies what yesterday's objection demanded: 11 RCTs, n=508 patients with patellofemoral pain — real knees, not collegiate athletes. The population objection is answered. What the answer says is that BFR does something other than what this gate needed. It is a clinically meaningful analgesic during loaded knee tasks (single-leg squat MD 2.13, deep squat 2.38, step-down 2.05 — above the paper's own cited MCID, sustained 45 min post-exercise; bfr-reduces-patellofemoral-pain-during-loaded-tasks), with a small strength gain (SMD 0.28) and no improvement in patient-reported knee function (AKPS MD 1.93, −0.52 to 4.38, I²=0% — an interval whose upper bound falls below the instrument's minimal important change, so this null is informative, not underpowered: bfr-knee-function-null-in-patients).

    The authors close the question in their own words: BFR is "an adjunctive load-management strategy for patients who have difficulty tolerating higher-load exercise, rather than a replacement" (bfr-is-adjunct-not-substitute-for-heavy-load) — and they explain their own functional null by noting that improvement "may depend more on the overall quality of exercise delivery and adherence than on any single technique."

    So: the exercise fixes the knee; the cuff makes the exercise possible. Nobody in 21 randomised trials has tested low-load BFR as a substitute for heavy load — Hou concedes its own comparisons were not dose-matched — so this gate does not move. And the safety flag gets stronger grounds, not weaker: across two independent syntheses and 21 RCTs, there is still no adverse-event tally (bfr-safety-not-established-in-trials). Heavy lower-limb loading stays capped at "proposed — review with your doctor/physio," and BFR joins it there — now as a specific, answerable physio question rather than a theoretical one.

    ⚠ Updated 2026-07-15 — the gate SOFTENS for the first time, on four small trials. Chen 2025 (PLoS One, 12 RCTs, n=642 middle-aged/elderly OA/TKA patients) finally runs the head-to-head this gate has wanted: low-load BFR vs high-intensity resistance training, strength SMD −0.11 (−0.75 to 0.53), p = 0.74 — no significant difference, in David's own population (bfr-matches-heavy-load-strength-in-knee-rehab). For the first time the substitute question is not untested — and it points toward occlusion matching heavy load for strength. But it is 4 trials, n=123, the smallest cell in the paper: an underpowered null, not demonstrated non-inferiority, and the vault refuses to read it as equivalence any more than it read Shen's, Wu's, or Jiao's underpowered nulls that way — especially when the convenient reading is "the gate opens." So the gate softens to a sharper physio question — is low-load BFR a sensible way to keep loading this knee, given in-population evidence it may match heavy load? — rather than lifting. Heavy loading stays capped; the safety absence has now replicated a third time (33 RCTs, three syntheses, zero adverse-event tallies).

  • ⚠ Stretching now has its own map, and it joins this knee's rehab sequence (new 2026-07-15). stretching was coined this run (Weerasinghe 2026) — a real range-of-motion benefit, an overstated injury-prevention claim, in young athletes. GOAL.md gates David's pickleball return on consistent stretching, and three maps now speak to his knee: restore range (stretching) → tolerable load (blood-flow-restriction) → strength (this map, heavy load capped). A rehab sequence, not competing levers. Note the irony the vault keeps live: stretching first entered as the near-sham control that "won" Jiao's cognition SUCRA leaderboard (exercise-type-cognition-ranking-not-supported) — the vault refused to coin from that artifact and waited for direct stretching evidence, which is what stretching is built on.
  • The cognition question this map does NOT get to claim (new 2026-07-12). Jiao 2026 ranks resistance training highly for cognition in older adults; the same paper's Results say no modality significantly beat any other, and its top-ranked modality is stretching (exercise-type-cognition-ranking-not-supported). RT's cognitive benefit is therefore not established in this vault — the honest cognition signal remains modality-agnostic (exercise-improves-cognition-low-certainty-modality-agnostic) and the hard-endpoint dementia association still belongs to daily-steps (HR 0.62), not here.
  • A mental-health thread that joins the vault's behavioral mood levers (new 2026-07-17). Chang 2025 puts resistance training alongside cbt-insomnia (Fiori 2026: CBT-I's depression benefit is durable past treatment, cbt-i-durable-depression-benefit) as a non-pharmacologic lever with a real mood effect — a skill/behavior, not a pill. It composes with purpose-in-life, whose ~30% mortality benefit survived depression adjustment (the reverse-causation killer): the behaviors that lower depressive symptoms and the sense of purpose that predicts survival are plausibly the same life, not two ledgers. Contrast the same run's supplement mood ingest ashwagandha (ashwagandha-effect-sizes-implausibly-large, impossible SMDs, industry-adjacent) — the free behavior carries the cleaner evidence, again. A framing/priority note, no safety edge, no co-recommendation.
  • Global-ripple: no safety interaction found with other vault interventions. A mild timing tension with intermittent-fasting (fasted training + a compressed protein window can undercut the training benefit) is noted on that map. The proximity-to-failure and fat-free-mass evidence adds no interaction with any supplement, drug, or recovery map; the BFR evidence adds the occlusion-safety watch item above (checked against all 35 maps, including this run's three new ones).   • ⚠ Supervised multicomponent training reverses measurable frailty in 80-year-olds — bundled with a supplement, so unattributable (new 2026-07-19). Olaso-Gonzalez 2026 (RCT, n=47 frail adults, mean age ~80) delivered supervised resistance/aerobic/balance training 3×/week for 24 weeks plus a branded 16 g-protein supplement against habitual care, improving frailty (SHARE-FI −2.5, p<0.0001), grip, gait speed, balance and independence (multidomain-intervention-improves-frailty-and-function-frail-elders, filed on multidomain-lifestyle). The components cannot be separated — the authors concede it, and the supplement's manufacturer part-funded the trial (multidomain-nutrition-exercise-bundle-not-separable): the seventh bundled-arm attribution failure the vault has caught. The most parsimonious reading is that the training did most of it, and "most parsimonious" is not "measured." Nothing is imported to this map's mortality claim — different population (frail 80-year-olds), different endpoints (function surrogates), 6 months. Recorded as directionally consistent with everything here and evidentially separate from it.
