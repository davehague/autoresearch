What we believe now

Resistance training carries its own mortality benefit, with an early dose-response plateau. In three prospective cohorts (147,374 adults, up to 30 years, 35,798 deaths), ~90–119 min/week of resistance training tracked with ~13% lower all-cause, ~19% lower cardiovascular, and ~27% lower neurological-disease mortality, with no further benefit above ~120 min/week. The plateau is the decision-relevant point: a modest, sustainable dose captures most of the benefit — which fits GOAL.md's least-work philosophy and David's aspirational-lifting goal without demanding "more is better."

Four framing points the vault should carry: (1) this is the base lever that creatine-monohydrate and protein-target are additive to — their benefit largely appears only when a training stimulus is present, so resistance-training is the foundation, the supplements are amplifiers; (2) resistance and aerobic training are complementary axes — combining ~30–<45 MET-h/week aerobic with 60–119 min/week resistance training reached the lowest mortality in this cohort (HR ~0.55), so it pairs with, rather than competes against, zone-2-cardio. ⚑ Point (2) is now backed by randomised evidence, and its mortality half is simultaneously bounded — see the 2026-08-03 entry below.

⚑ NEW 2026-08-03 — the pairing claim gets its first randomised test, and the two halves come back differently. Gois 2026 pooled 61 RCTs and 5,388 participants with coronary heart disease, comparing combined aerobic-plus-resistance training against each modality alone and against not exercising.

The fitness half is corroborated, and by the more interesting comparison. Against aerobic training alone, adding resistance work raised peak VO₂ by SMD 0.28 (0.10–0.46) across 18 trials, rising to 0.35 (0.17–0.53) in the cleaner relative-VO₂ subgroup where heterogeneity falls to I² = 37% (adding-resistance-training-to-aerobic-training-raises-peak-vo2). Lifting improved the outcome that supposedly belongs to cardio. Six-minute walk, 1-RM, peak torque and quality of life moved the same way. This is the vault's third independent line saying doing both beats doing either — after this map's own mortality cohort (aerobic-plus-resistance-lowest-mortality) and a Bayesian network meta-analysis on glycaemic control (combined-aerobic-resistance-tops-glycemic-control-in-older-adults) — and the first to say it from randomised trials on aerobic capacity itself.

⚠ The mortality half is bounded rather than corroborated, and the abstract obscures it. The headline is a composite RR 0.40 (0.22–0.72). Split: cardiac death RR 0.31 (0.15–0.66), p = 0.002 — real; all-cause death RR 0.61 (0.23–1.59), p = 0.31 — null, on an interval spanning a 77% reduction to a 59% increase (combined-training-mortality-benefit-is-cardiac-death-not-all-cause). The composite also pools a subset with its superset — cardiac deaths are all-cause deaths — across overlapping trials, so it is not a clean estimate and this map carries the components, not the composite. The honest position: this map's mortality claim remains observational, and the first randomised all-cause test of the pairing did not reach significance. That is not a refutation — 5 trials, 889 people, roughly 16-week interventions — but it is the counterweight, and the map states it rather than filing it.

⚠ And the population is not this map's population. Every participant had established coronary heart disease, with a 5.8% control-arm death rate. Relative risks applied to a healthy 40-something's baseline produce tiny absolute effects. The direction transfers; the magnitude does not, and Status is held emerging — a secondary-prevention corpus in which 41 of 61 trials did not report allocation concealment does not upgrade a healthy-adult map.

(4) Bone: two nulls, one conclusion. No intensity effect on BMD (lumbar SMD 0.28, −0.02 to 0.58 — an underpowered near-miss, not equivalence; rt-intensity-does-not-change-bmd) and no creatine effect beyond training (creatine-bone-bmd-null). Twice the vault has failed to find a lever that moves bone density in a training adult. Not "bone is unmovable" — but nobody should buy a supplement or add plates for their skeleton on this evidence. (5) The tissue this lever builds is priced on its own map — fat-free-mass (forked 2026-07-18). Low fat-free mass tracks with all-cause mortality (RR 1.42, falling to 1.31 after the authors' own publication-bias correction), but the effect shrinks as the instrument improves (calf 2.19 > DXA 1.52 > BIA 1.23), which is what a frailty marker looks like rather than a tissue effect (fat-free-mass-measurement-method-gradient). It was forked because it is not an intervention at all — an observational marker literature that kept being read as evidence for this lever. Nothing there shows that building fat-free mass lowers mortality; the case for lifting rests on this map's own cohort evidence. That child map also holds the glp-1 non-transfer refusal.

(7) Lifting reduces depressive symptoms — a mental-health thread, well-supported in direction, not David's number (new 2026-07-17). A meta-analysis of 29 RCTs (n=2,036 adults with clinically diagnosed depression) finds resistance training cuts depressive symptoms at SMD −0.94 (−1.16 to −0.72), larger in primary (−1.12) than comorbid (−0.66) depression, conflict-free with clean publication-bias tests (resistance-training-reduces-depression-symptoms). The direction is well-established behavioral medicine; the magnitude is discounted three ways — I²=80.1%, an open-label design on a self-reported outcome (expectation bias runs toward benefit), and a diagnosed-patient population whose symptom scales have the most room to move (resistance-training-depression-effect-heterogeneous-unblindable). So this is a genuine bonus reason to lift, not a reason to change anything or expect −0.94 in a non-depressed adult, and it does not touch the mortality claim — Status held emerging. It also rhymes with the vault's theme: the same run's supplement mood ingest (ashwagandha, Alsanie 2026) reports impossible effect sizes, while this behavioral lever reports a plausible one on a cleaner base.

(8) Lifting lowers blood pressure — a clinically meaningful surrogate, coherent with the mortality signal (new 2026-07-21). A meta-analysis of 51 RCTs (n=2,025, adults ≥60) finds resistance training reduces systolic BP −6.11 mmHg, diastolic −2.53, mean arterial −4.10 (all p < 0.001), larger in women (resistance-training-lowers-blood-pressure-older-adults). A ~6 mmHg systolic drop is guideline-relevant, and it sits beside this map's cardiovascular-mortality cohort signal as a plausible mechanism — RT plausibly lowers CV risk in part by lowering blood pressure, in the same population. But it is a surrogate (office BP, not events), so it adds a thread, not a tier — it does not consolidate the mortality claim — and the vault could verify only the abstract (CIs, I², and risk of bias behind a paywall), so it is carried suggestive. A bonus reason to lift, not a reason to change anything. Status held emerging.

Why emerging, not higher: the map's hard-endpoint evidence is a cohort association. Resistance-training volume is self-reported and cannot be randomized, so residual confounding (the "healthy exerciser") is possible despite extensive adjustment, and the cohorts are narrow (US health professionals, predominantly white). The new RCT-tier evidence is on a strength surrogate, in adults ≥50 broadly (healthy through osteoporotic), and does not touch the mortality claim — a higher-tier study of a different outcome does not consolidate a map (shared/evidence-model.md). Per the evidence model's deterministic tier gating, this is about as strong as observational evidence gets — held below consolidating by the design ceiling, the same honest gate as sleep-regularity.
