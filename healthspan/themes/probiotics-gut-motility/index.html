Probiotics for gut motility — supplements

Status: emerging (2026-11-11) — ⚑ auto-coined this run, flagged for human review. Coined from a single randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (Zhao 2026, Nature Communications, n=73 adults ≥60 with Rome IV functional constipation and objectively confirmed slow transit) testing a named probiotic strain, Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BL-99, against age-related intestinal hypomotility. This is a different claim from probiotics-metabolic (metabolic-syndrome markers: weight, waist circumference, lipids) and probiotics-sleep (sleep-quality indices) — a third, distinct outcome domain for probiotics in this vault, warranting its own map rather than blurring three different Statuses together.

What we believe now

One well-run, properly powered RCT found a large objective effect, with an inconsistent symptom-score picture and a plausible animal/cell-culture mechanism attached. A 4-week course of BL-99 (5×10¹⁰ CFU/day) cut whole-gut transit time by 28.6 hours more than placebo (95% CI −36.8 to −20.4, P<0.001) in older adults with confirmed slow transit — against a ≥59-hour baseline, a large and clinically obvious effect, not a marginal shift. Bowel-movement frequency rose correspondingly. But the patient-reported symptom and quality-of-life scores did not move as cleanly: one symptom score needed a post-hoc correction for a baseline imbalance to reach significance, and quality of life showed no effect at all. The trial's own mouse/cell-culture arm proposes a specific mechanism — BL-99 preserves a bile acid (taurochenodeoxycholic acid) that restores contractile-protein expression in colonic smooth muscle — tested in aged mice, not in the human participants.

This is one trial, one strain, one dose, 4 weeks, one set of affiliated institutions. Durability beyond 4 weeks, generalizability beyond this population, and whether the objective transit improvement reliably translates into how people actually feel are all open. No dose or product is recommended here — the trial's dose is recorded as the study's own, never as advice (shared/safety.md).

A loose thematic adjacency, not an interaction: GLP-1 receptor agonists cause constipation as a known GI side effect (glp-1); this map's mechanism (restoring colonic motility) is plausibly relevant to that population, but no source in this vault has tested a probiotic against GLP-1-associated constipation specifically — noted as an open question, not graded as evidence or a safety_flags entry.
