Creatine monohydrate — supplements

Status: consolidating (2026-07-07; forked 2026-07-08 and 2026-08-02) — high-tier RCT evidence, replicated, real effect, no live rebuttal. The best-evidenced supplement in the seed set. This map is the hub for two remaining sub-threads — strength/lean mass (strong) and bone (BMD-null / geometry-open). Two threads have been carved off: the cognition thread was auto-forked 2026-07-08 into creatine-cognition (at 12 bullets / 4 sub-threads), and the safety/renal thread was auto-forked 2026-08-02 into creatine-safety (at 11 bullets / 3 sub-threads). The live question across the tissues here is dose, and the evidence says the benefit of >5 g/day is unproven.

Sub-threads: (1) strength & lean-mass (additive to resistance training, flat above ~5 g/day) · (2) bone (BMD-null anchor; thin, contested high-dose geometry question) [reviewed 2026-08-02 @ 8 bullets]

What we believe now

Creatine monohydrate is safe and effective at ~5 g/day. The popular case for a higher 10 g/day maintenance dose — that "greedy" muscle saturates first, so a surplus is needed to reach brain and bone — does not survive the evidence, and has been undercut largely by its own leading proponents.

  • (1) Strength & lean mass (strong) — flat above ~5 g/day. Creatine alongside resistance training reliably adds a modest increment of lean mass and strength (Chilibeck 2017: +1.37 kg lean mass). A 61-RCT dose-response meta-analysis (Ashtary-Larky 2025, n=1,457) shows fat-free-mass gain is flat across dose — +1.35 kg at ≤5 g/day vs +1.37 kg at >5 g/day (p=0.965). For muscle, 10 g buys nothing over 5 g. Additive to training, not a substitute — and now sourced directly (Gu 2026, 37 RCTs, context-stratified): with resistance training FFM +3.39 kg / LBM +2.70 kg / squat 1RM +9.58 kg; without training the same outcomes are flat and null (FFM −0.89 kg, p=0.566). The muscle gain is the training's, amplified by creatine, and it vanishes when the training does. Anaerobic (Wingate) power rose in both contexts (creatine-lean-mass-requires-resistance-training).
  • Cognition → forked to creatine-cognition (2026-07-08). The cognition sub-thread — suggestive older-adult memory + an acute sleep-deprivation rescue, but a healthy-adult null and an unproven ≥20 g brain-loading dose — is now its own child map. Bottom line carried forward: cognition is a possible bonus, not a reason to raise the dose. See creatine-cognition.
  • (2) Bone (BMD null; geometry an open, thin question). The highest-tier evidence — a meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (Forbes 2018, n=193) — finds creatine + resistance training gives no greater BMD than training alone at any site (whole-body, hip, femoral neck, lumbar spine; all p>0.25), including in higher-dose arms. The "≥8 g builds bone" claim traces to a single narrative Letter to the Editor (Forbes 2022) that tallies 5 g → 0/4 trials positive vs ~7–9 g → 5/6 "positive" — but on heterogeneous bone geometry/ content outcomes (not BMD), with one high-dose trial flatly null, severe industry conflicts, and authors who co-wrote the very trials they grade. Per highest-tier-wins, BMD-null anchors; high-dose geometry is a thin, non-independent open question, not established.
  • Safety/renal → forked to creatine-safety (2026-08-02). The safety thread — renal markers, the creatinine lab artifact, GI tolerability and the retired DHT/hair-loss fear — is now its own child map. Bottom line carried forward: creatine at ~5–10 g/day is safe, the kidney scare is a measurement artifact rather than kidney damage (estimated GFR falls ~10.75 mL/min while tracer-measured GFR does not move, with four creatinine-independent markers flat), and the one thing worth doing about it is telling a clinician you supplement before a kidney panel. The safety evidence tops out at ~10 g/day and does not cover the 20 g brain-loading dose on creatine-cognition. See creatine-safety.

On the 10 g/day question (David's use case) — the honest bottom line. Take creatine: it is safe and well-evidenced. But the specific case for 10 g over 5 g has collapsed across all three target tissues, and mostly by the hand of the researchers who advanced it (Candow's group authored the dose-flat muscle meta, the null cognition-dose RCT, and the review saying 10 g is too little for the brain). The evidence says: ~5 g/day is the maintenance dose that delivers the muscle, and there is no reliable human evidence that 10 g adds muscle, cognition, or bone in a healthy adult. 10 g remains safe — if David prefers it as a saturation buffer or "insurance," nothing here argues against it (renal/hair clean; split AM/PM for GI) — but it should not be expected to outperform 5 g. The two genuinely dose-responsive ideas are (a) the acute sleep-deprivation "stress dose" (~0.35 g/kg single dose, not a daily regimen) and (b) an unproven ≥20 g brain-loading threshold that no healthy-adult outcome trial has validated. Not medical advice; no dose is prescribed here — this informs a clinician conversation.

  • 2026-08-27 — Ingested Bučević Popović 2026 (oral-creatine-bioavailability-rct-reporting-meta-research-bucevic-popovic-2026, Nutrients, meta-research: a complete PRISMA census of 357 reports mapping to 343 distinct creatine RCTs, 1994–2023; prospectively protocolled, academic funding, no conflicts, senior author a meta-research methodologist). Not an effect study — a census of what the creatine literature has and has not measured, and it lands on the live question this map has carried for a month: the dose. ⚑ The mechanism behind the vault's dose-flat finding now has a name. Creatine enters tissue through the sodium/chloride-dependent transporter CT1 (SLC6A8), which is down-regulated by the creatine already inside the cell; once stores fill, surplus blood creatine is not taken up and is excreted. A saturable transporter is exactly what produces the plateau creatine-dose-response-strength measured empirically (+1.35 kg FFM at ≤5 g/day vs +1.37 kg at >5 g/day, p = 0.965) — an independent group, working on a different question, describing the machinery that makes the flat line (creatine-uptake-is-transporter-limited-not-absorption-limited). The same physiology sharpens rather than rescues the brain-dose argument on creatine-cognition: brain creatine rises 5–15% against muscle's 20–40%, but the cause is sparse transporter density at the blood–brain barrier, not muscle out-competing the brain for a shared pool — so the ceiling is real and a bigger oral dose does not lift it. ⚑ Second finding, and it settles a consumer question this map had never addressed: the "more bioavailable creatine" claim has never actually been tested. Only 5 of 343 trials (1.5%) ever compared two creatine formulations and 3 (0.9%) did so while discussing bioavailability — monohydrate versus creatyl-L-leucine, creatine nitrate, and PEG-creatine hydrochloride. Monohydrate was the only arm to gain significantly in all three, and in all three it was handed 1.3–4.3× more creatine by moles (3 g/day of monohydrate is 20.11 mmol against creatine nitrate's 7.73 or 15.45). So neither "the premium forms are better" nor "monohydrate is better" has been demonstrated (every-head-to-head-creatine-formulation-trial-gave-monohydrate-the-larger-molar-dose). ⚑ Third, a calibration for everything on this map: median 24 participants randomised, median 4-week follow-up, 18.2% reporting a registered protocol, and 15.5% never stating which chemical form of creatine they used (the-creatine-trial-literature-is-small-short-and-largely-unregistered). That does not overturn the meta-analytic findings — pooling small short trials is what meta-analysis is for — but it is the right frame for how much any single new creatine trial should move anything. Only 2 of 343 trials ever aimed at raising brain creatine, which is the structural reason the 20 g brain-loading dose stays unproven: the trials that would prove it have essentially never been run. reliable (a census of documents estimates no effect, so no fatal red flag can fire); extends; Status unchanged (consolidating) and no dose changes — the vault's answer stays ~5 g/day.

  • The bone thread is now closed from a second direction (added 2026-07-10). creatine-bone-bmd-null established that creatine + resistance training gives no greater BMD than training alone.
