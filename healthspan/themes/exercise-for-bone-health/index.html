Exercise for bone health — which modality, and how much (exercise)

Status: emerging (2026-09-10) — ⚑ auto-coined this run, flagged for human review. Coined from a single (but very large) PROSPERO-registered hierarchical Bayesian network meta-analysis: 124 RCTs, 18,429 participants, published in the BMJ. Structurally homeless the way detraining and physical-activity-domains were: the question — what exercise, and how much, maintains bone mineral density and cuts fracture risk — cuts across daily-steps (walking dose), resistance-training-programming (resistance dose/modality), weighted-vest (this vault's prior bone-density evidence, all of it null about add-ons) and tai-chi (mind-body exercise) and was unfindable filed under any single one. git is the undo.

What we believe now

Brisk walking/jogging and combined aerobic-resistance training modestly raise bone mineral density; mixed aerobic exercise and mind-body practice modestly cut fracture risk; resistance training alone does not clear significance for either, in this network. Every estimate is honestly rated low-to-moderate certainty by the authors' own CINeMA assessment — a strong design (huge N, pre-registered, dose-response modeled, government-funded, no industry conflict) producing a hedged, not a strong, answer.

The headline reverses a reading this vault has carried since weighted-vest. Four times before tonight, this vault asked "does X add bone benefit on top of training" and got a null: creatine on top of resistance training (creatine-bone-bmd-null), raising training intensity (rt-intensity-does-not-change-bmd), a network ranking sites that didn't differ (bone-density-network-ranks-sites-where-no-intervention-differed), potassium citrate (potassium-citrate-bone-nulls-honestly-underpowered), and a weighted vest on top of the same exercise (weighted-vest-adds-no-bone-density-once-the-exercise-is-controlled-for). The reading was: loading the skeleton is what moves bone, and the accessories sold alongside the loading do not add to it. Tonight's source measures the base exercise itself, against non-exercise control, and does find a bone-density effect — for walking/jogging and combined training. The two readings are compatible (the accessory nulls were never about whether exercise works, only about whether an add-on improves on it) but this is the vault's first positive, base-exercise bone finding, not another null about an add-on.

⚠ The one finding likely to surprise anyone who has been told "lift heavy for bone": resistance training alone did not clear significance for BMD in this network. Only combined aerobic-resistance training did, at a magnitude statistically indistinguishable from walking/jogging alone (combined-aerobic-resistance-exercise-improves-bone-density-at-moderate-certainty). This does not contradict resistance-training-programming's strength/function evidence — it is a different outcome (bone density, not strength or mortality) — but it is a real tension worth carrying into that map's next reconciliation, not smoothing over.

Dose: roughly 400 METs-min/week for lumbar spine, ~600 for femoral neck/total hip — about 2–3 hours/week of brisk walking or jogging, comfortably inside a range David is close to hitting already on daily-steps grounds (exercise-bone-density-dose-response-is-non-linear-with-a-named-threshold).

⚠ For David (GOAL.md: least work for maximum benefit, adherence-first, knee/joint-safe): nothing enters the regimen from a single emerging map, but the shape is favorable — the top modality for bone (brisk walking) is already his lowest-friction lever, not a new purchase or a new joint-loading practice. Jogging carries more impact than walking; at the current knee-recovery stage, brisk walking is the knee-safer route to the same dose-response curve, and this map does not argue for jogging specifically over walking (their point estimates overlap).
