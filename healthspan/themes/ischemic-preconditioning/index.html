Ischemic preconditioning (cuff-based blood-flow occlusion before exercise) — recovery & mobility

Status: emerging (2026-11-28) — ⚑ auto-coined this run, flagged for human review. The vault's third Recovery & mobility lever (sibling to cold-water-immersion and sauna-heat), and mechanistically adjacent to blood-flow-restriction (same cuff-occlusion tool, opposite timing and purpose: BFR occludes during training to add a metabolic-stress growth stimulus; IPC here is applied before exercise specifically to blunt the damage that follows). What we believe now

Ischemic preconditioning (IPC) — a blood-pressure cuff inflated to occlude blood flow for a few minutes, in cycles, before exercise — shows a real but marginal benefit for next-day muscle soreness, and a more heterogeneous benefit for blood-vessel function, on a very small evidence base. Across 13 studies (263 participants, 243 male), local IPC (cuff on the limb being trained) reduced delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) at 24h and 48h after high-intensity eccentric exercise — but the pooled effect barely cleared significance (p=0.049, 95% CI -1.83 to -0.01), and remote IPC (cuff on a different limb) showed no such effect. A separate, more heterogeneous pool of protocols (acute remote, local-vs-remote comparisons, repeated local) found IPC improved flow-mediated dilation — a marker of how well a blood vessel widens in response to increased blood flow, and an early signal of vascular/endothelial health (ischemic-preconditioning-reduces-doms-and-improves-flow-mediated-dilation). IPC showed no measurable effect on blood biomarkers of muscle or cardiac injury (creatine kinase, troponin) at any timepoint.

Why emerging, not higher. This is the first systematic review ever conducted on this specific question — there is no independent replication to corroborate it. The primary soreness finding is borderline (a confidence interval that nearly touches zero), the participant pool is small and severely sex-skewed (243 male vs. 20 female), and no study registration is reported. The review's own authors call for "larger, higher-quality studies" before any protocol recommendation — this map exists to track the question honestly from here, not to have already answered it.

Not yet actionable for David. No specific IPC protocol (pressure, duration, cycle count, timing relative to the pickleball return-to-play goal) is established well enough to recommend; nothing here changes his current recovery routine.
