Potassium citrate (alkaline salt, dietary-acid buffering) — supplements

Status: emerging (2026-07-12) — auto-coined this run, flagged for human review, and it is a CLOSE CALL. The alternative (flag as a candidate intervention, coin nothing — the treatment creatine-monohydrate gave beta-alanine last run) was seriously weighed. The map was coined anyway for one reason: the vault's bone thread is a live, losing thread, and this is its third data point. The map's own headline is that the case for potassium citrate is unmade.

What we believe now

Nothing here supports taking potassium citrate for bone, and the paper that would have sold it doesn't try to. A GRADE-assessed meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis (5 RCTs, n=401 postmenopausal women) found:

  • One significant result: urinary NTX (a resorption marker) at 1 month, MD −4.05 (−8.05 to −0.05), p = 0.0474 — one hit among ~12 uncorrected marker × timepoint comparisons, with an interval whose upper bound is a rounding error from zero (potassium-citrate-marginal-single-marker-bone-signal).
  • Everything else null: CTX (3, 6 mo), P1NP (3, 6, 12 mo), urinary/serum calcium, PTH, urinary pH.
  • A trial sequential analysis concluding the evidence base is below the required information size — i.e. inconclusive, not negative (potassium-citrate-bone-nulls-honestly-underpowered).
  • The authors' own verdict: "The clinical relevance of this finding remains uncertain."

Two things make this map worth keeping, and neither is the supplement.

(1) The bone thread now reads 0-for-3. creatine-monohydrate adds no BMD beyond training (creatine-bone-bmd-null). resistance-training shows no BMD difference between heavy and moderate loading — and that one was an underpowered miss, not equivalence (rt-intensity-does-not-change-bmd). Now an alkaline salt, working through an entirely different mechanism (buffering dietary acid load rather than loading the skeleton), produces one borderline marker and a formal declaration that the evidence is insufficient. Three mechanisms, three failures to demonstrate anything a person could feel. The vault does not conclude that bone is unmovable — resistance training and adequate protein remain what a person actually does for their skeleton, and neither claim rests on these papers. It concludes something narrower and more useful: nothing in this vault has earned a purchase for bone health.

(2) It is the month's cleanest example of an honest null. Shen 2026 called falls RR 2.68 (CI to 11.11) a "similar safety profile." Wu 2026 called four underpowered nulls "as effective." Jiao 2026 ranked exercise modalities it had just declared statistically indistinguishable. Every one of those papers had more participants than this one, and every one was less trustworthy with them. This paper measured its own information size, found it wanting, and said so. shared/study-appraisal.md holds that reliability is what a study earns — and this is what earning it looks like. Recorded deliberately, as a reference case.

Why emerging and not fading: fading means superseded, debunked, or gone quiet. This is none of those — it is untested at adequate scale, by the authors' own trial sequential analysis. An honest "nobody knows yet" is not a debunk, and the vault will not manufacture one out of an underpowered null. That is the same error it has now caught three times in other people's papers.

For David: nothing. Population is postmenopausal women; outcome is a turnover marker (not BMD, not fracture); the effect is a single borderline p-value at a single timepoint. It does not enter the Protocol, is not ranked, and no dose is transcribed (shared/safety.md). It belongs on the Considered-and-not-doing shelf.

⚑ NEW 2026-09-15 — a different outcome domain arrives, favorable, and irrelevant to David; and the map's standing safety gap gets its first source. A narrative review (Talyshinskii 2026) reframes two 1990s RCTs in recurrent calcium stone formers via number-needed-to-treat: NNT ≈2 over 3 years to prevent one stone recurrence (potassium-citrate-stone-prevention-nnt-is-large-but-rests-on-two-old-small-trials). This does not change the bone verdict above — different outcome, different population — but it corrects a possible misreading: this compound is not uniformly unpromising, it has simply not earned a purchase for bone. The same source names hyperkalemia as the principal serious risk, concentrated in reduced renal function, RAAS-blocker or potassium-sparing-diuretic use, and advanced age (potassium-citrate-hyperkalemia-risk-factors-named) — the first sourced answer to this map's standing global-ripple gap ("no source in this vault characterizes" this risk). safety_flags: [interaction] now set on the map. Verdict for David is unchanged: nothing. Population mismatch (not a stone former) and the bone question this map exists to answer remains a non-purchase.
