Exercise for blood pressure — which modality, and how much (exercise)

Status: consolidating (2026-08-21) — ⚑ UPGRADED FROM emerging TONIGHT, two days after the map was coined, and a human should check the call. The map opened on one network meta-analysis (30 randomised trials, 2,137 adults with diagnosed hypertension, CINeMA certainty on all 90 comparisons). Tonight a second, entirely independent network arrived — 159 randomised trials, 10,821 adults aged 45+, six databases, PROSPERO-registered, built by different authors on a different population with a different node taxonomy — and replicated the core answer. shared/evidence-model.md defines consolidating as ≥2 independent groups, high-tier evidence, a real effect, replicated, with no live rebuttal, and all five clauses are now satisfied for the direction-and-magnitude claim.

⚠ The upgrade covers the claim that exercise lowers blood pressure by a clinically meaningful amount, and NOTHING about which modality is best. What we believe now

Exercise lowers blood pressure by a clinically meaningful amount, in people who have hypertension and in people who do not — and doing aerobic and resistance work together is the best-evidenced way to do it. Which precise variant wins is genuinely unsettled.

Two independent network meta-analyses, three days apart, now anchor this map. Lv 2026: 30 randomised trials, 2,137 adults with diagnosed hypertension, ten modality nodes, CINeMA certainty on all 90 comparisons. Hu 2026: 159 randomised trials, 10,821 adults aged 45 and over, seven nodes, six databases, PROSPERO-registered, with a model-based dose-response analysis attached. Different authors, different databases, different populations, different taxonomies — and the same headline answer.

⚑ The two estimates for combined training reconcile through a moderator that one of them measured. Hu found baseline blood pressure to be the only effect modifier of five tested: every 10 mmHg higher a group starts, exercise lowers it 2.49 mmHg more (−3.73 to −1.24); groups starting at or above 140 mmHg gained 3.86 mmHg more than groups below it (exercise-blood-pressure-benefit-shrinks-with-baseline-pressure-but-does-not-vanish). That is almost exactly the gap between −9.52 in hypertensive adults and −7.72 in a mostly-normotensive network. Age, intervention duration and measurement posture all did nothing.

⚠ THIS MAP HAS CORRECTED ITSELF ON ITS MOST IMPORTANT CAVEAT, AND THE CORRECTION IS THE HEADLINE. On 2026-08-19 this section read: "None of these millimetre figures transfer to someone with normal blood pressure." That was the right caution on a hypertensive-only network and it is now measurably too strong. Only 82 of Hu's 379 arms had a mean baseline at hypertension level — so his pooled estimates are predominantly estimates in people whose blood pressure was already normal, and every modality still lowered it. The honest form of the caveat is a discount of roughly 4 mmHg, not an exclusion.

⚑ How much: the dose-response, which neither the vault nor Lv could ask. Systolic pressure falls further as weekly dose rises, to a predicted maximum of −6.39 mmHg (−8.72 to −4.06) at 1,200 METs-minutes/week — the upper edge of the WHO physical-activity recommendation, 600–1,200 — then flattens (exercise-blood-pressure-dose-response-peaks-at-1200-met-minutes-then-rebounds). Minimum doses for a significant effect differ tenfold by modality: circuit training 61 METs-min/week, resistance 110, intervals and combined training 130, moderate continuous 210. ⚠ The apparent rebound past 1,200 sits in the sparse tail of the network, the authors do not defend it as biology, and this map declines it.

⚑ The convergence is now five literatures deep. Combined aerobic-plus-resistance tops blood pressure in both networks, and already topped endothelial function (combined-aerobic-resistance-tops-endothelial-dose-response), glycaemic control (combined-aerobic-resistance-tops-glycemic-control-in-older-adults) and all-cause mortality (aerobic-plus-resistance-lowest-mortality). Five evidence bases, four outcomes, one answer: doing both beats doing either.

⚠ What is NOT consolidating, and it is the half that gets quoted: the ranking. Lv's winner was combined training at high certainty; Hu's winner was circuit training at −13.52 mmHg — and it collapses under Hu's own sensitivity analyses, losing its rank whenever high-risk-of-bias or small trials are excluded, carrying the network's only significant local inconsistency, and resting on 10 arms and 267 people (integrated-aerobic-resistance-training-survives-the-quality-filter-that-sinks-circuit-training). ⚑ Once that filter runs, Hu's answer becomes Lv's answer — which is the strongest single fact on this map. Not one of Hu's 21 comparisons reached high confidence under CINeMA; 49 of Lv's 90 were very low. Rankings here are not evidence.

⚠ Isometric handgrip has moved in the vault's estimation, in both directions. Lv put it 3rd of ten and this map recorded it admiringly: the best benefit-per-burden ratio on the list, and the only lever that puts no load through a knee. Hu puts it last of six, at −4.18 (−8.31 to −0.06) — an interval missing zero by six hundredths — with the highest dropout of any modality, 21.70% (isometric-exercise-ranks-last-in-a-midlife-and-older-network). The magnitudes agree; the rank and the adherence argument do not. Hu's 11 isometric trials were all handgrip, with no wall squats, and wall squats are what the isometric guideline case was built on — so this bounds the claim rather than retracting it.

Why the base is still weak despite the upgrade. Hu: 73 of 152 contributing trials (48%) at high risk of bias, zero high-confidence comparisons, English-only, imputed change-score variances. Lv: 4 of 30 trials at low risk of bias, three databases, 49 of 90 comparisons very-low certainty. Both: an unblindable, measurement-sensitive outcome, median interventions of 12 weeks, and nothing anywhere measuring persistence.

⚠ Population and purpose. This map recommends nothing to anyone and sets no dose. Every number above describes what trials did.
