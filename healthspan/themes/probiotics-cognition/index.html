Probiotics/synbiotics for cognitive dysfunction — supplements

Status: emerging (2026-11-13) — ⚑ auto-coined this run, flagged for human review. Coined from a single meta-analysis (Zhang 2026, Ageing Research Reviews, 10 RCTs, n=739) finding probiotic/synbiotic supplementation moderately improves cognitive test performance in people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or Alzheimer's disease — but at very-low GRADE certainty, with substantial heterogeneity and a prediction interval wide enough to include no effect. This is a fourth, distinct outcome claim for probiotics in this vault, different from probiotics-gut-motility (age-related constipation), probiotics-metabolic (metabolic-syndrome markers), and probiotics-sleep (sleep-quality indices) — a different population (clinically diagnosed cognitive impairment, not healthy adults) and a different mechanism being proposed (gut-brain axis and neuroinflammation, not gut transit, lipids, or circadian signaling).

Sub-threads: none — one map, one claim: does probiotic/synbiotic supplementation improve cognition in people with diagnosed cognitive impairment. [reviewed 2026-11-13 @ 1 bullets]

What we believe now

Probiotics/synbiotics may modestly improve cognitive test scores in people with MCI or Alzheimer's disease — a real pooled signal, but one the evidence itself says is not yet reliable. Pooling 10 randomized trials (739 participants), cognitive test performance improved by SMD 0.50 (95% CI 0.19–0.81) versus placebo/usual care — moderate in magnitude, but with substantial heterogeneity (I²=76%) and GRADE certainty rated very low by the review's own authors. The 95% prediction interval (−0.57 to 1.57) is wide enough that a single new trial could find nothing, or even a reversal. No subgroup — disease stage, strain, dose, or duration — explained which formulation, if any, actually works.

Why emerging, not higher. A single meta-analysis, however methodologically transparent (RoB 2, Egger's, GRADE all genuinely applied), pooling a small total sample (739) across heterogeneous trials in a clinically-impaired population. The map needs an independent replication, and ideally a trial that isolates which strain or dose drives the effect, before it can move.

Not actionable for David (GOAL.md). This evidence is in people with diagnosed MCI or Alzheimer's — not healthy-adult cognitive enhancement. Nothing here changes David's regimen.
