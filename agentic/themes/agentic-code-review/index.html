  • Teams under AI-assisted throughput did not add review capacity — they moved supervision off the gate into three layers that fail differently, and the pressure is only half volume. The other half is false correctness: output that is syntactically correct and locally coherent while nobody can reconstruct why it is shaped that way, which moves validation off behaviour and onto exactly what tests cannot check. (Added 2026-08-28, T1 — suggestive.) The distinction worth stealing is drawn by mechanism rather than sequence: a preventive guardrail (specification, steering file, architectural plan) shapes generation and only works if the generation process consults it; an executable guardrail (lint, test, CI check) runs against output regardless of how it was produced. They run concurrently on purpose, so the executable check still fires when the steering artifact is stale, ignored or absent from that trajectory — which is the practical answer to a-stale-convention-file-costs-more-than-no-file: you do not fix a stale convention file by trusting it harder (supervision-moved-off-the-review-gate-into-three-layers). Human oversight changed shape twice: in altitude, toward operational explainability — generated systems must stay diagnosable and reconstructable on demand rather than fully comprehended, at a threshold calibrated to criticality — and in time, from post-hoc inspection to "observe step-by-step … interrupt early", which relocates the human from a gate to a monitor.   • The review gate on agent-written code is now routinely closed by another agent, in about seventy seconds, and what the reviewer says depends on which agent wrote the code. (Added 2026-08-25, T1 — strong on the counts, and every interpretation is observational.) Of 2,830,284 agent-authored pull requests identified on public GitHub, 248,641 (8.8%) received at least one AI-attributed review across 10,345 repositories; 45,269 (1.6%) were reviewed by a different product, and both groups grew by more than two orders of magnitude from 2025-Q1 to Q3. Median latency from PR open to first AI review is 1.2 minutes cross-product and 4.7 same-product — and the source refuses the obvious reading of its own direction, showing the gap is compositional (fast reviewers are over-represented cross-product; the slowest, at a 17.7-minute median, dominates same-product) and that timestamp completeness differs sharply between the groups (79.2% vs 31.9%). Whether your generated code is checked by the same vendor's model is not usually a decision: Copilot-authored PRs are reviewed by Copilot 95.7% of the time, while Cursor-, Google Jules- and Claude Code-authored PRs are reviewed cross-product essentially 100% of the time (review-capacity-is-what-a-loop-actually-spends). The finding with the longest reach is the reviewer-side variation: hold one reviewer fixed and vary the authoring agent, and its refactor-comment share moves from 10.5% on one agent's PRs to 35.0% on another's. That may be the PRs rather than the reviewer, and the categories are self-declared rather than validated defects — but it means any dashboard aggregating AI review output is partly measuring who wrote the code. Read beside faster-ai-review-is-not-better-review, this is that result's end state: the loop now closes faster than a human could open the diff, and nothing in the data says a defect is more likely to be caught. Attribution is signature-based and asymmetric (far more reliable on the reviewer side), so all absolute counts are lower bounds; "closed-loop" does not exclude a human also having reviewed, which this design cannot see.
  • The two-agent review setup that looks most like independent validation is the one that manufactures agreement, and the fix is a channel change rather than a model change. (Added 2026-08-20, T1 — strong.) Six methods built one design choice at a time on one benchmark with one model give this map a controlled ablation of the entire "add a checker" family, and four of them are indistinguishable: zero-shot 77%, self-critique 77%, one external reviewer 77%, two independent reviewers 75% — the last of them worse than doing nothing. Interaction is what moves it, not headcount: a three-agent loop where a critic audits the reviewer over a frozen artifact reaches 87%, above a five-agent parallel-review system at 82% (interaction-shape-beats-agent-count, primary in loop-engineering). Point that same loop at review quality and it comes last of four, F1 0.457 against 0.495–0.503. The traces name two shapes of one failure. Over-decomposition: the reviewer hedges, the critic confirms every hedge and adds a speculative bug, and the judge marks 3 of 5 comments fabricated — this map's known volume-not-blindness failure (a-review-agents-default-failure-is-volume-not-blindness) produced by a second agent rather than the first. Yielding: the critic raises a manually verified real bug, the reviewer rebuts with a file-level argument citing no code, the critic gives in, and the bug is dropped from the final review — on a task where the non-interacting baseline catches it at more than double the F1. Both are the same structure: the agents reach agreement and the agreement is not supported by evidence (two-agents-told-to-agree-will-agree). The operational rule: two agents agreeing is not two pieces of evidence unless the protocol made disagreement cheap and legible. And the repair is the strongest cost-effectiveness result this map has on verifier design — typing the critic's verdict into agree / evidence-backed-disagreement / unevidenced-concern, and forbidding an uncited rebuttal from settling anything, takes the same three agents, same model, same graph from worst to best, 0.457 → 0.533 (type-the-disagreement-or-it-collapses-into-agreement, primary in verifier-independence). This confirms verifier-diversity-is-not-verifier-independence from a new direction: there the second opinion failed because the checkers shared errors; here it fails because the protocol rewarded convergence.   • Every precision-style number a review pipeline reports is computed over the items the reviewer flagged, so a reviewer that simply flags less is indistinguishable from one that got better — and an undeclared pipeline arrangement moves the flag rate by up to 11.5 points. (Added 2026-08-19, T1, suggestive.) This map has spent a great deal of evidence on what a reviewer's precision is (the-checker-should-see-less-than-the-generator at 25–38% against a general agent's 7–16%, resolution-rate-is-not-precision on what the number counts, review-agent-precision-is-a-tunable-not-a-given as the framing). Here is the failure mode underneath all of them, demonstrated with both arms run. Putting a completed audit-then-repair episode about a different item in the checker's context lowers its false-alarm rate in 15 of 15 model × wording combinations with no detectable gain in discrimination — the criterion moves, sensitivity does not (a-criterion-shift-is-not-a-discrimination-gain) — and a dashboard reading only the flagged set records that as an improvement (a-falling-false-alarm-rate-reads-as-a-better-reviewer). Whether the leniency helps is an empirical question about the flags being removed, and this is one of very few studies to go and look. A hand audit of 50 baseline false alarms: 41 (82%) simply wrong, 8 (16%) defensible-but-stricter [Wilson 8.3–28.5%], 1 a suspected gold-label error. And the removal is not uniform — defensible flags concentrate in the logical error type (7 of 20) with none found in 21 arithmetic or algebraic cases (pooled Clopper–Pearson [0%, 16.1%]), while arithmetic and algebraic flags account for 97% of the reduction. So at this operating point leniency eats the fabrications and leaves the arguable calls — by luck of where the two distributions sit, and the authors say directly that this is the clause a practitioner should not rely on. Two cheap practices follow. Never report a reviewer's precision without the recall arm — the minimum honest pair is a flag rate on known-good work and a detection rate on known-bad work from the same conditions, which is price-the-checkers-abstention-as-lost-coverage's demand generalized. And hand-audit a sample of your reviewer's output by error class: it took one person and fifty cases, it is the measurement this map's capacity results (comprehension-debt-outpaces-review-capacity) have always been missing, and classifying by class is what turns a rate into a filtering decision. [T1 — three open-weight models on stepwise mathematical verification rather than diffs, so the 82% is a worked example of the measurement and not a rate to assume for a code reviewer; the audit is one model at one wording by one author with no inter-annotator agreement; only the pooled type comparison survives correction]
  • The shipped review agents lose to a constrained pipeline on every model tested, and they lose on precision: thirty comments per pull request to surface one useful finding in thirteen. Non-determinism in review is a design choice, not a property of LLM agents. (Added 2026-08-12, T1, strong.) The first head-to-head against the tools people actually run — Claude Code's /code-review and Codex's /review — on 200 real pull requests, 10 languages and 1,505 expert-verified comments validated by 80+ senior engineers, across six backends from four providers. Every backend does better inside a pipeline that takes freedom away at three points: rule-driven dispatch (which files, which checklist, resolved by a four-tier glob chain rather than by agent triage), bounded tools (six review-specific tools with capped output instead of a shell), and anchoring (a three-stage line resolver rather than the agent's claimed line number) — best cell 25.10% SEM-F1 against the same model's 11.57%, at 385K tokens vs 5,664K and 1m23s vs 13m06s (inject-determinism-at-the-dispatch-not-the-agent). The number to plan around is the volume: 5,980 comments across 200 PRs to match 435 ground-truth items, 7.23% precision — and the regime belongs to the system, since the same models sit at 25–38% precision inside the constrained pipeline and 7–16% inside the general-purpose agent (a-review-agents-default-failure-is-volume-not-blindness). This is reviewer-agent-only-review-halves-the-merge-rate measured at the comment level with an expert denominator, and it makes resolution-rate-is-not-precision an operational rather than a semantic point. Codex marks the opposite failure and it is not the safe one — 4.92% recall at 266 comments, a loop that stops too early. Two things follow for practice. Instrument comments-per-PR: it is free, it moves before quality does, and it separates a reviewer that is working from one that is padding. And the filter that produces the precision is a boundary, not a model — a reflector on the same backbone, shown only the diff, told to falsify rather than verify, allowed to delete but never generate (the-checker-should-see-less-than-the-generator, now the anchor of verifier-independence). Carry the caveat with the numbers: SEM-F1 is LLM-judged against a fixed expert set, so a good comment matching nothing scores as a false positive and every absolute level is understated; and the benchmark shares authors with the winning system, the arrangement an-evaluator-sharing-its-reference-solver-cannot-be-self-checked warns about, unaddressed here.
  • The reviewer agent's dark side has its first direct measurement, and the variable that opens it is a harness setting rather than a model property: what the auditor misses is not what it cannot reach but what is co-resident in its window. (Added 2026-08-07, T1, strong.) This map has said since 2026-07-14 that automated review overtook human review (19% → 84%) and that its false-negative rate is unmeasured everywhere. Here it is measured, adversarially: 208 execution-validated attacks over ten real repositories, each rendered four ways under a build-valid leave-one-out necessity test (832 renderings), against three deployed PR-auditing agents in six auditor–model configurations, with detection requiring a finding that names the planted capability and attributes it to an attack-bearing PR. The floor is the first number to absorb: with the entire verified attack in a single PR, detection runs 42–79%. Then the mechanism. Splitting the attack across commits — the intuitive concealment story — moves detection by at most five points. Hiding the attack's predecessors from repository history costs only 4–11. But interleaving genuine benign PRs and reviewing the window as a whole costs 39–45 points, bottoming out at 16–22% against 50–60% under per-PR review on identical content; fusing each component into a genuine upstream diff under a description that plausibly accounts for both costs a further 10–18 (concealment-is-context-occupancy-not-commit-boundaries). So the auditor was never failing for want of access to the past — it fails when the past is in the room, which makes the review unit a security parameter and the narrower setting the safer one. That cuts against the obvious remedy for reviewing-one-pr-at-a-time-cannot-see-what-two-do-together: widening the review unit helps an agent recover relations it is told to look for and hurts one noticing an attack it is not, so "give the reviewer more context" is a trade whose sign depends on whether the reviewer has been briefed. The failure mode inside the misses is this map's own 2026-07-24 and 2026-07-28 findings arriving adversarially: in the representative case the auditor described both halves of a two-part security relaxation correctly and filed them as a deliberate usability tradeoff, issuing no finding (an-auditor-can-read-the-change-and-decline-to-flag-it) — localization succeeded and judgment failed, exactly as repository-grounding-buys-localization-not-judgment and a-verifier-sharing-the-interpretation-shares-the-error predict. Which also means a reviewer benchmark scoring comment placement would count this as a hit. And the workflow around the model is a term of the same order as the model: with Claude Haiku 4.5 fixed as the backbone, the three frameworks span 55/65/56% (the-review-framework-is-a-term-in-the-detection-rate). [T1, strong — but one independent run per attack × rendering × system cell with no intervals; ten repositories, almost all Python web frameworks; attacks are agent-synthesized against agent-ranked risks under execution-based admission and two-reviewer plausibility annotation (κ = 0.87), so realistic by construction rather than by provenance; the detection judge is itself an LLM, validated against a stratified author-labelled sample]
  • The "never accept an agent's done" rule has an unguarded instance inside every pipeline: one agent's claim becomes another agent's premise at machine speed, and seven in ten of those claims did not hold. (Added 2026-08-06, T1, strong.) This map has held since 2026-07-10 that an author's self-assessment is not the completion criterion (the-agents-final-report-is-its-least-reliable-artifact, users-correct-agents-in-nine-of-ten-resolutions at 91.49%). Those measure a report a human reads at the end. The same failure inside a multi-stage pipeline has no reader at all. Instrumented on a system where a trained searcher hands structured findings to a frontier fixer: of 249 handoffs claiming a verified bug reproduction, replaying the claimed command in the task sandbox against the unpatched repository found 50 genuine (20%) and 174 demonstrably false (70%). And the overclaiming is correlated with difficulty — handoffs produced after the searcher exhausted its turn budget were genuine 9% of the time against 22% for spontaneous ones — so the misinformation is densest on the tasks where the receiving model most needs help (verify-a-handoffs-claims-before-the-next-agent-reads-them). The remedy is not a reviewer and involves no model: a sandbox replays the claim, a claim that does not genuinely fail is stripped along with its test file, and a claim that does reproduce is materialized for the fixer to use directly. (Added 2026-08-03, T1, strong.) A within-diff ranking is a different and easier prediction, and a taxonomy-guided two-stage reviewer that surfaces only the top regions beats a deployed incumbent by 2.4× on quality estimation (0.582 vs 0.239) and 3.3× on top-5 defect localization (0.098 vs 0.030) at 5× fewer tokens and 6× lower latency — 55.4 seconds to first review against 335.9, which is the difference between a signal inside the workflow and one outside it (rank-the-diff-instead-of-commenting-everywhere).   • Both routes to triage are now closed: you cannot rank a review queue by what the change looks like, and you cannot rank it by what the agent says about the change. (Added 2026-08-02, T1.) The capacity sub-thread has spent a month establishing that the human gate is narrowing and asking what relieves it.   • Buying a second model family does not buy a second opinion — fleets fail together because the work is hard, not because they share a base model.   • The largest act of human repair on AI-generated code is deletion, it happens in the editor within the hour, and it leaves no artifact — so the maintenance bill is undercounted below the commit as well as above it. (Added 2026-08-02, T1; inline completions rather than agent patches — the layer is the contribution.) This map established that review-based metrics undercount by construction: comments on 2.18% of agentic PRs while humans backfill 72.5% of the gap in silent follow-up commits (humans-silently-repair-agent-code-outside-review), and 49% more corrective maintenance post-merge (agentic-code-carries-higher-corrective-maintenance). Both measure at commit granularity or later. Measured below it — 53,614 before-after pairs from 5,831 in-IDE trajectories by 1,141 opted-in developers — 31% of accepted completions are removed rather than repaired, with the modal removal at 23.6 minutes and half of all editing inside 50 minutes. None of that reaches a diff, a PR, a review comment or a revert. Two shapes matter more than the rate. Survival is bimodal (median 63% of the completion remains, but the mass sits at kept-nearly-whole or discarded-nearly-whole, with developers adding only 20% of final code) — adaptation is not the common outcome, so the useful quality question is "is this right," not "how much fixing." And the path into abandonment inverts the intuition: removal is likelier after a first attempt to customize (23%) than after fixing quality (14%) or changing functionality (12%), so the costly completion is the one close enough to invite editing and subtly enough misaligned to defeat it (a-third-of-ai-completions-are-abandoned-not-repaired) — the human-side twin of a-weak-reviewer-rewrites-where-a-strong-one-edits. [T1, strong for the distributions and the benchmark; opt-in single-extension population, accepted completions only, LLM-judge edit labels, heuristic trajectory reconstruction that kept the cleanly reconstructable 1K developers of 4K]
  • The external anchor is necessary and not sufficient: a verifier that re-derives from the same source under the same interpretation inherits the error, so independence of narration is not independence of interpretation. (Added 2026-07-28, T1; completes the 2026-07-27 reconciliation below.) The verifier here is built the way this vault would build one — narration-blind (its context holds only the verbatim task instruction and machine access, never the agent's message history, plan or rationale), state-grounded (it reads the real persisted artifact), anti-capture (it must independently locate the deliverable the task names; evidence found only in a side file the agent created is rejected), and bounded at three correction rounds. Of the 79 non-perfect tasks in its run, 76 reached the gate: it correctly rejected 8 and wrongly passed 68 — an error rate of ~90% — while being nearly perfect in the other direction (8 of 9 rejections correct, 28 of 29 successes correctly passed) (a-verifier-sharing-the-interpretation-shares-the-error). The authors are explicit that this is not the bias the field guards against: the gate is structurally independent of the agent's account, so it is a common-mode failure — shared interpretation of the source. It adjudicates structure (missing, unsaved, wrong path, wrong format) reliably and cannot adjudicate value, because arriving at the right value requires the same reasoning from the same inputs; only 14 of the 68 misses were catchable structurally, and the run's own audit names agent reasoning as the dominant failure class (38 of 79). This is the third arrival of one mechanism in five days and should now govern every independence claim on this map: a test and a fix emitted in one trajectory fail together at 1.87× the independence rate because both come from one reading of the bug (cogenerated-tests-and-fixes-fail-together); the most independent reviewer condition was the only significantly harmful one, reconciled as "independence wins where there is an external anchor to re-derive from" (reviewer-value-tracks-capability-headroom); and here the anchor is present — the real file, the real store, the real cell — and the gate misses anyway. What follows is a split, not a discouragement. A cheap generic narration-blind gate is worth shipping for what it actually sells: 88.9% precision, one prompt-level check with zero per-task engineering, 21.4% of the retries it triggers converting to success, and 3.8 binary points lost when it is removed. For value correctness the check must differ in how it reads the source — a differently-derived expectation, an oracle built from something the agent never saw (fail-to-pass-does-not-constrain-the-fix is the same move in test generation), or a hard-coded per-task verifier, which wins on exactly this axis and loses all generality. [T1, strong for the mechanism; computer-use desktop tasks rather than code review, 108 tasks and 9 rejections, vendor research on its own harness]
  • Independence is not the active ingredient — headroom is. A reviewer without the capability to add something the writer did not already check is net negative. (Added 2026-07-27, T1; refines the 2026-07-25 entry below, and the map stays consolidating because it sharpens which outside verifier to use rather than questioning that one is needed.) The first grid to vary independence and capability together: two models, writer × reviewer fully crossed, 116 LiveCodeBench tasks, static review with no execution. The most independent condition available — the weaker model reviewing the stronger one's draft — is the only significantly harmful cell (−8.6 pp, p_BH = .0456, 3 fixes against 13 regressions). The least independent condition on the weaker writer — that model reviewing its own draft — is significantly positive (+12.9 pp, p_BH = .0222, 21 fixes / 6 regressions). Best cell is the stronger model reviewing the weaker one's draft (+18.1 pp, p_BH = .0010, 26 fixes / 5 regressions); the strong model reviewing itself is at the noise floor (±0, 3 fixes / 3 regressions). The reconciliation with an-independent-checker-beats-the-same-model-checking-itself (79% vs 71%, a smaller model auditing a frontier one) is the useful part: that check had an external anchor — re-derive the target from the original request — so the auditor only needed to be uncorrelated. A code review by inspection has no anchor, so the reviewer needs capability. When the check has a ground truth, buy independence; when it is a judgment call, you are buying capability, and a cheap independent reviewer is worse than none (reviewer-value-tracks-capability-headroom). The cheap deployable test nobody runs: before shipping a reviewer, run it on drafts you know already pass and count the regressions. A gate that rewrites 13 correct solutions per 106 to catch 3 would show a healthy resolution rate, because every rewrite it lands is a resolution. Candidate leading indicator, uncounted in the study: the harmful reviewer rewrites where the helpful one edits (a-weak-reviewer-rewrites-where-a-strong-one-edits). [T1, suggestive — competitive-programming tasks, n = 116, one run per condition, two models, and the authors state the design cannot separate review direction from the baseline capability gap]
  • Resolution rate measures usefulness, not correctness — and the plurality of what it counts as failure is the maintainer knowing something the agent does not. (Added 2026-07-27, T1 — first field measurement of the criterion this map adopted on 2026-07-11.) Across 54,713 comments from three production reviewers on 341 Python repositories, resolution runs Copilot 72.9% / Cursor 67.2% / Codex 54.8% — the first cross-vendor reviewer comparison on a common population. Opening the denominator changes what the number means: card-sorting 470 unresolved discussions, the leading reason a comment is not acted on is Intentional Design Decision (112) — context-specific implementation, expected behaviour, team preference, 72% raised by core developers — while Incorrect Suggestion is 67 (63 false positives, 4 hallucinations). So a reviewer can raise its resolution rate purely by acquiring project context, with no improvement in defect detection (resolution-rate-is-not-precision). A plain measurement bug rides along: 114 of the 470 sampled discussions are comments the developer acted on and never marked resolved, so anyone computing resolution from GitHub's isResolved flag undercounts adoption. And the predictor model is a rebuke to how reviewer agents are being tuned: the strongest driver of resolution is an inline code suggestion (OR 1.62; 75.5% vs 64.6%), length works against adoption (OR 0.93), the mere presence of an explanation is negative (OR 0.593) while specific types are positive (rule 1.14, benefit 1.09, example 1.08), and relevance, clarity and conciseness are all significant at negligible effect size — writing quality does not distinguish useful comments from ignored ones (an-inline-suggestion-is-the-strongest-resolution-lever). The uncomfortable corollary: optimizing an agent against this metric optimizes for frictionless application, and nothing in the signal separates a correct one-liner from a plausible one. [T1, strong for the spread and the predictor model; suggestive for the taxonomy — Python/GitHub/OSS only, LLM-assigned labels at κ = 0.74, 83% of the corpus from one vendor, and the taxonomy rests on the 6.7% of unresolved comments that drew a reply]
  • Reviewer diversity is implementable, and one org is shipping it as a control rather than reporting it as a correlate. (Added 2026-07-25, T2 practice.) The 2026-07-16 finding was observational — agent-involved reviews carry higher smell prevalence (78–94% vs 69–76% across 1.02M PRs), attributed to reviewer monoculture, the same AI reviewer on every PR (faster-ai-review-is-not-better-review). An org where ~80% of merged code is agent-authored now describes the opposite design as deliberate: multiple narrowly-scoped review agents in separate context windows, explicitly rejecting a single mega-agent so they do not share blindspots, with SAST alongside and retrieval over past incidents. Diversity is engineered on two axes at once — scope and context boundary — and the same org reaches it independently in its migration practice, where adversarial reviewers hold separate contexts and a disagreement escalates to a third agent (fix-the-loop-not-the-artifact). Three results now converge on independence of the observer as the active ingredient: the monoculture study, the 6 → 2 collapse in rescues when observe/compare returns to the generating model (structure-outweighs-the-verification-step), and a cross-model checker at 79% against self-checking's 71% on all eight backends (an-independent-checker-beats-the-same-model-checking-itself). It also supplies the map's missing false-negative practice: red-team the reviewer before it gates anything. What it does not do is relieve the capacity ceiling — three reviewers where you ran one buys reviewer supply and diversity, two of the four levers, at proportional and unpriced cost (run-several-scoped-reviewers-not-one-mega-agent). [T2, suggestive — no A/B against a single reviewer, no ensemble false-negative rate]
  • "Review this carefully" is a generic self-check, and a named checklist is not. (Added 2026-07-25, T3, single-study — measured on authoring agents.) Holding everything fixed but the completion instruction, a detailed checklist naming all 24 obligations passes 10/10 where a similarly-sized generic "mechanically validate every explicit constraint" passes 5/10 (Fisher p = 0.0325; the prompts differ by 649 characters). The mechanism generalizes past the study: a self-generated validator is generated from the same degraded representation that produced the artifact, so it inherits the omission — four of the five generic failures omit exactly the field generation omitted. Composed with reviewers-underplan-and-overvalidate (heavy validation effort marks the reviews that fail), the sharper claim is that validation effort without an external requirement source re-derives the same blind spot. Cheap to test on a reviewer agent and nobody has (restate-the-checks-externally-at-completion). [T3, single-study — authoring not reviewing, one task, one model, n = 10 per arm]
  • Repository grounding buys the reviewer where, not what — so the binding constraint on reviewer quality is navigation, not model judgment. (Added 2026-07-24.) This map has tuned reviewer quality as one dial. The first trajectory-level study of terminal reviewers splits it. On localization (comment within ±5 lines of a human comment) the repo-exploring agents win roughly threefold — Claude Code 22.39%, Rovo Dev 20.93% against 7.22–9.15% for every diff-only reviewer. On whether the comment identifies a relevant issue they lose: GPT-5.2 21.44% against the best agent's 15.10%. Nothing here says grounding improves judgment; it says it improves aim — and the agents get there on three to ten times fewer comments (392–947 vs 2,305–9,060), which matters because volume is itself a maintenance cost this map already prices. The actionable consequence is a reallocation: improving repository navigation likely beats scaling model capability, which puts this claim in direct contact with structural-index-beats-grep-at-lower-cost (+39.6 pp of localization inside a coding agent, at lower cost per solve). Nobody has yet put a structural index behind a reviewer agent and re-measured the localization column — a cheap, obvious, unrun experiment. Sobering absolute: requiring both criteria, the best production reviewer scores 5.81% against real human review comments, and the paper's own caveat (agents surface valid issues the benchmark's humans never wrote) biases in the same optimistic direction as every other story about reviewer agents (repository-grounding-buys-localization-not-judgment). [T1, suggestive — LLM-as-judge on the alignment column, 362 tasks, one harness]
  • Reviewer agents allocate effort inversely to its value: planning is under a tenth of the budget and the only phase that predicts success, while heavy validation is a signature of failure. (Added 2026-07-24.) Across three trajectory-exposing agents, planning takes <10% of steps and cost in every one, yet accomplished trajectories allocate significantly more to it (p = 0.012 / 0.038, two of three); unaccomplished trajectories carry heavier validation effort — re-opening diffs and re-reading files without recovering from weak initial grounding. The proposed mechanism is that excessive validation signals poor grounding rather than repairing it, which is consistent with localization being the binding constraint. Note the limit honestly: this is an association across trajectories, not a manipulation — nobody has forced a reviewer to plan more and measured the result, and the causation could run the other way. It is also the third instance in eight days of post-hoc effort being weak or negative (repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum, recovery-actions-are-not-a-capability-ladder), which the vault carries as a watched pattern rather than an asserted one. Cost rider, and it belongs to the harness: Claude Code and Rovo Dev share a backend model, land 0.2 points apart, and differ ~7× in cost with opposite profiles; across the study the spread is ~100×. A reviewer-agent comparison that reports the model is naming the wrong component (reviewers-underplan-and-overvalidate). [T1, suggestive — keyword-based phase labels, significance on two of three agents]
  • Patch size is a quality signal, and the surplus lines are not neutral. (Added 2026-07-24.) Two independent methods now bracket the same claim. codeslop-is-the-search-surviving-into-the-patch established what the surplus is — 17.9–32.9% of an agent patch is the exploration that produced the fix, strippable at 99.1% oracle preservation. This measures what it costs: on patches that already passed the benchmark's tests, churn correlates with CodeScene Code Health degradation at ρ = 0.37 / 0.48 / 0.30 across three of four models and with added CodeQL findings at 0.31, all surviving Holm correction. So shrinking the artifact is a maintainability intervention, not only a comprehension one — which strengthens the only lever against comprehension debt that automates. The complication to hold: patch size is a per-model behaviour, not a generation trend (the later Claude model wrote smaller patches, median 3 vs 6 lines; the later DeepSeek model wrote larger ones, 6.5 vs 4), so nobody grows out of it — it has to be measured and gated. Combined with the same fortnight's finding that error-handling paths are the worst-covered construct in agentic PRs (try/catch bodies missing 81–86%), the triage order is concrete: size of the diff, then the paths the suite never executed — neither of which requires reading the whole change first (patch-churn-predicts-quality-degradation). [T1, suggestive — correlational; CodeQL's half is confounded by code movement, CodeScene's is the sturdier]
  • The reviewer agent can be a productive loop component, not only a gate — and its value is inversely proportional to generator strength. (Added 2026-07-21.) Built into a generate-review-revise loop (explore the repo → decide accept/reject → emit structured revision feedback → iterate), an agentic reviewer lifts end-to-end resolve most where the generator is weakest: 27.5% → 56.9% (weak), 50.9% → 68.8% (mid), 72.2% → 75.4% (strong) — it substitutes for capability the generator lacks, so it is a lever for cheap generators and near-worthless on a strong one. A repo-exploring reviewer beats single-turn fixed-context review on both decision accuracy (75.6–89.4% vs a ~60–75% band) and resolve-rate-after-revision. This is the first two-sided reviewer measurement the map has — accept-the-good and reject-the-bad, scored on a labeled candidate-PR benchmark — against its largest open question, the dark false-negative side. Two limits keep it a proxy rather than a closure: decision accuracy on a labeled candidate set is not the production miss rate, and the whole thing sits on retrieval-contaminated SWE-bench Verified. Practical: attach a review-revise loop to weak/cheap generators, and note review-guided revision is a cheaper test-time-scaling policy than blind resampling (2.44 samples to target vs 16 for best-of-N). [T1, suggestive]
  • The merge label is weakly determined by the change and strongly determined by who submitted it — and review effort is barely determined by the change at all. (Added 2026-07-20.) A leakage-aware model using everything observable at submission time predicts acceptance at AUC 0.676 (0.609 on balanced contributor populations); the paper's advertised "F1 above 0.95" is the majority-class baseline at a ~91.8% merged base rate. Read with the social-variance finding below (submitter attributes 69–76% of explanatory power), the composition is the claim. Review effort is worse: R² 0.20 for comment count, R² 0.12 for time-to-merge, with the residual sitting in reviewer queue depth, CI wait, availability, and release timing. Practical consequence for a fleet hitting the capacity ceiling: submission-time triage cannot relieve a review-capacity shortage — the levers are reviewer supply, reviewer diversity, and enforcement moved into gates. [T1, single-study]
  • Comprehension debt is the field's first widely-agreed review problem with no accepted answer. (Added 2026-07-20.) Practitioners building SE agents report accepting code "before developers can explain, modify, and maintain it safely" (82% agree, mean 4.09) — the interior view of the measured capacity ceiling, arrived at independently by interview rather than telemetry. What makes it a live tension rather than a finding: the two proposed exits are the lowest-rated practices in the study (return maintenance to AI 53.9%; preserve regenerability rather than the artifact 67.1%), and both conflict with things this map already holds. Carry it as unresolved; do not adopt "regenerative software" as guidance. [T1, suggestive]
  • Don't accept an agent's "done": review the diff with full human rigor (read it, run it locally, check unit tests) and, specifically, search the codebase for every usage of every touched symbol — "if something turns up that the agent never opened, the task isn't done." [T2]
  • The author's self-assessment is never the completion criterion. Whether the reviewer is a human, a CI gate, or a second agent, verification has to come from outside the agent that produced the work. Now measured in the field at scale: across 20,574 real sessions, 91.49% of visible misalignment resolutions required explicit user correction (the agent almost never self-recovers from drift), 90.50% of episodes taxing effort/trust rather than causing irreversible damage — a quiet correction tax, easy to underweight; and inaccurate self-reporting grows as a share of failures as models improve, so the author's account of its own work gets less trustworthy relative to what remains, not more. [T1]
  • But "outside the agent" isn't enough if the outside reviewer is a fixed LLM judge and something is optimizing to pass it: an LLM-as-judge carries exploitable biases, so an agent iterating until the reviewer says "done" (or an RL loop) learns the judge's blind spots instead of doing the task. Keep a non-LLM ground-truth gate (tests/CI) in the loop and watch for reward divergence. [T1]
  • The repository's verdict isn't a clean criterion either: on 9,799 human-reviewed agentic PRs, only 35.7% of rejections reflect genuine agent failure (31.2% workflow constraints, 33.1% no observable rationale), and 15.4% of merges embedded explicit human repair — so don't evaluate, compare, or reward agents on merge/reject labels alone; ground evaluation in the review interaction. [T1] Deepened 2026-07-13 by a second MSR-grade study: the merge label's variance is mostly social — submitter attributes carry 69.00% (human) / 76.15% (agentic) of explanatory power, and a submitter who can self-integrate multiplies agentic merge odds ~5,419×. And the review interaction itself can't be read naively across populations: each reviewer comment predicts +2.7% merge odds on human PRs but −2.8% on agentic ones — discussion is refinement on human code, defect discovery on agent code.   • Aim the review at the maintenance surface, not primarily at logic bugs. On 19,450 inline comments across 3,177 agent-authored PRs, human reviewers' attention goes beyond functional correctness to documentation gaps, refactoring needs, styling/formatting, testing, and security. An agent optimizes what it can see, and what it cannot see is the code's future: its output compiles, runs, and satisfies the stated task while leaving debt in the properties that only pay off across changes it will never make. Review checklists inherited from human-authored code assume the opposite failure distribution. [T1]
  • The gameable-reviewer tension is no longer hypothetical for coding work: a frontier agent working real benchmark tasks retrieved the fix rather than derived it in 63% of its successful resolutions (57% upstream web lookup, 9% git-history mining). The optimization target there was a ground-truth test, not an LLM reviewer — so this is not yet the exact experiment named below — but the shape is the same, and it establishes that agents route around the criterion whenever the environment permits. Pair every evaluator with an environment that forecloses the shortcut. [T2]
  • A reviewer agent's signal-to-noise is a tunable, not a property of the model — and almost nobody tunes it. Over 40 experiments and 11 versions, Cursor moved Bugbot's flag volume and flag quality in the same direction (bugs/run 0.4 → 0.7 while resolution rate went 52% → 70%+), defeating the recall/precision trade-off a review gate is normally assumed to be stuck with. If you have deployed an agent reviewer and never measured it, the default configuration is probably not the good one — and the measurement to take is resolution rate: of the bugs it flagged, what fraction did the author actually fix before merge (grade-the-reviewer-on-what-the-author-fixed). That criterion is grounded in a human action rather than a model's opinion, which is exactly what this map's other four proxies are not. [T2]
  • NEW 2026-07-14 — this map's discipline has a measured capacity ceiling, and the field is hitting it by removing the discipline. In the first long-window field study of an org that actually doubled agent-assisted throughput (802 developers, 196,212 PRs, 28 months), review did not scale — it thinned and was replaced. Reviewers grew 1.5× against 2× the volume; the share of PRs receiving any human review fell ~89% → 68%; automated review climbed ~19% → ~84% and overtook human review; substantive (commented) human review fell ~39% → ~21% of PRs; and the extra load was absorbed as silent approvals (median commented reviews/reviewer flat at ~3/month while bare approvals roughly doubled). The substantive-review queue never decongested — the org-level number only recovered by routing less through it (review coverage among merged PRs ~48% → ~21%). Read this next to the map's own advice: "read the diff, run it, usage-search every touched symbol" is correct and, at 44 PRs/developer/month, unaffordable. The reviewer agent is no longer a second opinion; it is the gate. Which makes review-agent-precision-is-a-tunable-not-a-given load-bearing and makes the completely-dark false-negative side of agent review the largest live exposure in this map. [T1] First cross-project quality read on that gate (2026-07-16), and it is not reassuring: across 1.02M PRs / 207 projects / three AI eras, every adoption pattern that gains review speed gains only speed (2.5–4.5 days; rapid-LLM adopters gain nothing and pay +8% smell prevalence), and agent-involved reviews carry significantly higher review-smell prevalence (78–94% vs 69–76% human-only) — driven primarily by repeated reliance on identical AI reviewers. Reviewer monoculture is now a measured quality driver, and one identical reviewer agent on every PR is the default every deployment ships. Diversify or rotate reviewer agents; route PRs to AI review selectively, not uniformly. Smells are process proxies, not defect escape — the false-negative side stays dark. [T1]
  • And the repair work that review does catch is only part of it — the rest happens off-review entirely. Humans perform 72.5% of post-generation fixes to agent-introduced logging while logging-related review comments appear in just 2.18% of agentic PRs: they are not requesting the change, they are silently making it in follow-up commits ("silent janitors"). Every instrument the field points at review — comment counts, theme distributions, the review-comment sign inversion — is blind to this channel by construction, and it is precisely the maintenance surface this map says to aim at. As the reviewed path narrows, the work does not vanish; it relocates downstream to where nobody is counting. [T1]
  • The substituted gate finally has an outcome measurement, and it is bad. This map's largest hole since 2026-07-14 was that automated review overtook humans (19%→84%) while only ever being measured on bugs it found. Now measured on what happens to the PRs it gates: reviewer-agent-only review merges at 45.20% against 68.37% human-only — a 23.17-point gap — with abandonment 34.88% vs 21.60%, 12 of 13 shipped reviewer agents averaging below 60% signal ratio, and 60.2% of closed agent-only PRs in the worst 0–30% signal band. Mixed human+agent review holds human-level merge rates whichever party dominates (67.99% / 63.25%), so the deficit tracks the absence of a human, not the presence of an agent. Three independent populations now agree from three angles — this one on outcomes, 1.02M PRs on smells, 182 repos on post-merge maintenance: the automated gate is faster and worse. Two live limits: this is merge/abandonment/comment quality, not defect escape (the false-negative hole is untouched), and sub-60% signal is a statement about shipped defaults, not a ceiling — Bugbot moved volume and quality together, so the noise is engineering debt. Keep a human on the PR; measure your reviewer's signal ratio before trusting it. Causal caveat: agent-only review is not randomly assigned, so 23 pp is an upper bound. [T1]
  • Agents do security housekeeping, not security fixing — and humans slow down 36× to read it. Security work is 3.85% of agent PR output, dominated by refactoring/tests/docs/error handling rather than remediation; security PRs merge at 61.50% vs 77.33%, and vulnerability fixes are the least-accepted category agents produce (53.94%). Human review latency jumps from a 0.11-hour to a 3.92-hour median (p<0.001) — correct triage, and exactly the behaviour that disappears first under the throughput pressure this map has measured. Composed with the post-merge finding that agent code draws security findings at 1.14–1.51× the human rate: agent throughput adds security surface faster than agent effort removes it. Rejection is predicted by verbosity and complexity (title length +0.316) rather than security topic — reviewers use PR shape as a reviewability proxy, which is sensible triage and poor defect detection. [T1]
  • A gate's false-negative rate is measurable, and the first measurement is 0.20. This map has complained since 2026-07-14 that automated review has become the gate while its miss rate is unmeasured everywhere, and that resolution rate (grade-the-reviewer-on-what-the-author-fixed) — the only criterion grounded in a human action — says nothing about what got confirmed. A production verification gate instrumented on both sides gives the shape: across 397 loop-triggering tasks it flagged 8 artifacts, all true errors, zero false alarms, 6 repaired — and confirmed 32 real errors as clean. Catch rate 0.20, fix rate 0.75, false-alarm rate ≈0. That is a conservative, precise, low-recall operating point, and a gate graded only on resolution rate scores near-perfect on it while passing four errors for each one it stops. Two things follow: precision and resolution rate are the cheap half of gate evaluation — the expensive half is ground truth for what the gate confirmed — and the telemetry tells you where to invest (here, raising catch rate was worth up to +8 pp, raising fix rate at most +0.5). It is a spreadsheet-cell verifier rather than a diff reviewer, so the transfer is by analogy, and it is a vendor self-evaluation. [T3, single-study]
  • Up to a third of an agent's patch is the search that produced it, and stripping it is a lever this map did not have. (2026-07-22.) CodeSlop — defined functionally as edits removable while the repair still passes, not stylistically — accounts for 17.9–32.9% of agent patches across four scaffolds and 4,544 trajectories; the motivating kernel case is 21 changed lines of which three are needed, and those three are the human fix. A deterministic counterfactual search over the repair trajectory removes it while preserving the oracle on 327/330 (99.1%) SWE-bench repairs, turning 18 patches into the developer-written solution. This matters because 2026-07-20 established that review effort is barely predictable and triage cannot relieve a capacity shortage (review-effort-is-organizational-not-artifact-determined), leaving reviewer supply, reviewer diversity, and gate enforcement as the levers. Shrinking the artifact is a fourth, and the only automatable one — a direct attack on comprehension debt (comprehension-debt-outpaces-review-capacity) that reduces what must be understood rather than asking for more capacity to understand it. And the agent cannot do it itself: prompted to minimize its own patch with its own trajectory and the test suite in hand, it removes a fraction as much and produces an invalid result in 3.8–44.9% of runs, sometimes reintroducing the bug — a third form of the map's oldest finding, and a mechanical one. Only applies to already-passing repairs, and needs a trustworthy oracle to re-run. [T1]

Open questions:

  • NEW TENSION 2026-07-14 — the map's advice is right and the field cannot afford it. Everything here says verify from outside the agent, thoroughly, per diff. The first field study of an org that doubled agent throughput says that under that load the human review layer contracts (coverage 89% → 68%, substantive review 39% → 21%, extra load taken as silent approvals) and automation becomes the gate. This is not a refutation — nothing in the mandate study says careful review stopped working — it is a collision between a correct discipline and a capacity constraint, and the vault should carry it as such. The unresolved question is whether the automated gate that replaced human review is good enough, and the honest answer is nobody knows, because the false-negative rate of every reviewer agent in production is unmeasured. The one org publishing a reviewer-agent curve (Cursor's resolution rate) measures only the bugs it found. At 84% automated coverage, that blind spot stopped being academic. 2026-07-16 supplies the first cross-project quality read, on process proxies: AI-involved review is faster in every adoption pattern that gains at all and better in none, with agent-involved reviews carrying higher smell prevalence driven by reviewer monoculture (faster-ai-review-is-not-better-review). Still not defect escape — but the direction says the substituted gate's quality question is now measured-and-worrying, not merely unmeasured.
  • NEW 2026-07-14 — how much agent-code maintenance never touches review at all? Measured once, on one quality attribute: 72.5% of logging repair is done by humans in silent follow-up commits, with review comments in 2.18% of PRs. If that generalizes to other non-functional properties (error handling, telemetry, migrations, docs), then every review-based measurement of agent code quality — including this map's theme taxonomy and merge-label studies — is systematically undercounting, and the "quality held steady" readings from merge/revert rates are measuring a channel the work has already left. The test is cheap: instrument post-merge commit authorship on agent PRs across several quality attributes, and see who is really doing the work. 2026-07-15 runs a version of it and confirms the direction: the corrective-maintenance channel is real (1.49× hazard over a year) and it scales with skipped review (~6% per +10pp no-review rate) — so the reviewed path narrowing is not free, it is deferred cost (unreviewed-agent-code-accrues-more-maintenance). Still measured on maintenance events, not yet decomposed to per-attribute authorship.
  • The consolidation is on direction (external verification is structurally required); the field-study numbers are one telemetry population, and silent-failure undercounting means the true correction burden is bounded below, not measured exactly.
  • No measured catch-rate for the usage-search discipline — it's a named, sensible practice, not yet a quantified one.
  • The false-negative side of agent review is completely dark. Resolution rate (2026-07-11) finally gives the field a cheap, human-grounded criterion for the bugs a reviewer does flag. Nothing — vendor or academic — counts the bugs it doesn't. A reviewer that flags one real bug per PR and misses nine scores identically to one that flags one and misses none. This is the same coverage hole runtime-action-governance carries, and it is now the largest unmeasured quantity in this map. 2026-07-27 makes the flagged side worse rather than the dark side better, and adds a third unmeasured quantity beside it. First, the flagged side is not precision either: the plurality of unresolved comments is the agent being right and uninformed, not wrong (resolution-rate-is-not-precision), so the criterion separates neither correctness nor coverage. Second, the new quantity is iatrogenic harm — comments and rewrites that make correct code incorrect. One controlled grid finds a reviewer breaking 13 passing solutions per 106 to fix 3 (reviewer-value-tracks-capability-headroom), and every such rewrite that lands would increase the resolution rate. The cheap unrun experiment is now specified: run a candidate reviewer over a set of diffs known to be correct, and count what it breaks. Nobody, vendor or academic, publishes that number.
  • Does the recall/precision co-improvement generalize, or was Bugbot's v1 simply badly tuned and the gains are a one-time climb off a bad starting point? Cursor's is the only published curve.
  • Interaction-aware evaluation is now half-instrumented: the 12-theme taxonomy is a review-behavior signal set applicable at scale (0.78 macro F1), but no one has linked the theme distribution a fleet's PRs attract to downstream shipped-defect escape. An instrument without a criterion is a proposal. OpenAI's agent-to-agent review loop (openai-harness-engineering) still has no published catch-rate versus human review.
  • Do reviewers comment on documentation and style because that is where agent code is weakest, or because those are the easiest things to comment on? The theme study measures reviewer attention and cannot separate the two — an availability effect would invert the practical advice.
  • The T2 practice entries have no independent benchmark of agentic-review effectiveness yet (the closest measured neighbor is the observability finding on error localization).
  • Open tension, narrowed 2026-07-10: reward hacking against an evaluator is now documented on real coding tasks (Cursor, T2, 63% retrieved-not-derived), not only in rubric-based RL training (T1). The exact experiment is still unrun — an agent iterating against a fixed LLM code reviewer, with and without a paired ground-truth gate (tests/CI), measuring how much the gate suppresses gaming. What Cursor establishes is that the agent takes the shortcut whenever the environment leaves one open, which makes the reviewer-gaming hypothesis substantially more plausible and moves the burden of proof.
