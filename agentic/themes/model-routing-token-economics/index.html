Model routing & token economics — map

Coined 2026-07-05 during the nightly radar — first evidence in the previously-empty "Model routing & token economics" group. Flagged for David's review. Moved to consolidating 2026-07-14: a second independent T1 (SCATE) reaches the map's founding structure from a different object — allocating iterations rather than models — and the two compose into one positive instruction. Status moves both ways; the specific SeqRoute numbers remain unreplicated, and the upgrade rests on the composed claim, not on either paper's effect size. Flagged for David's review.

Status: consolidating

What we believe now:

  • Close the ledger past the inference bill, and price the cache before you price the model. (Added 2026-07-20, T3 — structure only, magnitudes not portable.) Two results from one production deployment. (a) Under agentic workloads with high prefix reuse, prompt caching can make the frontier API the cheap tier: a 99.3% hit rate over 15.31B input-side tokens cut realized spend 88.6% to an effective $0.573/M processed tokens, below a locally-served quantized 744B-class MoE at $2.83/M amortized. Cache design is a pricing decision, and a router scoring models on published per-token rates is using the wrong number. (b) A cheaper configuration relocates cost into repair labor rather than removing it — Fix Commit Ratio 45.9%→74.9%, net-new share 54.1%→25.1%, Mantel-Haenszel OR 3.61 holding inside every difficulty tier. Net of symmetric labor pricing the cheap arm still won TCO by 40.1% under shared GPU allocation (and lost by 43.8% under dedicated reservation), so the lesson is not "cheap models don't pay" — it is that an inference-only ledger does not close. And the routing frontier this produces is monotonic with no dominating policy: across 10,000 bootstrap resamples, no policy sending any tier to the local model matched the pure-API defect profile. Hybrid routing buys savings with defects, not instead of them. [T3, single-study — n=1 developer, non-randomized, self-study, regex-classified outcome]
  • Allocate agent spend sequentially, under a global budget, with a policy learned from cheap observable state — not greedily, and not on a fixed schedule. Two independent T1s now reach this from opposite objects, and they compose rather than merely coexist. SeqRoute allocates models across a session: a per-query greedy router spends its budget early and degrades on the hard later turns, while a budget-aware sequential policy learns "delayed gratification" (6.0–73.5% cost savings, bankruptcy <1%, quality held on the Pareto frontier). SCATE allocates iterations on one task — including the decision to quit — with a cost-penalized contextual bandit, and beats both the greedy single shot and the fixed 8-iteration schedule on quality and cost (82.8% line / 74.7% branch / 64.7% mutation at $0.693, vs 77.3 / 70.2 / 59.6 at $0.797). Same economics, different unit. The general form: the unit of routing is the next increment of effort. [T1 ×2]
  • The right budget is agent-conditional, so a fixed iteration cap is wrong in both directions at once. The same framework, same features, same reward, learns opposite policies for two agents: against a strong-zero-shot agent (Claude Code, 84.3% line coverage at iteration 1) it stops early on 95.0% of tasks at 2.1 iterations average; against a weaker, cheaper one (Gemini-CLI) it runs 7.9 iterations and reaches for its analysis tool three times as often (33.8% vs 10.0%). The default cap shipped in essentially every harness over-spends on the agent that finished at step 2 and under-spends on the one still climbing at step 8. [T1]
  • Expose cost/quality as a deploy-time dial, not a compiled-in operating point — different callers (CI bot, interactive user, nightly batch) want it set differently; one policy can navigate the cost-quality Pareto frontier without retraining. [T1]
  • Treat cost as a first-class selection signal, not a footnote: among models of comparable quality, operational cost can differ by up to three orders of magnitude, so quality-parity does not imply cost-parity and picking on a quality leaderboard alone can silently cost ~1000× more than an equally-good alternative. This is the dispersion that makes session-budget routing worth doing — and iteration-parity does not imply value-parity either. [T1]
  • The cheap tier is an engineering target, not a fixed menu item. Routing assumes a slate of models you pick among; but a harness optimizer that mines failure trajectories and auto-discovers tool/ instruction/loop adaptations can move task difficulty off the model and into the harness, letting a small model close much of the gap to a frontier LLM — best case 89.7% of LLM performance at 4% of cost, headline ~90% cheaper. So before routing away from the cheap model, consider adapting its harness up to the bar. Conditional (best for repetitive workflows and capable-enough SLMs; 16/21 task-model pairs improved, 7/21 fully closed the gap) and the optimizer's one-time search compute is the cost to weigh against recurring inference savings. A second, independent cost lever is the tool surface itself: restricting a coding agent to a single execute_code channel held pass rate and was cheaper in 3/4 regime×agent cells — cache-adjusted cost, not pass rate, decides the cheapest surface, and it depends jointly on task regime and agent design. [T1 ×2, suggestive]
  • The routing policy is not portable across agents. Each agent gets its own retrained policy at a real one-time cost ($48.40 / 13 h for one agent; $127 / 22.7 h for another) — the method transfers, the policy does not, and nobody has run the zero-shot cross-agent transfer experiment. Budget this as a per-agent fixed cost, not a one-off. [T1, single-study]
  • Knowing when to stop is a correctness control, not only a cost control — so the fixed iteration cap is wrong in both currencies at once. (2026-07-22.) learn-when-to-stop-not-just-which-model established stopping as the routing unit and priced it (more coverage for less money than a forced 8-iteration budget). A second, independent arrival makes the harder claim: in a noisy verify-repair loop, stopping on the sign of the estimated marginal gain rather than on acceptance votes reaches +60.6 pp of true validity at 0.72 rounds instead of 5 — seven times cheaper and sixty points better, because past the loop's interior optimum each additional round is buying damage (repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum). This map has treated the iteration budget as a spend-versus-quality dial; it isn't one on the far side of the optimum, where both move together. The transferable engineering detail is that the decision needs only sign identifiability, not accurate parameter recovery, so it calibrates from a few hundred cross-fitted samples — and when the verifier's discrimination approaches zero the sign is unidentifiable and the correct policy is an estimation-free retreat toward not repairing at all. Measured on 3–8B open models on a math-heavy adversarial setting; the mechanism transfers, the magnitude does not. [T1, suggestive]
  • Staffing a stage with a small trained specialist is a distinct spend lever from routing between frontier models. A production system runs each loop stage on the lightest model that performs it reliably — 0.5–4B post-trained specialists at 0.02–0.1× frontier per-call cost, 4-bit quantized — which is what makes "verify every step" affordable as a default rather than a luxury, and its 0.5B step classifier is credited with ~4 pp of end-to-end accuracy at net-negative cost. That is move-task-difficulty-from-the-model-to-the-harness applied at the granularity of a loop stage rather than a whole task, and it composes with restrict-the-tool-surface-to-cut-cost-not-capability: the cheapest configuration is a design question about surfaces and stages, not a menu choice between published per-token rates. Vendor self-report with proprietary weights; the cost multiples are internal serving estimates and end-to-end latency overhead is unmeasured. [T3, single-study]

  • 2026-07-21 — interaction edge from perf-optimization-benchmark-reliability (Chen/Sun/Shi/ Lo/Jiang, arXiv, "Are Performance-Optimization Benchmarks Reliably Measuring Coding Agents?", 2026-07-01, v2 2026-07-16, T1, strong): a caution for anyone routing on published efficiency numbers. The runtime/speedup figures these benchmarks report are measured on a substrate where the reference patch itself fails its own validity across cloud machine types (valid on 38% / 8% / 82% of GSO / SWE-Perf / SWE-fficiency tasks), so a per-task speedup is a machine-dependent artifact, not a constant to route on.   • 2026-07-20 — inference-economics-enterprise-agents (Peng/Lin/Lee, PEGAVERSE / Pegatron, arXiv, "Inference Economics of Enterprise Coding Agents", 2026-07-13, T3, single-study): the first source here that prices a routing decision against downstream engineering labor instead of tokens, and it lands two results pointing opposite ways. First, prompt caching inverts the cloud-versus-local intuition. Over 28 days a Claude Code + Opus configuration processed 15.31 billion input-side tokens (~480k prompt tokens per request) at a 99.3% cache hit rate, cutting realized spend from a nominal $77,059 to $8,785 (−88.6%) — an effective $0.573 per million processed tokens, below a locally-served NVFP4-quantized 744B-class MoE at $2.83/M amortized (shared 4×B200 seat) and far below $11.87/M dedicated. So "expensive model vs cheap model" is substantially a property of how well the harness reuses prefixes, which makes cache design a pricing decision and adds a lever below both agent-cost-spans-orders-of-magnitude and move-task-difficulty-from-the-model-to-the-harness. A router scoring models on published per-token rates is using the wrong number for any high-reuse workload — and agentic coding is that workload by construction. Second, the cheap configuration relocated the cost rather than removing it: Fix Commit Ratio 45.9% → 74.9%, net-new share 54.1% → 25.1%, Mantel-Haenszel OR 3.61 [2.46, 5.30] stratified by difficulty with per-tier ORs of 4.88 / 2.63 / 3.81 (the premium holds inside every tier, and the harder period was the cheaper-repair one), plus behavioural degradation across six timestamp-derived indicators (debugging-spiral share 35.0%→69.8%, longest repair run 18→58 commits, inter-commit interval 5.9→12.7 min). Priced with symmetric labor assumptions the cheap arm still wins TCO — 40.1% saved under shared GPU allocation, 43.8% more expensive under dedicated reservation, with a four-axis sweep keeping shared-allocation savings positive across 9–56%. The transferable part is the replay: repricing the pooled 613 commits under four routing policies yields a monotonic frontier (pure local $7,388 @ 73.4% FCR → high-tier-to-API $8,412 @ 64.6% → high+med $9,890 @ 57.9% → pure API $11,419 @ 49.1%), pairwise-disjoint bootstrap intervals, ordering stable in 10,000/10,000 resamples, and no policy sending any tier to the local model matches the pure-API defect profile. That answers this map's standing open question — does routing buy a free lunch — with a no, for one setting. Caps, and they are the reason this is T3: n=1 developer, non-randomized, sequential periods, and a self-study (the developer analyzed is the first author); FCR is a regex over commit messages reviewed by a rater explicitly not blind to the hypothesis, with no kappa; days and commits from one developer are not independent replicates; no quantization ablation; no data release; Taiwan-market and list-price parameters, with the authors noting a ~45% negotiated API discount would erase the advantage. Cite the structure and the frontier's shape, never the magnitudes. See prompt-caching-inverts-cloud-versus-local-economics, cheaper-inference-relocates-cost-into-repair-labor.
  • 2026-07-19 — execution-scope-estimation-e3 (arXiv, "Do AI Agents Know When a Task Is Simple?", 2026-07-14, T1, suggestive): a spend axis this map has been looking past. Every allocation unit here so far — SeqRoute's model choice, SCATE's iteration, the tool surface — is decided during a task. This measures the waste committed before the first tool call, and finds it distributed opposite to intuition: redundancy (realized cost over an oracle minimum) runs 22.1 at the easiest tier, 11.0 at cross-file, 5.4 at repo-level under a max-context-first policy, because the absolute cost of "gather everything" is roughly flat while the oracle floor grows with difficulty. The relative waste peaks exactly where nobody is looking for it — on the one-line edits that feel too cheap to optimize — and a scope-estimating policy keeps it flat instead (0.64 / 0.26 / 0.73). That inverts the usual budgeting instinct, which puts the guardrails on the expensive-looking tasks. Two honest qualifications: the headline −85% cost / −91% tokens is a property of a deterministic capability-controlled simulator with no LLM in the loop, and the live gpt-4o arm is much milder (−18% vs read-everything, −4% vs plain ReAct, one library, 5 tasks × 3 runs); and E3's edge over a strong adaptive-retrieval baseline is concentrated on the easy tiers (45% cheaper at L1, 43% at L2) and reverses on the hardest tier (0.73 vs 0.42 ACRR — E3 is costlier there). So this is a cheap-task optimization that should not be sold as a general one. Composes with rather than duplicates learn-when-to-stop-not-just-which-model: that decides when to quit, this decides how much to look at before starting. Primary map: context-engineering. See agent-redundancy-concentrates-on-the-simplest-tasks.
  • 2026-07-18 — interaction edge from scaffolding-evolution-agent-quality (Ben Sghaier/Li/ Adams/Hassan, Polytechnique Montréal / Queen's, arXiv, "Don't Blame the Large Language Model: How Scaffolding Evolution Shapes Coding Agent Quality", 2026-07-04, T1, strong): a cost channel this map has not been watching — spend drifts upward on its own. Across 35 sequential releases of one scaffolding with the model frozen, average token consumption rose 391K → 668K (+70%) with a strong monotonic trend (ρ=0.743, p<0.0001) while resolve rate showed no improving trend (23.0–39.0%, ρ=0.208, p=0.231). Every routing and budget policy in this map is calibrated against a per-task cost that a routine dependency upgrade can move 70% without notice, and move-task-difficulty-from-the-model-to-the-harness's optimism about engineering the cheap tier assumes a stable substrate this says you don't have. Operational rule: pin the scaffolding version like you pin the model, and re-measure cost after every harness upgrade rather than after every model change only. Caveats: n=1 scaffolding studied deeply, 50-task slice, single run per release. Primary map: agent-harness-architecture. See scaffolding-releases-swing-quality-without-improving-it.
  • 2026-07-15 — better-harnesses-smaller-models (Yang/Zhao/Wu/Kästner, CMU, arXiv, "Better Harnesses, Smaller Models: Building 90% Cheaper Agents via Automated Harness Adaptation", 2026-07-09, T1): the map's routing unit was model-or-iteration; this adds a third lever below both — re-engineer the cheap tier until it clears the bar.   • 2026-07-14 — scate-supervised-test-generation (Gu/Nashid/Mesbah, UBC, arXiv, "SCATE: Learning to Supervise Coding Agents for Cost-Effective Test Generation", 2026-07-09, T1, single-study): the independent confirmation this map's open questions have asked for since 2026-07-05 — arriving in a different object class, which is why it counts as composition rather than replication. Open questions:

  • The upgrade is on structure, not on numbers, and the distinction is load-bearing. SeqRoute's 6–73.5% and SCATE's coverage/cost deltas are each one setup's, in different object classes, and neither has been reproduced by anyone. What is now two-source is the shape — sequential, budgeted, learned-from-cheap-state allocation beats greedy and fixed. A direct replication of either headline number is still missing, and a study that ran both units (model choice and stopping rule) under one budget on one task set would be the first real test of whether they compose in practice or compete for the same savings.
  • Still no evidence on routing quality — how often the policy sends a hard query to a weak model, or stops an agent that was one iteration from success. Both papers measure cost saved and quality retained on average; neither measures the tail. A stopping rule that quits early on the 5% of tasks that mattered most would look excellent on both papers' metrics.
  • The stopping rule inherits the vault's coverage problem. SCATE's supervisor stops when measured coverage plateaus — a proxy. It cannot know whether the untested branches are the important ones, and lazy-generation-caps-agent-test-coverage shows the agent's own sense of "done" is worthless here. Routing on a proxy is evaluation-integrity's standing warning applied to spend: a contaminated or shallow quality estimate routes on a false axis.
  • No cross-agent policy transfer has been tested. The per-agent retraining cost is small in absolute terms ($48–127) but it is a recurring bill every time an agent or model version changes — which, per harness-is-tuned-per-model-and-version, is constantly. Nobody has measured how fast a routing policy goes stale across a model upgrade.
  • Interaction with the harness layer is asserted, not measured: Cursor reports routing decisions live in the harness, but no source ties session-budget routing to harness architecture with numbers.

See also:

  • context-engineering — token-economics sibling: both treat a session's resources (context window / model budget) as finite and decaying, to be managed across the whole horizon, not per step.
  • agent-harness-architecture — where a routing policy actually lives. SCATE's supervisor is a harness component with a stopping rule, which makes it the economic face of separate-the-generator-from-the-evaluator: the thing outside the agent that decides it isn't done.
  • ci-cd-for-agents — SCATE's object is the test suite, i.e. the CI backstop; the cost of producing a gate is itself a routing decision, and an under-covering suite is a cheap gate that isn't one.
  • evaluation-integrity — quality-parity ≠ cost-parity assumes you can measure quality, and the published record has no cost axis at all (0/8 benchmark papers). A router needs both numbers; the field publishes neither reliably.
  • observability — the 1000× cost-dispersion finding comes from RAMP, whose primary contribution is runtime observability; measuring cost per stage is itself an observability signal.
