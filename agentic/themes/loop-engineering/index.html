What we believe now:

  • The repair signal is a location, not a message. Holding the information roughly constant and varying where the run resumes from, one symptom-anchored intervention repairs nearly three times what three complete reruns do — and intervening at the visible symptom is the worst option tested, worse than intervening at random. (Added 2026-08-27, T1 — strong.) On 536 annotated failures across three frameworks: symptom-targeted intervention 20.15% at one replay, unguided restart 6.90% at three complete attempts, self-reflection 4.29%, critic-agent 3.73%, random node 3.73%, last node 1.31% — the last two on the identical single-replay budget, which is what rules out selective replay as the mechanism. Last-node performing worst of everything tested is the sharp version: repairing from where the failure became visible, which is what every self-correction loop does by construction, is the single worst place to intervene. This completes the 2026-08-23 thread — showing-a-failed-attempt-anchors-the-retry found the failed attempt costs accuracy independent of any diagnosis attached, execution-feedback-beat-a-specification-patch-at-a-fourteenth-the-cost found the cheap signal wins — by naming the variable that does carry the effect. The design consequence: a repair loop needs an anchor policy, and the harness must be able to resume from an arbitrary earlier node, which is a capability rather than a prompt. Bound the authors put first: target selection and repair guidance were evaluated jointly, so the isolating arm has not been run. See one-symptom-targeted-intervention-beat-three-full-reruns.
  • A loop's stop decision is the boundary where its authority actually lives, and it becomes checkable the moment the agent has to hand over a receipt the runtime can replay rather than a judgement it can assert. (Added 2026-08-26, T1 — strong.) Under Evidence-Carrying Termination an agent may return complete only when a typed certificate binds every required answer slot to a valid in-scope receipt in an immutable ledger and a deterministic replay of a declared, closed-language transform reconstructs the claimed value; any failure returns continue with reason codes, and internal exceptions fail closed (a-stop-decision-needs-a-receipt-not-a-judgement). Unsafe completions went to 0/288 against 252/288 for an inspected production termination critic on a locked static grid, and — in a separately prespecified, frozen 576-trajectory closed loop — 0/66 premature unsupported terminations against 40/66 for the critic's faithful controller, while supported completion was 97/132 against 92/132, clearing a prespecified −10-point noninferiority margin. The overhead is stated and small: +0.90 decision turns, +4,653 tokens, +USD 0.00246 per trajectory. The finding underneath the numbers is the one this map should carry: four independently designed stop signals — a completion token, a self-check, a permissive heuristic, and a real production critic — failed on exactly the same 252 snapshots and scored identical macro-F1, so they are not four options but one instrument (the-cheap-stop-signals-all-fail-on-the-same-snapshots). And the upgrade most teams reach for, a full-trace LLM critic, buys its improvement with the opposite error: 18 of 36 clean runs refused in the static grid, 21 of 66 in the closed loop, and the lowest supported completion of all four policies. A judge with more context does not become a verifier. One distinction transfers to any loop design: stuck is a separate controller decision requiring its own recovery-availability rule — verifier failure alone neither licenses completion nor proves recovery impossible. Six synthetic families with injected faults, so the rates are properties of the grid and the ordering and coincidence of the failure sets are what transfer; soundness is relative to a trusted contract and an honest adapter; replay covers a closed transform language, not judgement; no independent reproduction yet. [T1, strong]
  • Loops are running in public and the architecture everyone prescribes for them is not committed anywhere — and the principle the discourse actually converges on is that verification cost, not model capability, bounds how much you can hand off. (Added 2026-08-25, T1 — strong on the census, suggestive on the review.) The first measurement of this practice with a denominator. Scan 36,710 repositories already classified as engineered software projects (a frame defined without reference to loops), inspect every one of the 256 heuristic matches by hand, and confirm autonomous agent loops in 217 of them — 0.59%, at precision 0.868 [0.820, 0.907]. Then the split: triggers are committed (253 repositories, all but one of 323 occurrences a GitHub Actions workflow) and everything downstream of the trigger is not — zero stop conditions, zero verifier subagents referenced by a loop artifact, zero budget or pause-control files, zero cost logs with measured values, and of 1,334 candidate state files with real commit histories across 637 repositories, none carries the append-only run-log shape (the-loop-architecture-is-prescribed-and-not-committed). Read the zeros carefully, because the source does: parallel isolation is genuinely untraceable (worktrees are local), and the empty goal row is partly a fixed-phrase artifact — manual annotation found a stop-condition mechanism somewhere in 55 of 256 repositories, mostly as terminal states or retry caps inside committed prompts. What survives is that the artifacts you would need to audit a loop after the fact are the ones nobody commits. The gray-literature half supplies the design vocabulary and its own evidence grading: the building blocks are consistent across twelve sources but the sources are not independent (at least three build explicitly on one origin author), the maker/checker separation is the most-emphasized block, and every headline benefit — ~1,300 agent-written PRs/week at one company, 423 Firefox security fixes in a month — is self-report with no dataset, while the failure catalogue is documented far better than the benefit one (verification-cost-is-the-bound-on-delegation). The bound worth adopting: raise autonomy only as far as you can check the result at acceptable cost, and treat agency (how far one agent proceeds unattended) and orchestration (how many run at once) as independent axes rather than one ladder. Two method lessons that transfer to anyone monitoring their own loops: a successful workflow run is not evidence an agent ran — one repository logged 6,290 successful scheduled runs with zero agent executions because its gating search matched nothing — and fast automatic PR review is often a personal account-level auto-request invisible to the repository. Recall is unmeasurable by construction (it would need repositories known not to run loops), so 0.59% is a floor on committed-trace loops, not an estimate of the practice.
  • When you tell an agent its result is wrong, the part that changes behaviour is the enumeration of what it could call instead — not the diagnosis, not the witness, not the timing. (Added 2026-08-23, T1 — strong.) Five matched contrasts varying one part of a violation receipt at a time on the same 114 paired workflows, interpretations registered before execution: strip the recovery-tool list and the identical warning performs at baseline (+0.9 pp, not clearing zero); restore it and +11.4 pp returns (p = .028), while a localized expected-versus-observed witness beats a generic caution by −2.6 and on-time beats deferred delivery by +0.9 — both nulls bounded at an ≈18-point detectable effect rather than asserted (the-active-content-of-a-warning-is-the-list-of-things-to-do-instead). The behavioural trace says why: after a receipt the agent switches to a listed substitute 48.6% of the time and calls some other tool 33.5% — it is re-selecting from an enumerated set, not re-reasoning about the failure. This is recovery-actions-are-not-a-capability-ladder with a mechanism: what an agent does after a failure is governed by what it can see to do. A second result inside the same ablation changes how you would buy a detector: a schema-only version recovers most of the completion gain (+10.0 vs +15.0, head-to-head p = .42) while firing 166 receipts to 111 with only 12.0% able to name more than one recovery action against 91.9% — precision and affordance quality are what the better detector buys, and neither appears in the completion delta. Scope condition, stated by the authors as a deployment rule: gains concentrate where the fault blocks completion (+15 to +28 points where faulted baselines complete ≤16%, ≤+6.3 where most complete anyway), and every positive study uses a fault-enriched sample, so net benefit is bounded by fault prevalence — π ≳ 0.32 at the unfavourable interval endpoints. [T1, strong]
  • An execution trace and a document patch are both repair signals, and on the same failures the trace wins at a fourteenth of the cost — except exactly where the document is trying to overrule a strong prior. (Added 2026-08-23, T1 — strong.) A pre-registered head-to-head, one repair round each: showing every implementer its own psql stderr costs $0.03, cuts broken-code errors 85 → 46, and confirms both registered expectations; patching the specification costs $0.41 and fails all three of its own (execution-feedback-beat-a-specification-patch-at-a-fourteenth-the-cost). The scope condition is measured in both directions rather than inferred: specification patching worked where the added rule arbitrated between plausible defaults and failed where it contradicted a strong implementer prior — which is the bad news, because the rules with the most leverage are precisely the ones fighting a prior. The two mechanisms own disjoint divergence buckets, so they are complementary rather than competing (the same relationship an-executable-check-and-a-model-judge-fail-on-different-inputs reports for checkers), and the leftover is the useful part: roughly 60 events of missing behaviour are owned by neither, because a document cannot make an absence visible and an execution loop never trips on code that quietly fails to raise. Its natural owner is a test that asserts the error fires — make-the-agent-write-the-contract-before-the-test arriving as a measured gap rather than a preference. [T1, strong]
  • Four ways of adding a checker, all worth nothing, and then one worth ten points — the lever is the shape of the interaction, not the number of checkers. (Added 2026-08-20, T1 — strong.) A six-method construction on one benchmark with one model, each step adding exactly one design choice motivated by the measured failure of the last, gives this map a controlled ablation of its own subject. Zero-shot 77%; self-critique by the same agent 77% (the map's oldest finding, restated — an-independent-checker-beats-the-same-model-checking-itself); one separate external reviewer 77%; two independent reviewers 75%, worse than doing nothing. Independent samples do not push past the level. Then the shape changes rather than the count: a parallel three-reviewer-plus-meta-reviewer system breaks out at 82% with five agents, and a three-agent loop where a critic audits the reviewer over a frozen artifact reaches 87% — two reviewing roles against four (interaction-shape-beats-agent-count). The frozen-artifact separation is the structural idea: the inner loop exchanges review text only, so the agents cannot jointly rewrite the solution while still disagreeing about what is wrong, and — because it never touches the artifact — the inner loop can be lifted out and scored on its own, which is how its false-consensus failure was found at all (two-agents-told-to-agree-will-agree). A loop whose stages can be scored independently is a loop whose failures can be located. The protocol also survives being written down as prose and handed to an autonomous agent with no orchestrator forcing any step (75.2% against 71.6% zero-shot on 500 real issues), which is a real constraint on how elaborate a loop can usefully be. And the trade is priced rather than hidden: ~4.5× zero-shot tokens, Pareto-frontier on all three benchmarks, with the authors' own cases showing the loop moving a 45-line symptom patch to a ~20-line root-cause patch that passes, and turning a reviewer's speculative aside into an out-of-scope patch that fails where a smaller one passed — so it helps when disagreement redirects toward the true bug and hurts when it expands the repair, an argument for intervene-on-a-trigger-not-on-a-schedule applied to the review loop itself. [T1, strong]
  • The within-agent limb has been measuring loops that revisit one artifact; here is the other way to spend budget, and a scaffold that makes independent re-runs compound instead of repeat. (Added 2026-08-19, T1, strong.) Every interior optimum on this map — repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum and its two independent replications — describes a loop returning to the same artifact until it degrades. Independent re-sampling is the other axis, and it is usually a lottery: more draws from one distribution. Grounding the agent in an explicitly extracted behavioural contract changes that, and the signature is that the advantage widens monotonically with k — +4.2 points at one run (p=0.3075), +6.0 at two, +7.2 at three (marginal), +8.7 and +9.8 at four and five, both p=0.0352 — while pass@k, the rate at which the generated artifact compiles and passes at all, is flat across the whole ladder (94.4% vs 94.2% at k=1; 98.9% for both at k=5) (a-contract-converts-extra-runs-into-new-paths-not-rerolls). A constant advantage per draw would give a flat gap; a widening one means each additional draw explores somewhere the baseline's draws do not. The distributional evidence backs it rather than resting on the trend: per-task detection is strongly bi-modal — caught in almost every run or almost none — and the scaffold moves bi-modality 65.6% → 57.8% specifically by shifting tasks out of the 0% bucket into the 20–80% conditional region, with 12 uniquely detected against the baseline's 3. That is the mechanism by which extra draws start paying, and read against resampling-headroom-is-inverse-to-capability it says something this map should carry: resampling headroom is not a fixed property of the model, it is how much of the task distribution sits at exactly zero, and a scaffold that moves mass off zero creates headroom that sampling then converts. The budget arithmetic is the rare favourable one — +38.0% tokens for +18.8% yield, so 1.16× per unit of found work, the opposite direction from failure-quadruples-the-compute-of-a-turn and failed-recoveries-burn-compute-not-terminate. [T1 — one model family, one organisation's codebase, 90 tasks with single-file fixes; bi-modality measured over five runs per task so the extreme buckets are coarse; McNemar's p is computed per k on discordant pairs and no test of the widening itself is reported, so the monotonicity is descriptive; the ladder stops at k=5 with the gap still growing]
  • The near-quadratic cost of adding agents is a handshake, paid once — and on large teams it stops being paid at all. (Added 2026-08-18, T1, strong — the first instrument that measures the coordination rather than the run's output.) Represent a run as a temporal network in which agents and files are both nodes and every message, write and read is a timestamped, costed edge; apply it to 1,902 graded runs on a pinned model. Messages do grow at exponent 1.92 (CI [1.80, 2.05]), reproducing to two decimal places across sessions. But traffic per ordered pair falls from ~3 to 1.27 as the team grows, messages get shorter at every step in team size, and 90% of the pairs a run will ever use appear by τ ≈ 0.2 — the growth is a round of introductions, made early, after which a small established core carries the rest. On a sixteen-step chain where every agent owns exactly one step, so the team cannot be running out of work, growth stops: 21.4 / 47.0 / 46.8 messages at four / eight / sixteen agents, slope 0.00 (CI [−0.34, 0.34]), the break significant against a pre-registered hypothesis. The mechanism is a channel switch — named-peer messages 34.6 → 12.2 while broadcasts rise 12.3 → 34.0, with twelve of twenty sixteen-agent runs coordinating by broadcast alone (the-quadratic-cost-of-a-team-is-mostly-a-handshake). A budget or topology sized for sustained all-to-all messaging provides for traffic these teams do not produce. Also measured: an overstaffed team is not idle — the four agents holding no part of a four-part specification send 62% of all messages, because an agent that holds nothing must ask the others for everything.
  • Naming one agent the coordinator in its prompt creates no communication hub and buys no reliable success. What decomposition actually creates is interfaces, and every interface needs an owner. (Added 2026-08-18, T1, strong.) A disparity-filter backbone leaves 0 of 1,170 channels at eight agents on the distributed task and 2 of 1,077 on the chained task — under the coordinator condition as much as the flat one. On success, the apparent four-agent advantage (8/10 against 5/10) dissolves once the second byte-identical flat collection is pooled (8/10 against 12/20, p=0.42); the one contrast surviving correction is a reversal that fails to reproduce under a 244-run sealed replication, where flat and coordinator are level under every file policy. Both the effect and its reversal were noise (naming-a-coordinator-creates-no-structural-leadership). The constructive half is the sharpest failure mechanism this map has: an eight-step chain succeeds 9/10 at two and four agents and fails all ten at eight, every time on the same seam — round at each step or once at the end — because at eight agents the convention falls between two owners and belongs to neither. Rounding was discussed in all ten runs and those teams messaged more than the four-agent ones. Talking more does not close an interface nobody owns, the mismatch is invisible to each agent whose own step is correct, and it is invisible in the finished code, which runs. So the design question is not "how many agents" but "does every seam land inside somebody's ownership."
  • The loop has an idle window in it, every turn, and the reasoning put there is worth more than the same tokens spent on the critical path. (Added 2026-08-17, T1, strong — a limb this map did not have: not how many rounds to spend, but that part of the round is spending nothing.) In a ReAct loop deliberate reasoning happens only in the Thought phase, so for the whole Action–Observation interval nothing is thinking. Fork four branches at the instant the Thought closes — Check (audit the assumptions the plan just made), Recall (resurface constraints that have receded), Rehearse (pre-compute the next step for plausible outcomes), Alternative (draft the pivot and its trigger) — kill them when the observation lands, keep what closed. Turn count falls in all nine model×benchmark pairs; main-thread decoding falls in six by up to 43%; Pass@1 is statistically unchanged in seven and significantly +12.4 and +10.2 in two. The result that makes it a finding rather than a trick is the compute-matched control this vault keeps demanding (a-coordination-arm-needs-an-always-serial-reference): forcing the identical token budget onto the main thread's own thought burns 65.6k tokens and loses 3.3 points where the parallel version holds accuracy at 20.8k — so it is not that reasoning tokens help, it is that the same tokens are worth more off the critical path (the-idle-window-is-free-reasoning-capacity). Two design constraints do the work and both generalize: branches share the main thread's prefix cache, and every branch emits self-contained ≤25-word units so an interrupt costs at most the unit in flight. The ceiling is stated honestly — letting branches finish reaches 56.7%, +4.7 over the default, by moving their whole budget back onto the critical path, so the shipped version deliberately captures 41% of the attainable gain at zero sequential cost, and harvest tracks idle time rather than turns (the 28.7% of turns that harvest hold 86.7% of it). The bill goes the other way, which belongs to model-routing-token-economics: +66% to +182% per task, all of it cached prefix reads (off-the-critical-path-is-not-off-the-bill). [T1 — training-free, three benchmarks, three models, one harness each; the compute-matched control could not be run in two of nine cells and the paper says so; wall-clock rests on a 50-instance paired replay on one model; the domain where it barely helps is the one whose failures are retrieval- and policy-shaped rather than planning-shaped, so the branch set is domain-conditioned]
  • The interior optimum is not an artifact of verifier noise: it survives with a deterministic checker, and the damage is a check the repair silently traded away. (Added 2026-08-15, T1, strong — the replication repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum most needed.) The standing objection to that result was that the whole effect lived in the learned process-reward verifier — give the loop a checker that cannot be fooled and there is nothing to fool. This is that experiment in production shape: the verifier is Checkov, a static rule engine with no model in it, thirty CIS check IDs that either fire or do not, 5,968 scenario timelines. The loop still degrades — at least one security regression in 13.8% of scenarios (3.3% under the authors' strict definition), raw pass rate peaking at iteration 4 and falling at 5, and the recommendation to stop at iteration 3 (a-repair-round-trades-one-check-for-another). So the optimum survives when the only noisy component is the repairer, and the mechanism is legible: the model is shown one failing rule, edits the resource, and takes a different rule with it. Four properties matter more than the rate. It is not transient — only 36.6% self-correct (mean 1.2 iterations later), and 28.5% of scenarios oscillate a check pass → fail → pass, which is the signature of a loop cycling between two configurations rather than converging. It concentrates where rules interact: three IAM checks alone account for 886 events, a third of all regressions. It is early, peaking at the 1→2 and 2→3 transitions — the same window the-correction-window-is-the-first-third-of-the-trajectory found for steering. And it is cheap to detect from the diff alone: regression transitions carry 2.6× the churn (140.1 vs 53.8 lines, d = 0.90) and far higher check volatility (11.55 vs 2.38, d = 1.49), so stop-on-the-sign-of-the-marginal-gain finally has a per-round trigger that does not require rerunning the checker, and it is the same signal patch-churn-predicts-quality-degradation found on agent-authored patches from an unrelated corpus. [T1 peer-reviewed (ESEM '26) — Terraform and Checkov only, Checkov an explicit proxy for security rather than security, AWS-only corpus, two models both a generation behind the frontier, one run per configuration, 13% of cells missing; a full 4× of the headline is definitional (13.8% loose vs 3.3% strict) and the apparent 17.3× model gap vanishes under the strict rule, so the honest summary is "repair rounds do break passing checks, at single-digit percentages, and everything above single digits was the ruler" — and the loop carried no memory of prior rounds' failures, which a loop that did might not oscillate]
  • The repair limb's interior optimum may be a property of self-referential verification rather than of iteration — a loop whose referent is fixed and predates the artifact ran seventeen rounds without degrading. (Added 2026-08-14, T1, single-study — a scope condition offered, not a correction made.) repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum puts true validity's peak at round 2 with decline below the no-repair baseline in six settings of eight, and this map has carried "stop on the sign of the marginal gain" as the practice that follows (stop-on-the-sign-of-the-marginal-gain).   • The iteration loop has an interior optimum, and the verifier's discrimination decides where it sits — below a threshold the loop is worth less than not looping at all. The live tension (what "contested" means here): There are now two candidate reconciliations, and as of 2026-07-11 the second one has displaced the first as the more promising. The vault should not pretend the conflict is resolved in either direction.

(A) The substrate hypothesis (2026-07-11; REFINED 2026-07-12 — the sharper one). The scaling study's degradation may not be a tax on coordination but on the substrate. First cut (icat-agent): the tax is shared context — messages carry what a peer needs and nothing else leaks. Second cut (DeLM): that can't be the whole story, because a shared substrate wins too, provided what's shared is verified at admission, compact (~100-token gists), and written directly rather than routed through a serializing orchestrator — and its ablation shows verification alone is worth 4.9 points. The refined hypothesis: the tax is raw, unvalidated state moving through a serializing router, and both event-passing and verified-gist blackboards avoid it. This retro-explains the sweep's own internal gradient (its worst performer never validates the pool; 17.2× vs 4.4× error amplification says the orchestrator's value was validation, not scheduling) and why git branch-and-merge worked so well for CAID (isolate-and-merge-parallel-agent-work): a worktree is event-passing with a filesystem for a mailbox, and the merge gate is the validation. The decisive test is cheap and unrun: re-run the sweep with a raw-shared-pool arm, an event-passing arm, and a verified-gist arm, everything else fixed. Prediction if (A) holds: the last two tie, and both beat the first.

(B) The regime hypothesis (2026-07-10, still live but weaker). Intra-task coordination — sub-agents splitting one problem (MACU, CAID) — is what the Google sweep measures. Inter-ticket fleets — one whole agent per independent ticket (Symphony, Cursor) — parallelize across units a human already decomposed, which under the scaling study's own theory is the decomposable regime where coordination should help. This is a rescue that saves the production numbers without explaining icat-agent, which is intra-task, SWE-shaped, and wins anyway. (A) explains more of the evidence with fewer moving parts.

If (A) holds, the map's advice changes concretely and for the better: coordinate freely, but never through a shared window. If (B) holds and (A) fails, fleets pay off because a human did the decomposition, not because agents coordinate well. If neither holds, the +500% and 40% numbers are throughput on work a single strong agent would have done better serially — unchecked, because the production evidence has no quality criterion behind it (evaluation-integrity).

Both were single-configuration comparisons, not paired same-model replications across seeds. Neither is refuted — the floor is local to τ²-bench/Haiku and does not transfer — but neither has been floored, and the cost of flooring them is a re-run, not a new benchmark. Until someone does, the substrate hypothesis rests on two unfloored deltas, and that is a weaker footing than the map has been treating it as. This now sits alongside the contamination confound (benchmark-scores-inflate-when-agents-can-retrieve-the-fix) as the second independent reason the SWE-bench evidence may not mean what it appears to.
  • DeLM vs icat-agent is itself an unrun comparison. Both beat single agents on SWE benchmarks, one on Verified, one on Pro, different models, different baselines — near-duals in design but never on the same field. Whoever runs the three-arm sweep gets this for free.
  • The prior decisive experiment is still unrun and still worth running: a fleet versus a strong single agent on a dependency-ordered SWE testbed (RoadmapBench, version-upgrades-defeat-frontier-agents; or SaaSBench, failures-concentrate-before-business-logic), holding model and token budget fixed. Note that SWE-bench Pro is not a substitute: it is contaminated in a way that flatters exploratory scaffolds (benchmark-scores-inflate-when-agents-can-retrieve-the-fix), which is exactly what icat-agent is.
  • Does the ~45% saturation threshold survive icat-agent at all? A scaffold beating a 59.1% baseline is a direct violation of it. Either the threshold is benchmark-specific, or it is a property of shared-context topologies only, or the SWE-bench Pro win is a retrieval artifact. These are distinguishable, and which one is true decides whether fleet architectures have the shelf life the map has been warning about since 2026-07-10.
  • Does the intra-task / inter-ticket distinction actually carve the evidence, or is it a rescue? A falsifiable version: Symphony-style ticket fleets should show the coordination penalty on tickets whose dependency graph is deep rather than wide. Nobody reports per-ticket-graph-shape outcomes.
  • The ~45% saturation threshold implies fleet value is decreasing over time as base models improve. If it holds for SWE tasks, today's fleet architectures have a shelf life, and the +500% and 40% production numbers are partly artifacts of the model generation they were measured on. Untested, and cheap to test on any two adjacent model versions.
  • The three stitch mechanisms (manager-forwarded distilled state, git branch-and-merge, deterministic daemon over ticket states) still haven't been compared head-to-head — but the error-amplification result reframes the comparison: score them on how much they verify, not on how they transport.
  • Production evidence remains output-count grade: Symphony's 500% is a landed-PR count on self-selected teams; Cursor's 40% is a share of PRs. No defect-escape, rework, or quality measure for fleet-produced code exists anywhere yet. See evaluation-integrity for why that is a load-bearing hole and not a footnote.
  • Failure mode of the reconcile step itself (bad merges, forwarded-but-stale state) is named as critical but not quantified.
