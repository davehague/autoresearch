The score belongs to the pair: what you actually measured when you measured an agent

As of 2026-08, seven independent groups have measured the same thing on seven different subjects, and it comes back large every time. The thing is the apparatus: the loop, the scaffold, the container, the wrapper on the plumbing, the inference backend, the review protocol, the calibration corpus. Not whether the benchmark was clean, not whether the labels were right, not whether the judge was honest — that is the parent map's subject, evaluation integrity, and it asks whether a number is a valid measurement of the agent. This map asks the separable question underneath it:

Does the number describe the agent, or the box you ran it in?

A benchmark can be uncontaminated, correctly labelled and honestly judged, and still report a figure that is eight to twenty-one points of somebody else's infrastructure. This map forked out of evaluation integrity on 2026-08-06 and reached the write threshold the following night, on an arrival that pushed the confound one layer further than anyone had looked: into the model weights.

Its status is consolidating, not consensus — held there for reasons stated plainly at the end, the sharpest of which is that the field's published practice has not moved at all.

Part 1 — Seven layers, one result

The layers below run from the most familiar to the least. Read them as a stack: each one is a place where two teams running "the same evaluation" can get different numbers with nothing about the agent differing.

The loop

The control surface above the harness — the thing that carries objectives, progress criteria and residual work across an extended run — has been an unnamed constant in every long-horizon figure this vault holds. LoopsBench [T1] names it and sweeps it in both directions on 112 dependency-graph tasks. Hold the model fixed at one backend and vary only the loop: resolve rate runs 18.75% down to 7.14%, a 2.6× ratio. Hold the loop fixed and vary the model across eight backends: 25.00% down to 4.46%. The two spreads are the same order.

Two details matter more than the headline. A single boolean in the runner — whether an outer continuation loop restarts the agent on residual work — is worth +3.6 to +8.0 points in every cell of both sweeps, which is larger than most published model-to-model gaps in this vault. And the loop families differ in shape, not just in strength: closed-source loops recover more of the task's prerequisite structure and organize execution as a tree while slightly over-parallelizing; open-source loops driven by linear control flow form a near-chain and emit patches 2.3–2.5× the reference length against 1.6–1.8×.

The agent scaffold

Two grids now measure this directly.

Tencent WorkBuddy Bench [T1] ran seven models × 260 tasks × three runs under two harnesses: mean absolute shift 8.6 points on one subset, rank order changing, one cell moving 13.5 points with nothing varied but the harness. Frontier models separate by roughly 2–14 points on the same benchmark — so the unreported term is the order of the reported effect. The authors say it themselves: "the harness is visibly not a neutral measurement instrument."

PolyWorkBench [T1] answered the two obvious objections to that — only two harnesses, one of them the publishing vendor's — by publishing a four-harness matrix unprompted, none of them the authors'. Every model run under two or more harnesses spreads ≥0.08 Pass@1; one model spans 0.209 on a single swap; the authors' own stated range is 8–21 points.

And the structural half is the one that kills the obvious remedy: the ordering of harnesses is not model-invariant. Harness A beats harness B for one model and loses for another. There is no correction table to publish, and a score measured on someone else's scaffold is not adjustable into a score on yours.

The plumbing between the runner and the provider

Adversarial Test-Hardening for AI-Written Code [T1] is the receipted incident, and it is the one to tell people about, because the proxy was fine. Every verdict was a rule-injected mutant and a failing test run — no model ever scored another model's output. The bias entered one level out, through an undetected 16,000-token output cap on one provider's wrapper that truncated replies and logged them under an unrelated-sounding rejection note. The cap applied to every call on that wrapper but only bit on verbose replies, and verbosity is a model property. So the failure correlated with the arm under test and produced a pooled p = 9.5 × 10⁻⁶⁶. Corrected: p = 0.0625, not supported.

The generalized hazard is any dimension on which the apparatus treats two models differently and which correlates with the outcome — a timeout, a token budget, a retry policy, a parser that tolerates one model's formatting quirks.

The inference backend

What We Observe as LLM Behavior Can Be a Side-effect of Inference Backend [T1] goes below the agent entirely, to the software that renders the chat template and calls the kernel. Fully crossed: 3 models × 5 serving frameworks × 6 benchmarks × 4 generation modes, with weights, prompts, precision, hardware and evaluation protocol held constant.

The design earns its zero before it claims anything — a wrapper around the same in-process model object as the reference diverges 0.000 on all 14 metrics. Then, still under greedy decoding, where no sampling noise exists at all: LangChain diverges 0.049 on identical weights and engine, from prompt-template rendering alone; vLLM diverges 0.025 on one model of three, significant on 10 of 11 metrics, from nothing but a different attention kernel under non-associative floating-point arithmetic. Per-item disagreement reaches 48%, so a score that is stable in the mean can be composed of different correct answers.

The remedy splits the term usefully: roughly 39% is structural and 61% is avoidable by decoding greedily and stating the generation configuration. That is three extra fields on a harness card, not a research programme. Bounded hard, though — ~1B models on English multiple-choice and short-form generation, with code, mathematical reasoning and agentic tasks named by the authors as uncovered.

The review protocol

PRWeaver [T1] supplies the first instance on a security detection rate — the quantity teams are currently using to decide whether a reviewer agent may gate a merge. With one backbone model held fixed across three deployed auditing frameworks, detection spans 55 / 65 / 56%: ten points from the surrounding workflow alone.

The larger term is a configuration decision nobody prints. Reviewing pull requests one at a time versus handing the auditor the whole release window moves detection 34–38 points on identical content — 50/60/54% against 16/22/22% — with auditor, model and content all constant. A resolve-rate confound wastes engineering budget. A detection-rate confound means a gate that misses four-fifths of adversarial changes was bought on a number measured at 50–60%.

The monitor's calibration deployment

Real-Time Detection and Repair of LLM Agent Failures [T1] extends the argument to the instrument you would buy to watch the agent. A trajectory monitor calibrated on one model family and deployed on another sits at chance (AUROC 0.527, healthy false-alarm rate 0.75) against 0.885 recalibrated on itself. Imported to another group's corpus, something more interesting happens: the ranking transfers zero-shot and the operating point does not — AUROC 0.745, detection 0.048 at a 5% false-alarm budget.

The mechanism is disclosed rather than asserted. The detector's advantage over a cheaper baseline is a monotone function of how many steps a failure has to develop after onset (+0.09 at ≤3 steps, +0.14 at 4–8, +0.40 at ≥9), and the importing corpus has ≥9 post-onset steps on only 6.9% of its failures. So the failure-shape distribution of the calibration corpus is a hidden term in every published detection rate, and nobody discloses it.

The weights

DCAS [T1] is the newest arrival and the one that breaks the pattern, because it is the only layer here that cannot be disclosed away.

The open coding-agent ecosystem collects its fine-tuning trajectories almost entirely under one scaffold. Models trained on that data carry its conventions wherever they go: one reads 49.7 / 46.0 / 35.8 / 20.4 across four scaffolds, a 29-point collapse; another reads 52.6 / 44.2 / 8.4 / 37.6, where the 8.4 is a tool-call format mismatch leaving most trajectories with zero parseable actions.

The control is what makes this causal rather than observational: the untrained base models those were fine-tuned from spread less across the same four scaffolds than any model fine-tuned from them. The divergence is installed by training. Mixing scaffolds in training is the obvious fix and has been tried — a model trained on diverse multi-format trajectories still falls from 50.5 to 26.2 and 15.6.

The authors state this map's thesis in their own words: "single-scaffold evaluation is a methodological confound," and a claim of the form "model X achieves Y% on SWE-bench Verified" is "methodologically incomplete without specifying the scaffold."

Part 2 — What to do, reading and building

Reading a published number

Treat a single-harness leaderboard gap under about ten points as unresolved. Two independent grids put the harness term at 8.6 mean absolute and 8–21 points respectively, against frontier separations of 2–14 on the same benchmarks.

There is no correction table, at any layer. Both scaffold grids find the harness term to be an interaction rather than an offset — the ordering changes by model. So does the backend term: one model family is near-immune while another is severe in 14 of 16 configurations. You cannot learn a per-harness adjustment once and subtract it.

For an open model, ask what it was trained under before you ask what it scores. The expected drop under a non-training scaffold is not noise; it can be half the score, or a parser failure that zeroes it.

Ask which layer the score was measured at, and assume the ones below it were not held. The two grids that vary the agent scaffold say nothing about the serving stack. The one study that varies the serving stack has no agent in it at all. Nobody has run the crossed experiment, so it is unknown whether the terms add, interact, or whether one dominates.

Building a system

A candidate model evaluated on someone else's harness has not been evaluated for your system. The cheapest pre-deployment test is a run under your own scaffold, and it is not optional.

The review unit is a security parameter. Per-PR review retained 50–60% where whole-window review retained 16–22% on identical adversarial content, and hiding repository history cost only 4–11 points. So more context is not monotone: the narrower window is the safer default, with history as a tool the reviewer may call rather than material you pre-load.

A monitor evaluated on someone else's deployment has not been evaluated for yours, and the specific thing you cannot import is the threshold. Ranking may transfer. The operating point will not.

Where a deterministic invariant exists, use it — the confound question does not arise. In the same study whose monitor sits at chance across model families, three recomputation checks transfer across families with nothing retuned, at 110 of 110 detections and 0 of 10 false positives. Nothing to calibrate, nothing to disclose, nothing to confound.

Publishing a number

Ship a harness card. As of tonight the fields with measured justification are: the loop implementation and its continuation policy; the agent scaffold, name and version; the container specification; the inference backend, its version and its generation configuration; and — for anything that gates — the review unit. For an open model, add the collection scaffold of its training trajectories, and report at least one arm outside it.

Part 3 — What is not settled

The remedy is demonstrably incomplete, and now provably so at one layer. The disclosure argument has proposed a five-field schema, a container specification, a backend and generation configuration. Nothing establishes that any of those lists is sufficient — the plumbing incident was a wrapper-level output cap that no proposed schema would have caught. And DCAS's layer is worse than incomplete: a scaffold baked into fine-tuning weights is not a reporting failure, so no disclosure practice repairs it. That is the first evidence here that this map's own prescription does not reach the whole problem, and it is one study.

The crossed experiment has never been run. Same benchmark, two scaffolds × two backends. Since both terms are model-dependent interactions rather than offsets, an interaction is the likelier prior — and would mean neither term can be honestly reported alone.

The backend result has never been measured on an agentic task. Its own authors exclude code, multi-turn dialogue and agentic workflows, which is exactly where output length, stopping behaviour and tool-call formatting interact with the serving stack most. A long agent trajectory contains hundreds of generations, each a chance for a template difference to compound. The ~1B single-turn magnitudes are a floor with no evidence about the ceiling.

Nobody has tested whether partial adjustment recovers anything. There is no correction table, but whether a rank-preserving within-harness comparison is usable — or whether cross-harness comparison is simply void — is unmeasured.

And the honest limit on this whole map. The two largest scaffold grids are two and four harnesses respectively, on one benchmark each. The backend study is at ~1B scale on non-agentic tasks. The weights study is four models on one benchmark at single runs per cell. Several of the strongest entries here — the plumbing incident, the loop grid, the weights result, the monitor result — are single studies, two of them by single independent authors. What makes the map consolidating rather than emerging is that seven groups working on seven different layers, with no shared method, all found the apparatus term to be of the order of the effect being reported. What keeps it below consensus is that not one of those results has been replicated by an independent group re-running another's harness on its own benchmark, which is the measurement that would settle it.

Meanwhile this vault is still ingesting single-harness leaderboard comparisons weekly.
