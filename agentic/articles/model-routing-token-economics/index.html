Model routing and token economics: budget the next increment of effort

As of 2026-07, three independent T1 results have reached the same structure from three different objects — one routing models across a session, one routing iterations within a task, one routing the kind of the next attempt after a failure. None replicates another's numbers. What they replicate is the shape, and the shape is the finding:

Allocate agent spend sequentially, under a global budget, from a policy learned on cheap observable state. Not greedily. Not on a fixed schedule.

The corollary is the part most teams can act on this week: the unit of routing is not the query, and not even the model — it is the next increment of effort. The decision "should this agent take another step, and what kind of step" is a routing decision, it is the one nearly every harness makes badly, and until recently nobody was measuring it.

Restructured 2026-07-31. This article was written when the thesis was two papers and a hope. Since 2026-07-22 it shipped as a production product, gained a third independent academic arrival, and grew three claims the old outline had nowhere to put — so this is a rebuild, not an append. What changed: (1) the headline deployed at a scale three orders of magnitude past any paper here, and agreed with its sharpest part; (2) the router itself turned out to be a trained artifact, not a prompt, with frontier models failing at the job; (3) the increment has a shape, not just a size, which breaks the cascade abstraction most cost-aware tooling is built on; (4) the increments that justify a premium are the ones whose output is reused, now priced; (5) the ladder has a rung below "cheap model" — no model at all; and (6) routing has a second currency, trust, which no operational metric collects.

The claim shipped, and it brought a field baseline

The map's founding claim — that a router is a trained artifact fitted to your own traffic — is now a deployed product. Cursor's router is a classifier over query, context, task complexity and domain, trained on 600k+ live requests from the deployment it serves and validated in an online A/B test across millions of requests, reporting 60% cost savings at "frontier-quality performance" (Cursor Router, [T2], suggestive).

Three things it adds that no paper here has.

  • A field baseline. ~60% of developers pin one model as their default — the first field measurement of how common the fixed policy every result in this article argues against actually is. The baseline being beaten is a behaviour, not an algorithm.
  • A unit. Dollars per commit (Balance $4.63, Intelligence $6.76, Opus 4.8 $7.34, Fable 5 $12.69) — the first denominator that folds retry cost, abandoned trajectories and cache behaviour into the price a buyer actually pays.
  • Two quality criteria that are not benchmarks. User satisfaction classified from user responses, and keep rate — how much agent-written code survives in the codebase over time.

Two design details are worth copying regardless of what you think of the numbers. The router is cache-aware in training and evaluation, without which it would be optimizing a per-token price that does not describe its own workload. And the cost-quality dial ships as three named modes rather than a continuous λ, which is a product decision and also a measurable one.

Discount it properly, because the caveats are structural rather than incidental: this is a vendor post for a paid product, with no confidence intervals, no task distribution, no public definition of keep rate's window or denominator, an unvalidated LLM judging user satisfaction for its own product, and quality parity asserted alongside the price comparison rather than measured. Two different savings populations (60% from the A/B, 30–50% from early-access customers) are reported as one story. Take the architecture as confirmed and every magnitude as a vendor's.

The routing policy is a trained artifact, not a prompt

This is the claim most likely to save you a wasted sprint, and it is the one two organizations reached independently at three orders of magnitude apart in data.

Handed the same problem statement, execution verdict, stderr trace and action descriptions zero-shot, frontier models route no better than the dumbest useful fixed policy and cost several times more: Claude Sonnet 4.6 45.3% at 6.67 m$, Gemini 3.1 Pro 36.7%, GPT-5.4-nano 33.1%, GPT-5.4 32.8% — the best merely matching always-replan's 45.3% at 1.59 m$, two of them scoring below it. A Qwen3.5-4B fine-tuned on the deployment's own execution rollouts reaches 81.7% (CodeRescue, [T1], suggestive).

The diagnosis is the transferable part: the routing signal is not semantic. Nothing in an action description tells you that this stderr shape at this difficulty is one a cheap repair solves. That mapping exists only in rollouts you have to pay to collect, and it is specific to your model pair, action set and task distribution. The ablation reinforces it — a coarse metadata prefix (source, difficulty, algorithm tag) bought more (0.656 → 0.697) than upgrading the backbone did.

The trade: a learned router is a recurring bill, and the policy is not portable across agents. Each agent gets its own retrained policy at a real one-time cost — $48.40 / 13 h for one agent, $127 / 22.7 h for another — so budget it as a per-agent fixed cost, not a one-off [T1, single-study]. What softens this is CodeRescue's deployment shape: budget control is a Conformal Risk Control layer over a stored breakpoint table, so a new budget is a re-scan rather than a retraining run.

Read against the shipped router, the agreement is the point: a 4B model trained on 360 examples and a production classifier trained on 600k live requests reach the same conclusion about where the signal lives. Hold the CodeRescue magnitudes loosely — one 360-example test split, and the zero-shot arm got no few-shot or prompt-engineering attempt, which is a real limit on how hard "prompting fails" was tested.

Route the session, not the query

Route each query in isolation and you get a per-query classifier that has no idea a session is coming. It spends freely on the early, easy turns and arrives at the hard later ones with nothing left. Modeled instead as a budget-constrained finite-horizon decision — an offline-RL policy over 10k sessions and 2.38M transitions — the router learns something a per-query classifier structurally cannot: delayed gratification. Measured cost savings of 6.0–73.5%, bankruptcy under 1%, quality held on the Pareto frontier (SeqRoute, [T1]).

The trade: you need session-level state to route on, and you need to have decided what your budget is. A per-query router is stateless and needs neither. That simplicity is exactly what it is paying for.

Ship the cost/quality setting as a deploy-time dial, not a compiled-in operating point [T1]. A CI bot, an interactive session and a nightly batch want the same policy set to three different places on the frontier, and one trained policy can navigate it without retraining.

Two refinements on when to spend the routing effort:

  • The waste is not where you would guess. Measured by a compute-to-requirement ratio, relative over-spend peaks on the easiest tasks — 22.1 on simple, 11.0 on cross-file, 5.4 on repo-level — because the absolute cost of "gather everything" is roughly flat while the oracle floor grows with difficulty (execution-scope estimation, [T1], single-study). That argues for estimating scope before gathering context. Two honest qualifications the headline hides: the −85% cost / −91% tokens figure comes from a deterministic simulator with no LLM in the loop, the live arm is far milder (−18% against read-everything, −4% against plain ReAct), and the method's edge reverses on the hardest tier. This is a cheap-task optimization, not a general one.
  • A cheap dry run beats accumulated history for task-conditioned choice. A deterministic simulation costing 1.3% of the tokens of real execution improves Top-1 model selection from 38.6% to 61.5% overall (coding 28.6% → 50.0%) against a baseline that ranks models by their real scores on every other task. The advantage closes by Top-3, where global model strength catches up — so the value is task-conditioned early filtering, not ranking (OrchBench, [T1], suggestive).

The increment has a shape, and the shapes are not ordered by price

This is the structural finding that arrived since the last revision, and it invalidates a mental model rather than extending one.

Cost-aware inference has been built almost entirely as a cascade: try the cheap model, defer the hard cases up. That treats a cheap attempt's failure as evidence that more capability is needed. In an executable environment the failure is also diagnostic, and when you measure which recovery action actually works, the actions turn out not to be a capability ladder. On the pooled holdout, 28% of recoverable failures are cheap-only, 45% escalation-only, 27% both — with the mix moving by domain (CodeRescue, [T1], suggestive).

So the cascade abstraction is right about the 45% and wrong about the 28%, and pays full escalation price for both. Quantified: fixed policies solve 27.5% / 45.3% / 68.6% at 1.24 / 1.59 / 7.22 m$, while the learned router reaches 81.7% at 5.51 m$, dominating always-escalate on both axes. The budget-calibrated frontier hits 71.7% at 2.56 m$ — above always-escalate's 68.6% at 35% of its cost, and above a binary cascade at comparable cost (63.6%).

Two details from the same result that constrain what you can promise:

  • The risk guarantee is deliberately on expected cost, not solve rate, because non-monotone actions make the cost-quality curve neither concave nor monotone. A router over non-ordered actions cannot offer a quality floor the way a cascade appears to.
  • ~30% of attempted problems are recovered by no action in the set. Routing improves the reachable population; it does not enlarge it. That number bounds every claim in this article.

A second, independent instance of the same "shape not size" point arrives from the opposite direction — a case where the expensive option is also the losing one. At 1.5B and 3B, a blind resample of the original prompt beats every feedback-bearing retry condition on pass@1 and costs 2.5–5.5× fewer tokens (49k–76k against 122k–345k at k = 8); at 7B it ties at the same discount (blind resampling, [T1], strong at ≤7B). A reflect-style repair loop at these scales pays a premium for a negative effect, and the cheapest post-failure action — discard and re-sample — is also the best one measured. The economics should not be assumed to transfer upward: the anchoring penalty that produces the result scales with baseline error at r = 0.96, so it ought to shrink on a strong model. One benchmark family, single author, 1.5–7B open weights.

The same non-ladder logic reaches diagnosis, too. An explicit cost ladder over debugging strategies (heuristics → one whole-trace pass → binary search → per-step → budgeted ensembles) is worth climbing only as far as needed: the expensive multi-call method costs 12.8K tokens against 8.1K (1.6×) and, on hosted backbones, a single global reading is already stronger with adjudication adding nothing (AgentDebugX, [T1]; general agentic traces, not coding).

The trade: learning the shape requires labeled rollouts of each action on your failures, which is strictly more data collection than a cascade needs. A cascade needs only a success signal; a non-monotone router needs to know what would have happened under the road not taken.

Rank the increments by reuse: route by role, not by turn

If the unit of routing is the next increment of effort, the obvious follow-up is which increments justify a premium. Two independent organizations answered it in six days, and the second one priced it.

The allocation inverts instinct: small models implement in parallel — one case runs 12 concurrent implementer agents on Sonnet — while the largest models are reserved for reviewing and for writing the rulebook (Anthropic code migrations, [T2], suggestive). The principle behind it is reuse: a rule amended once changes an entire batch, a review catches a class rather than an instance, an implementation is consumed once. Rank roles by how much their output governs other work, and the allocation falls out.

The price, on one fixed task under four model mixes, all reaching 100% of the suite: a frontier model in both planner and worker roles costs $10,565 (workers alone $9,373) against a frontier planner with a cheap worker at $1,339 (workers alone $411) — a ~7.9× spread at equal terminal score (Cursor agent swarms, [T2], suggestive). The structure explains it: workers carry 69–90%+ of the token volume and a minority of the cost, planners the reverse. The cost curve is dominated by one role and the quality curve by another.

The first published unit cost for an agentic migration lands here too: 1M LOC Zig→Rust for 5.9B input / 690M output tokens ≈ $165k, with the resulting binary 19% smaller and 2–5% faster.

Discount all three figures the same way. A fan-out over a shared plan or rulebook is a high-prefix-reuse workload, so the effective rate sits well below list and the absolute numbers are an upper bound that does not port to low-reuse work. "Equal terminal quality" is a vendor-chosen test suite with no manual reading of the output, and the line-count gap between configurations suggests the outputs were not equivalent where the scores were. And the $165k excludes the substantial human effort the practice explicitly front-loads into writing and stress-testing the rulebook — the un-priced input the whole result rests on.

A sharper version of the same allocation shows up when the roles are asymmetric in kind rather than in seniority. Once a main agent carries the task on program state rather than screenshots, swapping the frontier GUI subagent for a compact 31B specialist barely moves four of five benchmarks — even though that model scores 7.6 against 54.8 standalone (StateAct, [T1], suggestive). The premium model holds the task; the cheap one serves a peripheral modality.

The trade: role allocation needs your loop to have roles. A single-agent loop offers no seam to route on, so this lever is only available to architectures that already paid the coordination cost.

Stopping is a correctness control — and the right effort is agent-conditional in sign

The second original arrival at this article's thesis came from supervising an agent writing tests. A contextual bandit chooses, at each iteration, one of three actions: generate, analyze-then-generate, or stop, reading seven cheap features under a reward with an explicit cost penalty. Against a forced 8-iteration budget on 80 held-out Java classes it delivers 82.8% line / 74.7% branch / 64.7% mutation coverage at $0.693, versus 77.3 / 70.2 / 59.6 at $0.797 (SCATE, [T1]). More quality, less money, from deciding when to keep going.

Past the optimum, spend and quality move together

A second, independent arrival makes a harder claim than "stopping saves money." In a verify-repair loop where both the verifier and the repairer are noisy, additional rounds do not merely cost more — they destroy correct work. True validity peaks at round 2 and then collapses; across eight settings six decline monotonically, with per-round damage probabilities of 0.615–0.938. The raw labels are the part to sit with: 55% of correct plans get repaired into incorrect ones, and 24% of those damaging repairs win majority acceptance (VRR-Stop, [T1], suggestive). Reported acceptance keeps climbing while true validity falls, which is why a budget tuned on "the verifier looks happier now" runs past the optimum by construction.

Stopping on the estimated sign of the marginal gain rather than on acceptance votes reaches 0.722 true validity at 0.72 average repair rounds, against 0.116 at five fixed rounds — seven times cheaper and sixty points better. It slightly beats a reference policy handed the ground-truth parameters, which is the tell that fixed round budgets are a category error rather than a tuning error.

So past the loop's interior optimum there is no cost/quality trade-off, because every additional round is buying damage. The stopping rule is a correctness control that happens to be cheaper, and it sits in this article only because the same machinery implements both.

Two engineering details transfer even where the magnitudes do not:

  • The decision needs only sign identifiability, not accurate recovery of the loop's noise parameters, so it calibrates from a few hundred cross-fitted samples rather than a training run. [T1]
  • Know when your own instrument is unidentifiable. When verifier discrimination approaches zero, estimation error flips the stopping sign, and the correct policy is not a better estimator but an estimation-free retreat toward not repairing at all. [T1]

Hold the size loosely: 3–8B open-weight models, a deliberately adversarial setting, a math-heavy headline with code generation only in the robustness table.

The sign is a property of the agent, not the task

Point the same allocator at two different agents and it learns opposite policies. Against a strong-zero-shot agent (Claude Code — already at 84.3% line coverage on iteration 1) it stops early on 95.0% of tasks, averaging 2.1 iterations, and reaches for its analysis tool on 10.0%. Against a weaker, cheaper one (Gemini-CLI) it runs 7.9 iterations and uses analysis three times as often (33.8%). Same framework, same features, same reward [T1].

So the fixed iteration cap that ships as the default in essentially every agent harness is wrong in both directions at once. Two results since have widened this from a magnitude claim into a sign claim.

  • A review pass can be a strict loss. Adding a second pass costs $0.12–$0.25 and 29–74 seconds per task. On a weaker writer both reviewers pay for themselves (+12.9 pp for the cheap same-model option at $0.312/task, +18.1 pp for the stronger cross-model one at $0.443). On the stronger writer every reviewed condition is a strict loss — $0.16 and 50s for zero gain, or $0.16 and 32s for an 8.6-point accuracy loss (cross-model review, [T1], suggestive). A fixed "always add a review pass" policy is the same category error as a fixed iteration cap.
  • The marginal call can be net-negative even when it is rare. In one controlled planning domain, once declarative structure is in place the model fires on 4.3% of turns behind a gate with four runtime-computable preconditions, for +0.005 F1 and the lowest win rate in its family — the revisions sharpen local targeting while consuming a finite budget (harness heavy lifting, [T1], suggestive). Bounds that matter: n=54, a single planning domain rather than software engineering, and no dollar accounting — invocation rate is the cost proxy here, not a price.

The provider-exposed reasoning-effort dial behaves the same way. Raising it is worth +3.0 points on one model, 0.0 on another and −2.7 on a third, and the mechanism the authors give is the one to carry: most lost trials are lost to a mis-applied or ignored rule, and additional sampling around the wrong conclusion does not repair that (HANDBOOK.md, [T1], suggestive).

The trade: every one of these is an argument for measuring your own agent before setting a budget, which is a measurement bill the fixed cap does not charge.

The cheap tier is something you build — and its bottom rung is no model at all

"Route to a cheaper model" remains the least interesting lever in this ledger. Two families of result say you can move the price of a unit of quality without changing the model.

Build the cheap tier.

  • Adapt the small model's harness up to the bar. A harness optimizer mining failure trajectories let small models recover up to 89.7% of a frontier LLM's performance at 4% of the cost (better harnesses, smaller models, [T1], suggestive). Before routing away from the cheap model, consider adapting its harness up. The optimizer's one-time search compute is the cost to weigh against recurring savings, and 5 of 21 pairs did not improve.
  • Cut the tool surface. Restricting a coding agent to a single execute_code channel held pass rate and was cheaper in 3 of 4 regime×agent cells (execute_code ablation, [T1], suggestive). Cache-adjusted cost, not pass rate, decides the cheapest surface.
  • Design the retrieval. A structural codebase index (embeddings + a graph of definitions and call edges + BM25) landed at $2.30 per solved task versus $2.92 for an agentic-grep baseline while resolving more (structural index, [T1], suggestive). A competing measurement warns the lever has two handles pointing opposite ways: retrieval strategies differing 1.63× and 1.32× in cost had the cheaper option also the slower one, so a harness tuned on latency chooses differently from one tuned on tokens (Code Finder, [T2], single-study).
  • Design the cache. In one production deployment a 99.3% prompt-cache hit rate made the frontier API the cheap tier — $0.573/M effective, below locally-served quantized open weights at $2.83/M (inference economics, [T3], n=1 self-study). Published per-token rates are the wrong number for high-reuse agentic work.
  • Staff the stage, not the task. A production system runs each loop stage on the lightest model that performs it reliably: four post-trained specialists at 0.5–4B, served at 0.02–0.1× frontier per-call cost, which is what makes "verify every step" an affordable default. Its 0.5B step-type classifier is credited with ~4 pp of end-to-end accuracy at net-negative cost (reliability decomposition, [T3], vendor self-report, proprietary weights, latency overhead unmeasured).

Or keep the model and make it cheaper. This is a distinct family: same model, same task, lower bill.

  • Synchronize trajectory state with the repository instead of storing snapshots — cost down 8.3–50.3% across four models at comparable pass@1 ($5.27 → $2.62 per instance on the strongest), because it removes duplicate file copies and the reasoning cycles spent recovering from stale ones (CORVUS, [T1], strong).
  • Ground the agent in program state rather than its rendering — ~9× ($72 → $7.8 per task) while raising accuracy on the same backbone (StateAct, [T1]).
  • Rewrite the history deterministically. A one-time compaction with no model in the path cuts trajectory tokens 50–87% across five models at preserved localization (AnswerRecall@5 within ε = 0.05, 95% confidence lower bound) (CodeNib, [T1], strong for the token result). The boundary to keep attached: nothing in that study measures repair, so it is a priced efficiency result, not a capability one.

One disclosed tension runs through this group and nobody has measured it: synced or rewritten context changes every cycle, which limits prompt-cache reuse on that block — and caching is what made the frontier API the cheap tier in the first place. These levers interact, and the interaction is unpriced.

And the rung below all of them is no model call at all. Three results now point at it.

  • A deterministic prefilter can dispose of most of the traffic. A 530-pattern filter costing ~139 ms removes 67.2% of a corpus before any inference happens, and the escalated remainder is sent as bounded windows around the matches rather than whole documents: 76.9% fewer input tokens, 4.3× fewer per classification, ~818 ms weighted against ~6,281 ms for full-file model screening, at F1 0.817 versus 0.287 (SkillGate, [T1], suggestive). The rule that keeps it honest is the division of labour: the prefilter escalates and never convicts, so the cheap layer removes work and the expensive layer keeps authority — the opposite of the usual cost-cutting move where a cheap component decides cases it is unqualified to decide. Two imported costs: the escalation rate is a dial trading money against misses, and a fixed pattern vocabulary silently sets the gate's false-negative profile. One workload, one judge backend, a hand-crafted corpus, and nondeterministic judge behaviour (7–20 false positives across three runs) — the architecture transfers, the 67.2% skip rate does not.
  • Structure can beat the model it replaces. In one controlled domain, non-reasoning LM-only captains calling the model on every turn land below a no-LLM baseline regardless of size: Llama-4-Scout at 109B MoE reaches 0.353 F1 with zero wins against belief-only structure's 0.522 F1 / 50.0% win rate, and a ~4B model lands below uniform random. A mid-reasoning model reaches the declarative planner's win-rate neighbourhood (77.8% vs 74.1%, overlapping CIs) at 100% LLM invocation against 0% — the same outcome bought at radically different cost (harness heavy lifting, [T1], suggestive). The authors' own conclusion is a routing statement: model size is not the missing ingredient, inference-time reasoning is — so parameter count is the wrong axis to route on. Read the bounds hard: n=54, a single MCMC-friendly planning domain the authors concede favours declarative planning, and no dollar accounting.
  • Delete the prompt. Over 80% of a production harness's system prompt was removed with no reported eval loss (context engineering for Claude 5, [T2], single-study). Discount it carefully — that prompt was the cached prefix, and at 99.3% hit rates cached input is where the cheap tokens already were, so the saving is smaller than 80% suggests and the post prices nothing. The durable point is about deliberation rather than tokens: a contradictory instruction is paid for in output reasoning, which is the expensive side of the ledger.

There is a lever in this family that is really a warning: scale. On book-length retrieval tasks, as the corpus grows the cost–accuracy frontier recedes down and to the right — accuracy falls and the per-question cost of buying any fixed accuracy rises, so a larger corpus is strictly less efficient to read, not merely harder (progressive disclosure, [T1], suggestive).

The bill is not made of what the price list says

Every routing policy above is calibrated against a per-task cost. Five results say that number is not the one on the invoice.

The dispersion is real and undisclosed. Among models of comparable quality, operational cost can differ by up to three orders of magnitude on production-like serial workloads (RAMP, [T1]). And a pilot audit of twelve benchmark papers found 0 of 8 agent benchmarks disclose inference cost (disclosure audit, [T1], single-study). Anyone routing on published quality numbers is routing on one axis of a two-axis decision, with the missing axis known to span 1000×.

The price belongs to the harness, not the model on the invoice. Two review agents running the same backend land within 0.2 points of each other on overall performance and differ roughly 7× in cost ($0.10 vs $0.75 on validation alone), with a ~100× spread across four systems ($0.007 to $0.75+). And the money goes somewhere actively unhelpful: validation effort is characteristic of the reviews that fail (terminal review trajectories, [T1], suggestive). This is stronger than diminishing returns — it is spend on a phase that predicts the wrong outcome.

It belongs to the framework, too. Real wall-clock is nearly uncorrelated across execution frameworks (r = −0.104), and the time-versus-token relationship is inconsistent within them — which is why OrchBench's own simulated cost predictions failed to transfer (r = −0.264 and −0.607) and were demoted to diagnostics [T1]. A cost comparison that does not name the harness is measuring the harness.

And it drifts upward on its own. Across 35 sequential releases of one scaffolding with the model frozen, average token consumption rose 391K → 668K (+70%) with a strong monotonic trend (ρ=0.743, p<0.0001) while resolve rate showed no improving trend (23.0–39.0%, ρ=0.208, p=0.231) (scaffolding evolution, [T1], strong; n=1 scaffolding, 50-task slice, single run per release). Operational rule: pin the scaffolding version like you pin the model, and re-measure cost after every harness upgrade, not only after every model change.

What the bill is actually made of is the finding a per-token or per-task budget cannot see. Normalizing per outcome — dollars per mutant killed, $0.0244 for one configuration against $0.0038 for the other, 6.4× cheaper per outcome at a similar rate card — the gap decomposes arithmetically from receipts: the expensive configuration emitted 678,564 output tokens against 100,545 (6.7×), and output volume alone accounts for $10.18 of its $11.14. Rejected rounds (10 against 3) supply the rest (critic-loop autopsy, [T1], single-study). Neither the published per-token price nor the run count explains the bill. Output verbosity and failed rounds do — and Cursor's dollars-per-commit is the first published denominator that folds them in.

One boundary on what a spend decision can buy: on the instances two model generations both resolve, the later model's patches show zero median difference on every static quality measure and slightly higher CPU and peak memory (+0.048 s, +4.524 MiB; ratios ≤1.02) (beyond resolved rate, [T1], strong). Upgrading the model is a coverage lever — it buys resolve rate on tasks you could not solve — and not a quality lever on the tasks you could. The quality term in any cost-quality trade needs its own measurement rather than being inferred from the capability tier. The same caution applies to published efficiency numbers: replaying the official reference patches of three performance benchmarks across cloud machine types found them valid on only 38% / 8% / 82% of replays (benchmark reliability, [T1]). A published speedup is a claim about a machine.

The costs that move instead of disappearing

Two results measure where saved money goes rather than whether it was saved. They are the ones most likely to change a routing decision.

Into repair labor. In a year-long production deployment the cheap configuration relocated cost rather than removing it: fix-commit ratio rose 45.9% → 74.9%, net-new share fell 54.1% → 25.1%, Mantel-Haenszel OR 3.61 holding inside every difficulty tier. Priced with symmetric labor assumptions the cheap arm still won TCO by 40.1% under shared GPU allocation — and lost by 43.8% under dedicated reservation — so the lesson is not "cheap models don't pay," it is that an inference-only ledger does not close. Repricing the pooled commits under four routing policies yields a monotonic frontier with no dominating policy: across 10,000 bootstrap resamples, no policy sending any tier to the local model matched the pure-API defect profile (inference economics, [T3], single-study). Hybrid routing buys savings with defects, not instead of them. Cite the structure and the frontier's shape, never the magnitudes: n=1 developer, non-randomized, a self-study, regex-classified outcomes rated by someone not blind to the hypothesis, list-price parameters the authors note a ~45% negotiated discount would erase.

Into trust. This is new to the ledger, and it is the first entry priced in something other than dollars. Because a third-party API router terminates the client connection and reconnects to the provider, it holds plaintext system prompts, repository context, tool specifications and tool-call payloads, and can rewrite the response before the agent acts on it — and no agent verifies that what it consumed is what the provider produced. Downstream, every client-side control measured reads 0% (API router trusted path, [T1], suggestive).

The distinction that decides a deployment is first-party versus third-party mediation, not routing versus not routing. A vendor routing inside its own trust boundary does not create this gap; a multi-provider aggregator — precisely the thing that makes the cheap-tier allocation this article keeps recommending convenient — does. So the cost-optimal and integrity-optimal architectures point in opposite directions, and the quantity that would settle it is one nobody publishes: the aggregator's price advantage over a first-party or self-hosted path, set against the population base rate of tampering. A cited survey of 428 commodity routers supplies a numerator (9 injecting malicious tool calls, 2 using adaptive evasion, 18 abusing in-transit credentials) and no denominator. Unlike repair labor, an integrity liability has no smooth expected value and appears in no operational metric the deployment collects. Bounds: the security study is a controlled instrument (400 hand-built injections, three tasks) rather than a prevalence estimate, and the economic half of the trade-off is entirely unmeasured.

And budget the comparison, not just the deployment. Measured per-cell variance implies existence claims are cheap (k ≤ 5 runs) while ordering two configurations Δ ≈ 0.18 apart at σ ≈ 0.3 needs k ≈ 46 runs per condition (repair reliability variance, [T3], single-study). Nobody pays that, which is a concrete mechanism behind a literature of single-run deltas that flip sign 33–44% of the time. Applied here: if you cannot afford k for your own workload, claim existence rather than ordering — "this policy beats always-escalate" is affordable; "policy A beats policy B by 4%" usually is not. The σ that sets k = 46 comes from computer-use RL and is not yours; the transferable move is computing k from your own measured σ.

What to do

  • Set a session budget, and route against it. Not a per-call cap. [T1]
  • Train the router; do not prompt it. Frontier models handed the routing decision zero-shot performed at or below a trivial fixed policy at several times the cost. Collect rollouts from the deployment you intend to route. [T1, T2]
  • Make "stop" an action your harness can take, and learn when to take it. A fixed retry/iteration cap is a policy — just an unexamined one. Past the loop's optimum it is a correctness bug, not an overspend. [T1 ×2]
  • Stop assuming your recovery actions form a ladder. Measure which failures your cheap action solves that escalation does not. In the one study that looked, that was 28% of recoverable failures. [T1]
  • Rank roles by reuse, and spend there. Premium capacity belongs on the plan, the rulebook and the review — the artifacts consumed by many downstream units — not on each unit. [T2 ×2]
  • Measure your verifier's discrimination before you fund the loop. If you cannot measure it, stop early; acceptance rates will not warn you. [T1]
  • Estimate scope before gathering context. The proportional waste concentrates on the easy tasks. [T1]
  • Build the cheap tier before you route to it — harness adaptation, tool-surface reduction, retrieval structure, cache design, stage staffing, state grounding, deterministic compaction. [T1 ×5, T2, T3 ×2]
  • Look for the rung below the cheap model. Deterministic prefilters, declarative structure and model-free compaction each removed most of a workload's inference. Keep the division of labour honest: the cheap layer removes work; the expensive layer keeps authority. [T1 ×3]
  • Price the cache before you price the model, and re-measure after every harness upgrade, not only every model upgrade. Scaffolding drift moved per-task tokens 70% with the model frozen. [T3, T1]
  • Retune the budget policy per agent. Budget the retraining as a recurring per-agent cost (~$48–127 and 13–23 hours in the published accounts), not a one-off — and expect it to go stale on every model upgrade, the way the rest of the harness does (harness staleness, [T2]). [T1]
  • Expose cost/quality as a runtime dial. Different callers want different points on the frontier. Shipping it as a few named modes rather than a continuous knob is a defensible simplification. [T1, T2]
  • Decide first-party versus third-party mediation deliberately. The cheap aggregator path is the one with the unmeasured integrity liability. [T1]
  • Measure your own cost per unit of quality, per unit of repair labor, and per commit. You will not find any of the three in the papers. [T1, T2, T3]

The trade every one of these buys

A learned allocator is a second system to train, monitor and re-tune, and it introduces a component that can be wrong in a way a fixed cap cannot: it can stop an agent that was one iteration from success. The published policies optimize average cost against average quality. None of them measures the tail. A stopping rule that quits early on the 5% of tasks that mattered most would look excellent on every metric in every source cited here.

The non-ladder result adds a second, subtler cost. A cascade at least offers a monotone story — spend more, get more. A router over non-ordered actions cannot, which is exactly why CodeRescue's risk guarantee is on expected cost rather than solve rate. You are trading an intuitive (if wrong) quality floor for a real cost bound.

And the stopping rule inherits this vault's oldest problem. SCATE stops when measured coverage plateaus — a proxy. It cannot know whether the untested branches are the important ones, and the surrounding suite frequently does not execute the agent's own changed lines at all (test coverage of agentic PRs, [T1]). Routing on a proxy is evaluation integrity's standing warning applied to spend: a shallow or contaminated quality estimate routes on a false axis, confidently. VRR-Stop is the sharpest form of it — there, the proxy sits inside the control loop, so its error is not merely reported but acted on, and compounds.

Open and contested

  • The upgrade is on structure, not numbers, and that distinction is load-bearing. SeqRoute's 6–73.5%, SCATE's coverage/cost deltas, CodeRescue's 81.7% and VRR-Stop's +60.6 pp are each one setup's, in different object classes, and none has been reproduced by anyone. Treat the shape as consolidating and every specific effect size as a single study's.
  • The first production router publishes savings and withholds the parity check. A 60% savings figure means nothing without the quality term, and the two criteria offered are both proprietary and both graded by the vendor. Keep rate is the interesting one — it asks whether the code was still there later, which is exactly the quantity independent survival analysis has measured pessimistically. A published keep-rate definition would let those two numbers be compared for the first time. Without one, they are two unrelated statistics about the same question.
  • Nobody has run two units under one budget. Model choice, iteration count and recovery-action kind are three allocators pointed at the same wallet. Do they compose, or compete for the same savings? Still untried.
  • The cache lever and the state levers are in tension and nobody has measured them together. Synced or rewritten context breaks prefix reuse; prefix reuse is what made the frontier API the cheap tier. Both results are real; their interaction is unpublished.
  • Routing quality is unmeasured. How often does the policy send a hard query to a weak model, or stop an agent one step from done? Cost saved is measured; the failure mode is not.
  • Policies do not transfer across agents, and nobody has measured how fast one goes stale across a model upgrade — which, given that harnesses need re-tuning on every release, is the question that decides whether this is a technique or a treadmill.
  • The trust/price trade-off has a numerator and no denominator. Nine of 428 surveyed commodity routers were observed injecting malicious tool calls, but there is no prevalence estimate and no published measurement of what the aggregator path actually saves. Until both exist, the deployment decision is being made on vibes in both directions.
  • Does the repair-damage result survive a frontier agent with an executable oracle? The damage rates are measured on small open models with degraded verifiers. Take a frontier coding agent, a real fail-to-pass suite as the verifier, and measure the sign of the marginal gain per additional repair round. The trajectories already exist on every leaderboard submission. Until someone does, we do not know whether a good oracle makes unbounded iteration safe or merely moves the optimum outward.
  • An unverifiable public claim worth a follow-up, not a citation. A harness generator reports SWE-bench Verified 55.6% via a "GLM→Opus escalation" at ~$0.15/instance and LiveCodeBench 44% → 62% by cost cascade (metaharness, [T3], single-study). The shape matches what this article carries and the price would sit at the low end of the documented range, but there is no escalation policy described, no trigger definition, no pass@1-versus-best-of-k statement and no seeds — including nothing that says whether the ladder was tuned on the benchmark it reports. Carried as a lead only.
