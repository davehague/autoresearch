Model routing and token economics: budget the next increment of effort

As of 2026-07, two independent results — one routing models across a session, one routing iterations within a task — have converged on the same instruction, from opposite ends. Neither replicates the other's numbers. What they replicate is the structure, and the structure is the finding:

Allocate agent spend sequentially, under a global budget, from a policy learned on cheap observable state. Not greedily. Not on a fixed schedule.

The corollary is the part most teams can act on this week: the unit of routing is not the query, and not even the model — it is the next increment of effort. The decision "should this agent take another step" is a routing decision, it is the one nearly every harness makes badly, and it is the one nobody is measuring.

Greedy routing spends the budget on the easy turns

Route each query in isolation and you get a per-query classifier that has no idea a session is coming. It spends freely on the early, easy turns and arrives at the hard later ones with nothing left. Modeled instead as a budget-constrained finite-horizon decision — an offline-RL policy over 10k sessions and 2.38M transitions — the router learns something a per-query classifier structurally cannot: delayed gratification. It reserves the expensive capacity for the turns that will need it. Measured cost savings of 6.0–73.5%, bankruptcy under 1%, with quality held on the Pareto frontier (SeqRoute, [T1]).

The trade this buys: you need session-level state to route on, and you need to have decided what your budget is. A per-query router is stateless and needs neither. That simplicity is exactly what it is paying for.

Ship the cost/quality setting as a deploy-time dial, not a compiled-in operating point [T1]. A CI bot, an interactive session, and a nightly batch want the same policy set to three different places on the frontier, and one trained policy can navigate it without retraining.

The stopping rule is a routing decision, and it is agent-conditional

The second result comes from a completely different problem — supervising an agent writing tests — and lands on the same economics. A contextual bandit chooses, at each iteration, one of three actions: generate, analyze-then-generate, or stop. It reads seven cheap features (lines of code, weighted methods per class, response-for-class, current line and branch coverage, missed complexity) under a reward with an explicit cost penalty. Against a forced 8-iteration budget on 80 held-out Java classes it delivers 82.8% line / 74.7% branch / 64.7% mutation coverage at $0.693, versus 77.3 / 70.2 / 59.6 at $0.797 (SCATE, [T1]). More quality, less money, from deciding when to keep going.

The finding worth changing your harness over is what the policy learns when you point it at two different agents. It learns opposite policies:

  • Against a strong-zero-shot agent (Claude Code — already at 84.3% line coverage on iteration 1), it stops early on 95.0% of tasks, averaging 2.1 iterations, and rarely bothers with the analysis tool (10.0%).
  • Against a weaker, cheaper one (Gemini-CLI), it runs 7.9 iterations and reaches for analysis three times as often (33.8%).

Same framework, same features, same reward. So the fixed iteration cap that ships as the default in essentially every agent harness is wrong in both directions at once: it over-spends on the agent that was finished at step 2, and it under-spends on the one still climbing at step 8. Iteration-parity does not imply value-parity.

Cost is a selection axis, and the published record does not have one

Among models of comparable quality, operational cost can differ by up to three orders of magnitude on production-like serial workloads (RAMP, [T1]). Quality-parity does not imply cost-parity. Pick on a quality leaderboard alone and you can silently pay ~1000× more than an equally good alternative would have cost.

That dispersion is what makes budgeting worth doing at all — and it is invisible in the literature you would use to make the decision. A pilot audit of twelve benchmark papers found 0 of 8 agent benchmarks disclose inference cost (disclosure audit, [T1], single-study). Anyone routing on published quality numbers is routing on one axis of a two-axis decision, with the missing axis known to span 1000×.

What to do

  • Set a session budget, and route against it. Not a per-call cap. [T1]
  • Make "stop" an action your harness can take, and learn when to take it. A fixed retry/iteration cap is a policy — just an unexamined one. [T1]
  • Retune the budget policy per agent. The correct amount of effort is a property of the agent, not just the task. Budget the retraining as a recurring per-agent cost (~$48–127 and 13–23 hours in the one published account), not a one-off — and expect it to go stale on every model upgrade, the way the rest of the harness does (harness staleness, [T2]). [T1]
  • Expose cost/quality as a runtime dial. Different callers want different points on the frontier. [T1]
  • Measure your own cost per unit of quality. You will not find it in the papers. [T1]

The trade every one of these buys

A learned allocator is a second system to train, monitor, and re-tune, and it introduces a component that can be wrong in a way a fixed cap cannot: it can stop an agent that was one iteration from success. Both published policies optimize average cost against average quality. Neither measures the tail. A stopping rule that quits early on the 5% of tasks that mattered most would look excellent on every metric in either paper.

And the stopping rule inherits this vault's oldest problem. SCATE stops when measured coverage plateaus — a proxy. It cannot know whether the untested branches are the important ones. Routing on a proxy is evaluation integrity's standing warning applied to spend: a shallow or contaminated quality estimate routes on a false axis, confidently.

Where this connects

The agent that SCATE is supervising was writing tests — the mechanical backstop that agentic code review and CI both depend on. Left unsupervised, it exhibits what the authors call lazy generation: it "systematically avoids complex logic," plateaus near 50% line / 44% branch coverage, and reports success anyway. Their opening exhibit is an agent announcing tests "generated and verified" with branch coverage at 31% [T1].

That is the same self-report failure measured across 20,574 real sessions, where 91.49% of visible misalignment resolutions required explicit user correction (misalignment study, [T1]) — arriving somewhere worse. A green test suite over untested branches looks exactly like a green test suite. What recovered the thirty missing points here was not a better prompt: it was an external supervisor with a stopping rule, which is the generator/evaluator split with an economic policy bolted on. The thing outside the agent does not just grade the work. It decides how much more to spend finding out whether the work is done.

Open and contested

  • The upgrade is on structure, not numbers, and that distinction is load-bearing. SeqRoute's 6–73.5% and SCATE's coverage/cost deltas are each one setup's, in different object classes, and neither has been reproduced by anyone. Treat the shape as consolidating and every specific effect size as a single study's.
  • Nobody has run both units under one budget. Model choice and stopping rule are two allocators pointed at the same wallet. Do they compose, or do they compete for the same savings? No one has tried.
  • Routing quality is unmeasured. How often does the policy send a hard query to a weak model, or stop an agent one step from done? Cost saved is measured; the failure mode is not.
  • Policies do not transfer across agents, and nobody has measured how fast one goes stale across a model upgrade — which, given that harnesses need re-tuning on every release, is the question that decides whether this is a technique or a treadmill.
