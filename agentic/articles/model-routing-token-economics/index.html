Model routing and token economics: budget the next increment of effort

As of 2026-07, two independent results — one routing models across a session, one routing iterations within a task — converged on the same instruction from opposite ends. Neither replicates the other's numbers. What they replicate is the structure, and the structure is the finding:

Allocate agent spend sequentially, under a global budget, from a policy learned on cheap observable state. Not greedily. Not on a fixed schedule.

The corollary is the part most teams can act on this week: the unit of routing is not the query, and not even the model — it is the next increment of effort. The decision "should this agent take another step" is a routing decision, it is the one nearly every harness makes badly, and until recently nobody was measuring it.

Refreshed 2026-07-22. Two things changed since this article was first written. The stopping rule turned out not to be a cost lever at all — it is a correctness control, and past a certain point spend and quality move together rather than trading off. And the "cheap tier" stopped being a menu item: four separate results now show it is something you build, out of harness design, tool surface, cache behaviour, retrieval structure, and stage staffing.

Greedy routing spends the budget on the easy turns

Route each query in isolation and you get a per-query classifier that has no idea a session is coming. It spends freely on the early, easy turns and arrives at the hard later ones with nothing left. Modeled instead as a budget-constrained finite-horizon decision — an offline-RL policy over 10k sessions and 2.38M transitions — the router learns something a per-query classifier structurally cannot: delayed gratification. It reserves the expensive capacity for the turns that will need it. Measured cost savings of 6.0–73.5%, bankruptcy under 1%, with quality held on the Pareto frontier (SeqRoute, [T1]).

The trade this buys: you need session-level state to route on, and you need to have decided what your budget is. A per-query router is stateless and needs neither. That simplicity is exactly what it is paying for.

Ship the cost/quality setting as a deploy-time dial, not a compiled-in operating point [T1]. A CI bot, an interactive session, and a nightly batch want the same policy set to three different places on the frontier, and one trained policy can navigate it without retraining.

One refinement worth having before you tune anything: the waste is not where you would guess. Measured by a compute-to-requirement ratio, relative over-spend peaks on the easiest tasks — 22.1 on simple, 11.0 on medium, 5.4 on hard (execution-scope estimation, [T1], single-study). Agents burn the most proportional budget on work that needed almost none, which argues for estimating scope before gathering context rather than pruning after.

The stopping rule is a routing decision — and it is a correctness control

The second result came from a completely different problem — supervising an agent writing tests — and landed on the same economics. A contextual bandit chooses, at each iteration, one of three actions: generate, analyze-then-generate, or stop. It reads seven cheap features under a reward with an explicit cost penalty. Against a forced 8-iteration budget on 80 held-out Java classes it delivers 82.8% line / 74.7% branch / 64.7% mutation coverage at $0.693, versus 77.3 / 70.2 / 59.6 at $0.797 (SCATE, [T1]). More quality, less money, from deciding when to keep going.

The finding worth changing your harness over is what the policy learns when you point it at two different agents. It learns opposite policies:

  • Against a strong-zero-shot agent (Claude Code — already at 84.3% line coverage on iteration 1), it stops early on 95.0% of tasks, averaging 2.1 iterations, and rarely reaches for the analysis tool (10.0%).
  • Against a weaker, cheaper one (Gemini-CLI), it runs 7.9 iterations and uses analysis three times as often (33.8%).

Same framework, same features, same reward. So the fixed iteration cap that ships as the default in essentially every agent harness is wrong in both directions at once: it over-spends on the agent that was finished at step 2, and under-spends on the one still climbing at step 8. Iteration-parity does not imply value-parity.

The 2026-07-22 upgrade: past the optimum, spend and quality move together

A second, independent arrival makes a harder claim than "stopping saves money." In a verify-repair loop where both the verifier and the repairer are noisy, additional rounds do not merely cost more — they destroy correct work. True validity peaks at round 2 and then collapses; across eight settings six decline monotonically, with per-round damage probabilities of 0.615–0.938. The raw labels are the part to sit with: 55% of correct plans get repaired into incorrect ones, and 24% of those damaging repairs win majority acceptance (VRR-Stop, [T1], suggestive). Reported acceptance keeps climbing while true validity falls, which is why a budget tuned on "the verifier looks happier now" runs past the optimum by construction.

Stopping on the estimated sign of the marginal gain rather than on acceptance votes reaches 0.722 true validity at 0.72 average repair rounds, against 0.116 at five fixed rounds — seven times cheaper and sixty points better. It slightly beats a reference policy handed the ground-truth parameters, which is the tell that fixed round budgets are a category error rather than a tuning error.

So revise the claim above. The iteration cap is not wrong in two directions on a cost/quality trade-off; past the loop's interior optimum there is no trade-off, because every additional round is buying damage. That reframes what the stopping rule is for: it is a correctness control that happens to be cheaper, and it belongs in this article only because the same machinery implements both.

Two engineering details transfer even if the magnitudes do not:

  • The decision needs only sign identifiability, not accurate recovery of the loop's noise parameters, so it calibrates from a few hundred cross-fitted samples rather than a training run. [T1]
  • Know when your own instrument is unidentifiable. When verifier discrimination approaches zero, estimation error flips the stopping sign, and the correct policy is not a better estimator but an estimation-free retreat toward not repairing at all — which never collapsed across seven shifted settings, at the price of trailing fixed repair in the one genuinely favourable case. [T1]

Hold the size of this loosely: it is measured on 3–8B open-weight models in a deliberately adversarial setting, with a math-heavy headline and code generation appearing only in the robustness table. It is also in visible tension with a result from the week before, where a reviewer scored against executable tests did lift a weak generator from 27.5% to 56.9% and hit target in 2.44 samples versus 16 for best-of-N (SWE-Review, [T1]). The reconciling variable is verifier quality relative to generator quality — and the practical instruction that survives both: measure your verifier's discrimination before granting the loop a budget, and stop early when you cannot.

Cost is a selection axis, and the published record does not have one

Among models of comparable quality, operational cost can differ by up to three orders of magnitude on production-like serial workloads (RAMP, [T1]). Quality-parity does not imply cost-parity. Pick on a quality leaderboard alone and you can silently pay ~1000× more than an equally good alternative would have cost.

That dispersion is what makes budgeting worth doing at all — and it is invisible in the literature you would use to make the decision. A pilot audit of twelve benchmark papers found 0 of 8 agent benchmarks disclose inference cost (disclosure audit, [T1], single-study). Anyone routing on published quality numbers is routing on one axis of a two-axis decision, with the missing axis known to span 1000×.

It is worse than undisclosed on the performance side, where the number is also environment-conditioned: replaying the official reference patches of three performance benchmarks across cloud machine types found them valid on only 38% / 8% / 82% of replays, with scoring flipping 9 of 28 rankings (benchmark reliability, [T1]). A published speedup is a claim about a machine, not about code.

The cheap tier is something you build

The largest change since this article was written is that "route to a cheaper model" turned out to be the least interesting lever in the ledger. Five results now point the same way — you can move the price of a unit of quality without touching the model at all:

  • Adapt the small model's harness up to the bar. A harness optimizer mining failure trajectories let small models recover up to 89.7% of a frontier LLM's performance at 4% of the cost (better harnesses, smaller models, [T1], suggestive). Before routing away from the cheap model, consider adapting its harness up. The optimizer's one-time search compute is the cost to weigh against recurring savings.
  • Cut tool surface. Restricting a coding agent to a single execute_code channel held pass rate and was cheaper in 3 of 4 regime×agent cells (execute_code ablation, [T1], suggestive). Cache-adjusted cost, not pass rate, decides the cheapest surface.
  • Design the retrieval, not the model. A structural codebase index (embeddings + a graph of definitions and call edges + BM25) landed at $2.30 per solved task versus $2.92 for an agentic-grep baseline while resolving more — cheaper because it finds the edit site faster and wastes fewer turns (structural index, [T1], suggestive).
  • Design the cache. In one production deployment a 99.3% prompt-cache hit rate made the frontier API the cheap tier — $0.573/M effective, below locally-served quantized open weights at $2.83/M (inference economics, [T3], n=1 self-study). Published per-token rates are the wrong number for high-reuse agentic work.
  • Staff the stage, not the task. (New 2026-07-22.) A production system runs each loop stage on the lightest model that performs it reliably: four post-trained specialists at 0.5–4B, served at 0.02–0.1× frontier per-call cost, which is what makes "verify every step" an affordable default — the compare stage ran on all 397 loop-triggering tasks rather than a sample. Its 0.5B step-type classifier routing across model families is credited with ~4 pp of end-to-end accuracy at net-negative cost (reliability decomposition, [T3], vendor self-evaluation).

There is a sixth lever that is really a warning: scale. On book-length retrieval tasks, as the corpus grows the cost–accuracy frontier recedes down and to the right — accuracy falls and the per-question cost of buying any fixed accuracy rises, so a larger corpus is strictly less efficient to read, not merely harder (progressive disclosure, [T1], suggestive). The frontier's top point also migrates from bare-agent navigation to a packaged context pack only once the corpus is large, which is the same "it depends on the size of your search space" conditional as the structural index.

The bill you move rather than remove

One result deserves its own heading because it is the only one here that measures where the saved money goes. In a year-long production deployment, the cheap configuration relocated cost into repair labor: fix-commit ratio rose 45.9% → 74.9% (odds ratio 3.61, holding within every difficulty tier), leaving a monotonic routing frontier with no dominating policy — hybrid routing bought savings with defects, not instead of them (inference economics, [T3], single deployment).

Take the structure, not the number: a cost saving that shows up in the inference bill and not in the engineering-hours bill has probably not been measured end to end. Nobody else has published this decomposition, and it is the measurement most likely to change a routing decision.

What to do

  • Set a session budget, and route against it. Not a per-call cap. [T1]
  • Make "stop" an action your harness can take, and learn when to take it. A fixed retry/iteration cap is a policy — just an unexamined one. And past the loop's optimum it is a correctness bug, not an overspend. [T1]
  • Measure your verifier's discrimination before you fund the loop. If you cannot measure it, stop early; the asymmetry is severe and acceptance rates will not warn you. [T1]
  • Estimate scope before gathering context. The proportional waste concentrates on the easy tasks. [T1]
  • Try to build the cheap tier before you route to it — harness adaptation, tool-surface reduction, retrieval structure, cache design, and stage staffing all move price without moving the model. [T1 ×3, T3 ×2]
  • Retune the budget policy per agent. The correct amount of effort is a property of the agent, not just the task. Budget the retraining as a recurring per-agent cost (~$48–127 and 13–23 hours in the one published account), not a one-off — and expect it to go stale on every model upgrade, the way the rest of the harness does (harness staleness, [T2]). [T1]
  • Expose cost/quality as a runtime dial. Different callers want different points on the frontier. [T1]
  • Measure your own cost per unit of quality, and per unit of repair labor. You will not find either in the papers. [T1]

The trade every one of these buys

A learned allocator is a second system to train, monitor, and re-tune, and it introduces a component that can be wrong in a way a fixed cap cannot: it can stop an agent that was one iteration from success. The published policies optimize average cost against average quality. None of them measures the tail. A stopping rule that quits early on the 5% of tasks that mattered most would look excellent on every metric in every paper cited here.

And the stopping rule inherits this vault's oldest problem. SCATE stops when measured coverage plateaus — a proxy. It cannot know whether the untested branches are the important ones, and as of 2026-07-22 we know the surrounding suite frequently does not execute the agent's own changed lines at all (test coverage of agentic PRs, [T1]). Routing on a proxy is evaluation integrity's standing warning applied to spend: a shallow or contaminated quality estimate routes on a false axis, confidently. VRR-Stop is the sharpest form of it — there, the proxy sits inside the control loop, so its error is not merely reported but acted on, and compounds.

Open and contested

  • The upgrade is on structure, not numbers, and that distinction is load-bearing. SeqRoute's 6–73.5%, SCATE's coverage/cost deltas, and VRR-Stop's +60.6 pp are each one setup's, in different object classes, and none has been reproduced by anyone. Treat the shape as consolidating and every specific effect size as a single study's.
  • Nobody has run both units under one budget. Model choice and stopping rule are two allocators pointed at the same wallet. Do they compose, or compete for the same savings? Still untried.
  • Routing quality is unmeasured. How often does the policy send a hard query to a weak model, or stop an agent one step from done? Cost saved is measured; the failure mode is not.
  • Policies do not transfer across agents, and nobody has measured how fast one goes stale across a model upgrade — which, given that harnesses need re-tuning on every release, is the question that decides whether this is a technique or a treadmill.
  • NEW: does the repair-damage result survive a frontier agent with an executable oracle? The damage rates are measured on small open models with degraded verifiers. Take a frontier coding agent, a real fail-to-pass suite as the verifier, and measure the sign of the marginal gain per additional repair round. The trajectories already exist on every leaderboard submission. Until someone does, we do not know whether a good oracle makes unbounded iteration safe or merely moves the optimum outward.
