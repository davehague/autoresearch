Loop engineering: four things hide inside "independent verifier" — and the null is bigger than most reported effects

Refreshed 2026-07-31 against fourteen new through-line entries. Three things changed, and the first is a correction to this piece. The old headline — "coordination isn't the tax, routing raw, unvalidated state is" — stated a contested hypothesis as a finding. The map has been contested since 2026-07-10 and still is; the substrate story is candidate reconciliation (A), it has been tilted twice more by production evidence since, and it has never been tested. It is carried below as an open tension, where it belongs. Second, the load-bearing word in this article's own through-line got unpacked: a connect sweep on 2026-07-29 found that "independent verifier" names four separate properties, and four results that looked like they contradicted each other stop contradicting once you separate them. Third — and this is the one that should change what you measure on Monday — the null under every loop result on this page now has a number, and on mid-tier models it is larger than most of the effects.

Refreshed again 2026-08-09 against twelve further through-line entries, and this one is the largest change since the piece was written, because the counterfactual it called unrun has been run. Since 2026-07-25 the map had named the arm nobody had tried: apply the same admission discipline with one strong agent, serially. A confirmatory study ran it as a fourth arm, and always-serial ties static admission inside every cluster — same pair pass, same integration rate, same conflict-stratum rate, and faster. That does not kill hypothesis (A); it relocates it. The discipline is what pays, and on this task class the concurrency it was protecting bought nothing back. Three other things moved. The loop is now a scored object: at a fixed model, swapping loop implementations moves resolve rate 18.75% → 7.14%, a spread larger than most model-tier gaps on this page. Stopping got a deployable controller — a 0.6B monitor reclaiming a fifth of execution tokens at a 5% false-positive budget — and with it the reconciliation this article owed between "blind resampling wins" and "restarts are expensive." And the noisy-verifier story got a sharper replacement: a loop whose verifier is perfectly reliable and simply measures the wrong thing walks the artifact downhill while every reported number climbs.

Refreshed again 2026-08-25 against thirteen further through-line entries, and this one changes the frame rather than a finding. Everything above was measured on loops in laboratories. A mining study has now put the practice inside a denominator — 36,645 public engineered repositories, 217 confirmed autonomous agent loops, every candidate inspected by hand — and the result is that the architecture this article has spent three refreshes arguing about is almost entirely uncommitted. Triggers are in git. Stop conditions, verifiers, budgets, cost logs and loop state are not, anywhere. That does not make the engineering arguments wrong; it means nobody has been able to check them from outside, and it explains why every benefit number in the practitioner discourse is a self-report. The same source supplies the principle this piece had been circling without naming: verification cost, not model capability, is the bound on delegation. Two adjacent arrivals land beside it. The other end of the loop got measured too — closed-loop AI-reviewing-AI now runs at 248,641 pull requests with a 1.2-minute median from open to first review — and the context an unattended loop carries turns out to shed its own governing rules at 0.53 after one compaction round and 0.10 after five.

Prior history, kept because this piece has been wrong three times: in early July it argued long-horizon work wants a coordinator plus isolated workers, and a controlled scaling study made that contested. The 2026-07-10 rewrite swung to "coordinate decomposable work, not long work" with a ~45% saturation rule, and a fleet that coordinates tool-heavy SWE from a 59.1% baseline killed the rule. The 2026-07-11 rewrite said "the tax is shared context, never share a window," and a shared-context fleet won anyway. The 2026-07-25 version then promoted the surviving hypothesis into the title, which is the error this refresh undoes.

As of 2026-07-31, one sentence survives at both scales: an unvalidated loop is negative value. Whether the loop passes state between workers or rewrites a candidate in place, the load-bearing component is the check — not the schedule, not the iteration count. Everything else on this page is either an argument about what a check has to be or an argument about what you are comparing the loop against, and in the last week both got sharper.

Read in that order, because it is the order in which the money is lost:

  1. Price the null. An extra sample is worth 10–21 points on a mid-tier agent. Most loop results have never been compared against one.
  2. Separate the four properties of "independent." Three of the vault's four verifier results were filed as conflicting; they are measuring different axes.
  3. Then engineer the loop — stopping rule, evidence binding, recovery action, topology.

Part one: price the null before you credit the loop

The extra sample is worth more than the feedback

The standard self-repair comparison is loop versus no retry, which quietly charges the mechanism for something a blank retry would have bought. Decompose it and the mechanism's share is not detectable. Four conditions at k_max = 8 on MBPP+ (378 tasks) with Qwen2.5-Coder 1.5B/3B/7B — blind resample (the failed attempt is never shown), placebo (the failed program plus a content-free failure notice), feedback (plus real test output), reflect (plus a prose diagnosis) — put the informational payload of execution feedback, feedback minus placebo, at +0.000 / +0.026 / +0.008, none significant at any scale [T1] (Try Again, Don't Look Back). Meanwhile merely seeing your own failed attempt costs −0.061 pass@1 at 1.5B (p = 0.006) and −0.069 at 3B (p < 0.001). Blind resampling wins outright at 1.5B and 3B, ties at 7B, and does it at 2.5–5.5× fewer tokens.

The mechanism is measured rather than inferred: with its own output in view the model regenerates a near-identical program (similarity > 0.95) in 33–68% of retries against 2–14% blind. And the size of the penalty is predicted by baseline pass@1 alone at r = 0.96, so it is the cost of committing to a bad first attempt, and it shrinks as first attempts get good (showing-a-failed-attempt-anchors-the-retry).

Bounds, and they are tight: 1.5–7B open-weight models of one family, quantized, function-level synthesis on one benchmark family, a single unfunded author, one reflection-prompt wording, feedback drawn from the scoring suite. No claim about agentic harnesses or frontier models — 7B is already non-significant, and the r = 0.96 fit predicts the penalty vanishing as baseline quality rises. The methodological claim travels further than the magnitudes: the placebo arm is the treatment condition with the payload blanked, which makes it nearly free to run (a-retry-study-needs-a-placebo-arm).

And now the null has a magnitude

Nobody had said how large "another attempt at the same budget" actually is. A multilingual long-horizon benchmark measured it incidentally, across 15 leaderboard entries run more than once: best-of-3 minus single-run runs from +0.007 at the top of the board to +0.206 at the bottom, growing monotonically as base capability drops [T1] (PolyWorkBench) — +0.10 to +0.11 for the DeepSeek entries regardless of harness, +0.141 to +0.206 for the mid-tier Qwen and GPT-5.5 cells (resampling-headroom-is-inverse-to-capability).

That number sits directly underneath a result this vault has leaned on. A review-revise loop lifts resolve rate inversely to generator strength — weak generator 27.5% → 56.9% (+29.4 pp), mid 50.9% → 68.8%, strong 72.2% → 75.4% (+3.2 pp) [T1, suggestive] (SWE-Review). A pure-resampling arm with the same shape and the same order of magnitude now exists — nothing was fed back, the agent simply drew again — so the share of that lift attributable to information returned into the loop, rather than to another sample, is unestablished on exactly the models where the lift is largest.

Two practitioner readings, pointing opposite ways:

  • Deployment. A substantial fraction of a mid-tier agent's single-run failures are transient stochastic errors recoverable by sampling. A saturated top-tier entry has no headroom for any technique that works by re-drawing — including, plausibly, your repair loop.
  • Measurement. The noise floor is not constant down the leaderboard. A fixed "gaps under N points are unresolved" rule is too strict at the top and far too lenient in the middle.

The scope condition is real and cuts against over-reading this: a benchmark's oracle can pick the good sample and a production loop generally cannot (report-current-correctness-not-only-ever-correct). So this bounds what the loop is competing against, not what the loop delivers.

The same discipline, one level up: which implementation, not which idea

Architecture comparisons have the matching problem, and it is worse than the seed. Freeze a mechanism as an outcome-blind card, implement it three times in fresh sessions, replay each artifact byte-identically: same-artifact rerun variance is 4–6% of within-task variation while the choice among legitimate implementations of the same mechanism is 33–45% — five to ten times larger — and the winner reverses 25.6% and 43.6% of the time [T1] (One Run Is Not an Idea). In a deterministic-evaluator arm where rerun variance is zero by construction, implementation still accounts for 71–82% (one-run-scores-an-implementation-not-an-idea).

"Event passing beats a shared pool" is a claim about a mechanism. Each arm of the decisive experiment below would be one particular implementation of that mechanism, and a reversal rate of a quarter to nearly half is the same order as the +8.3 and +9.3–10.5 point effects this article is arguing about. The experiment therefore needs several independent implementations per arm. Caveats the authors state plainly: tabular ML, model-generated idea cards, model-assigned fidelity labels, one evaluator, and rates that describe their deployed workflow rather than an error rate for arbitrary projects.

The starting-state discipline is the third leg, and it has now been run rather than merely demanded. An adversarial test-hardening study let each arm draw its own round-0 state and found five independent draws on one subject spanning 29 to 45 kills, one configuration producing 69/168/50/62/65 on another [T1, suggestive for this edge] (Adversarial Test-Hardening). Under a frozen, shared round 0 the critic loop's incremental contribution is a clean within-replicate 78% of remaining survivors (adversarial-critic-rounds-add-kills-over-a-frozen-suite). Freeze and share the starting state, commit it before any continuation may run, report a within-replicate delta — otherwise you are measuring your own initial draw (coordination-deltas-need-a-measured-noise-floor, control-the-data-draw-before-the-seed).

Part two: "independent verifier" names four different things

This is the reframing the map named on 2026-07-29, and it carries no new evidence — it is a reconciliation of four existing T1 claims that had been filed as conflicting. State them together and the conflict is obvious:

  • A cheap call to a different model, re-deriving from the original request, recovers 79% of injected errors against self-verification's 71%, with a ground-truth oracle at 80% — the practical cross-verifier landing within a point of the oracle on all eight backends, and the winning verifier smaller than what it checks [T1] (Binding Drift).
  • A cross-family verifier was the worst of three at matched coverage — risk 0.240 against 0.041 and 0.050 — and error dependence turned out to be positive for all three pairs at 75% coverage [T1] (Looping Is Not Reliability).
  • A narration-blind verifier reading the real artifact still passed 68 of the 76 wrong answers it saw, while being 8-of-9 precise on the ones it rejected [T1] (StateAct).
  • A weak independent reviewer on a strong draft is a significant net loss — 3 fixes against 13 regressions, −8.6 pp — while the same operation with the capability ordering reversed is +18.1 pp [T1] (Cross-Model LLM Code Review).

Four axes, and no result varies more than two at once:

AxisWhat it meansMeasured how
independent weightsa different model in the verifier slottrivially — a vendor diff
independent narrationinspecting the artifact, not the agent's account of itby construction of the harness
independent errorsstatistically uncorrelated failureserror correlation at your acceptance operating point — never inferable from a vendor diff
independent capabilityenough headroom to add anythingsolo baselines of writer and reviewer

The discriminator the results imply: what a cheap different model buys is re-derivation from an external anchor, not independence. Which is why a much smaller model suffices where an anchor exists — the original instruction, a test suite, program state — and fails where the verifier must supply judgment with nothing to re-derive from. There, headroom sets the sign.

Practical rule: anchor present → buy cheap independent weights. Anchor absent → you are buying capability, and a weaker reviewer is negative value. [T1 ×4, reconciliation only — stated for the first time on 2026-07-29 and untested as a discriminator. It does not resolve this map's contested status; it localizes what would.]

Two riders. The anchor is necessary and not sufficient: the narration-blind gate above had the anchor — the real file, the real store — and missed nine errors in ten anyway, because independence was arranged over what the checker may see and not over how it reads the source. Only 14 of its 68 misses were things a structural check could catch. Buy that gate for what it sells — structural completeness, at zero per-task engineering, converting 6 of the 28 tasks it retried and costing 3.8 binary points when removed — and not for value correctness. And the cheap test nobody runs: before deploying a reviewer, run it over drafts you already know pass and count the regressions (reviewer-value-tracks-capability-headroom). A gate that rewrites 13 correct solutions per 106 to catch 3 has negative value and a resolution rate that looks fine.

Part three: the loop inside one agent

First ask whether the loop can reach the failure at all

New as of 2026-08-09, and it belongs before everything else in this part, because it bounds what any of it can buy. Inject five failure modes into a staged pipeline run by a real agent and recovery splits into three tiers that do not blur: a tool-invocation error recovers 1.00 with cascade radius 0 — the agent notices the tool failed and redoes the work by hand — ambiguous delegation recovers 0.30, and context pollution, conflicting sub-agent outputs and premature action recover 0.00, corrupting every downstream stage [T1, single-study] (OrchestraBench). The ordering holds across three capability tiers of the same model family — only the stochastic ambiguous cell moves, and non-monotonically — and survives reframing the identical computation as a loan-approval workflow, where the absolute rates shift (tool 1.00 → 0.70, ambiguous 0.30 → 0.40) while the ranking does not.

The dividing line is whether the failure announces itself. That yields an uncomfortable statement about every repair loop on this page: the failures your loop can self-heal are the ones your monitoring already catches. The tiers it cannot reach are the silent ones, and there the default remediation is worse than neutral — retry reproduces the fault and only lengthens time to detection. Depth multiplies it: mean cascade radius grows 0.93 → 4.67 across pipeline depths 3 to 7, though the authors flag that on a chain where each stage consumes the upstream value that growth is partly structural, so carry the ordering rather than the slope.

Read this next to the ~30% of failures that no in-loop recovery action reaches, from a different study below: two groups, two constructions, both finding a floor that stopping rules and better verifiers do not move. Heavy caveats — one agent simulating orchestration rather than N wired agents, a synthetic verifiable chain, n=30 per mode with the 0.00 and 1.00 cells as point masses by construction, and the authors framing all of it as controlled-chain mechanism probes rather than domain-workload claims.

Check what the feedback measures, not just how noisy it is

The founding result in this part is a noisy verifier destroying correct work. The sharper version, added 2026-08-08, has a verifier that is perfectly reliable and measures the wrong thing. In regression-oracle generation the free signal is "does the test pass"; the objective is "does it catch a fault." On 500 Java instances graded by mutation kill rate inside the behavioural scope the golden oracle exercises, an iterative self-repair pipeline reaches a higher pass rate than a single shot (68.4 / 73.2 against 55.0 / 61.4) and a lower kill rate on the problems both solve (87.54 / 86.31 against 92.77 / 91.65) — on both models, with the oracles first passing at later rounds progressively weaker [T1] (Escaping the Self-Repair Trap). The descent is three lines long: assertEquals(1, …) → assertTrue(… > 0) → assertNotNull(…), each edit a rational response to a reported failure and each removing a check.

The scope condition matters more than the result: an adversarial critic loop graded on mutation survivors improves the same artifact class. So the variable is what the feedback measures, not whether the loop iterates — which upgrades this part's rule from don't iterate against a noisy verifier to don't iterate against a proxy. The remedy is itself a loop finding, stated negatively: a non-iterative context-first pipeline beats the loop on both quality axes at roughly a quarter of the API cost and up to 77% less wall-clock.

The upstream version of the same problem got an instrument on 2026-08-06. Execution-guided self-correction builds its verification signal from generated reference code and then trusts it; grading that reference first — without labels, from execution behaviour alone, with per-candidate error rates fitted by Dawid–Skene — cuts the number of problems where the pipeline emits feedback on already-correct code from 113.2 ± 3.06 to 14.0 ± 1.67 on the strongest model tested (and 81.2 → 11.6, 50.4 → 7.4, 30.0 → 1.2 on the others) [T1] (ExeCRE). That is this part's "55% of correct plans repaired into incorrect ones" attacked before the stop rule rather than after it, and the iterative curve shows no early-round dip where one baseline stays below its own base for the entire run.

The stopping rule now has a controller, and it settles the resampling argument

Part one said blind resampling beats feedback at ≤7B. This part said stop on the sign of the marginal gain. Neither told you when to pull the trigger in a real agent, and a 2026-08-05 result does, with a reconciliation attached [T1] (Fail-Fast, Restart-Smart).

The monitor is small and it transfers. A 0.6B classifier reading the issue statement plus a window of the last eight thought/action/observation steps and a pin of the latest patch-producing step — no policy logits, no hidden states — trained on one policy's trajectories and transferred unchanged to three others including a closed-weight model. At a 5% false-positive budget it reclaims 20.4% of execution tokens against a log-probability predictor's 12.5% and a step-count control's 11.4% (14.6–20.4% across four policies; 16.0% on the closed API, where the log-probability baseline is structurally unavailable). At a 25% budget it intercepts 68.3% of failing runs and reclaims 49.0% of tokens. Scaling the backbone 0.6B → 4B loses recall (45.5% → 41.3% at 10% FPR) — prefix-level failure signals saturate early, so this is a cheap component by nature rather than by compromise.

And what crosses the restart boundary is the part that reconciles Part one. Cold restart — blind resampling with a monitor choosing the moment, i.e. exactly what the placebo result appears to prescribe — is worth +0.2 (66.6% → 66.8%). A fresh rollout with no prompt history plus the aborted run's diff as a removable git apply-backed overlay that starts off and is framed as an unverified lead reaches 71.8% (+5.2), at 28.9% true-failure recovery against 20.2%, and 8.8% false-alarm loss against 27.5%. Across three open-weight policies: +4.0 / +5.2 / +3.0 against +1.6 / +0.2 / +1.4.

So the rule sharpens rather than reverses. The anchoring penalty is real — the authors report abandoning a text-summary channel because verbose summaries induce severe anchoring, arriving at Part one's finding from the design side — but it attaches to narrative, not to artifacts. Carry the diff, not the story; default it off; label it a lead, not a result.

It has an interior optimum, and past it the loop destroys work it already had

With verifier and repairer both noisy, true validity peaks at round 2 and then declines while verifier acceptance keeps rising: 55% of correct plans get repaired into incorrect ones, 24% of those damaging repairs win majority acceptance, and fixed five-round repair takes GSM8K validity from a 0.700 no-repair baseline to 0.116. An independent-resampling control at the same budget shows no decline, so the damage is path-dependent — from rewriting the same candidate. Stopping on the sign of the marginal gain instead of on acceptance votes is worth +60.6 pp of final validity at 0.72 rounds instead of 5 [T1] (Verify, Repair, Repeat, or Stop?). Caveat the authors print themselves: 3–8B open-weight models, adversarial setting, math-heavy — and in the favourable setting fixed repair slightly beats calibrated stopping.

New, and it holds validation constant. Over 900 trajectories / 2,700 revisions under forced revision, ever-correct rises 0.820 → 0.847 → 0.853 while current correctness falls 0.820 → 0.673 and recovers only to 0.693. At revision 3, 16.0% of trajectories had produced a correct patch and lost it [T1] (Looping Is Not Reliability). Pass@k and best-of-k report the first curve. A team reading them sees improvement across exactly the interval in which the submittable state is degrading (report-current-correctness-not-only-ever-correct).

Depth is not the variable — the shape is evidence-specific — and the paper makes the stopping rule explicit. With α = P(wrong→right) and β = P(right→wrong), continuation has negative expected value once q > α/(α+β): the licence for another round is a property of your current state, not of your budget. Same shape as the marginal-gain rule, arrived at from retention rather than validity.

The dominant mechanism is evidence that is true about the wrong state

A report generated from the previous revision reverts a fixed Caesar shift in 5/5 seeds. Under a common-state design that freezes one wrong and one correct program before assigning conditions — removing post-treatment risk-set bias over 2,430 branches — stale traces harm 34/135 correct starts against 4/135 for current ones: +22.2 points, task-cluster 95% CI [8.9, 37.0], exact Holm p = 0.0337. Harm is clustered rather than diffuse: 4 of 27 tasks at 7B, 12 of 27 at 14B.

The fix is a hash. Carry evidence as (source-hash, suite-hash, payload) and let it drive an edit only on a match (bind-evidence-to-the-state-it-describes). Read alongside the binding-drift result below, the operative variable is the one context-engineering named for files — pinned versus re-derived — now applied to verdicts.

Read the remedies honestly. State binding intercepts every stale-condition harm in replay (19 → 2, and 69 → 33 total harm with typed actions), and prospectively the same bundle eliminates correct-start harm (13/135 → 0/135) while costing seven repairs and failing its pre-registered joint criterion. The repository arm removes every unsafe completion and the only sound one. Safety and liveness trade; the authors claim an auditable specification of obligations, not a net win. Bounds: HumanEval function repair plausibly in training data, 27 tasks selected for observed repairability, and only one of six pre-registered contrasts survives Holm correction.

One free win worth taking today: all five 7B current-trace harms on one task were replies containing only STATUS: DONE and no code, so a fail-closed parser mapping an unparseable action to "keep" retains the checkpoint in every recorded branch. A harness edit, not a model edit.

Where the loop actually fails: the trigger, not the repair

A planning-domain harness gates its revise step as a typed action, legal at a turn only if four runtime-computed predicates hold — confidence below threshold, a sustained low-confidence streak, a cooldown at zero, and a counterfactual one-step preview clearing a minimum delta. None requires a model to evaluate, so "should I revise" is answered by the loop rather than by the thing being revised [T1 for this edge; planning domain, not software engineering] (How Much Heavy Lifting Can an Agent Harness Do?).

Three results to carry. The gate is what makes the loop cheap: the LLM enters on 4.3% of turns, and invocation rate is measured as an outcome of the gate rather than budgeted in advance. A revision loop can be net-negative on the metric that matters while improving the one it optimizes: the LLM-backed variant posts the highest F1 in its family (0.557) and the lowest win rate (53.7%), because revisions sharpen local targeting while spending turns from a finite budget — an interior optimum sitting below one revision per opportunity. And per-board effects span +0.140 to −0.148 F1 in two opposed regimes — recovery where confidence is sustainedly ambiguous, over-revision where the dip is transient and self-resolving — cancelling to −0.001 on aggregate. The preset library is fine; the firing condition is miscalibrated. Bounds: n = 54, one MCMC-friendly domain, one locally-served 9B model behind the gate.

That is the mirror image of the persistence failure. An agent that resolves an entity once and then acts on it through steps that only say "it" will drift; the intuitive defence — pin the first binding and reuse it — eliminates drift to 0% while amplifying wrong actions 3.0× (bootstrap CI [2.8, 3.3]) and taking strict workflow success from 29.2% to 0.0%, measured on disjoint workflow sets so the trade cannot hide inside one number [T1] (Binding Drift). The amplification rises with model capability — 1.8× on the smallest backend to 8.5× on Claude Opus 4.5 — because stronger models treat carried state as authoritative. Dependent chains amplify 16.6×, because the failure becomes self-consistent. Carry state, and re-derive it before anything irreversible (persisted-state-without-revalidation-amplifies-errors) — but note the other side: revalidating too eagerly is also a failure mode.

"Repair it" remains the weakest available response

Given execution feedback, three recovery actions — reflect (repair from the feedback), replan (fresh cheap attempt), escalate (stronger model) — solve 27.5% / 45.3% / 68.6% as fixed policies. Reflect, the default every coding harness ships, is the worst, below naive discard-and-retry. The actions are not a ladder: 28% of recoverable failures are cheap-only, 45% escalation-only, 27% both. Roughly 30% of failures are reached by none of them [T1] (CodeRescue) — a ceiling on what any within-agent loop can reach, however well validated.

The placebo result supplies the missing mechanism for that ranking: what distinguishes reflect is precisely the self-exposure measured above. The cheap harness change is to make discard-and-resample an available recovery action.

And the constructive complement: policies that repair from the symptom (4/73, 5/73, 6/73 across three named ones) are beaten 2–3× by one that attributes the failure to a step first and reruns from there (13/73; overall 55.8% → 63.6%) [T1; general agentic traces, not coding] (AgentDebugX). It stays on the safe side of the interior optimum by design — one rerun from an attributed checkpoint, not an open-ended loop (close-the-loop-from-attribution-to-rerun). The bounded caveat is the authors': the +7.8 points bundles attribution with the rerun.

Two boundary conditions close the limb. Not every correction is installable: a single fixed stop token installs at 0.97 ± 0.06 while open-ended corrections do not (spatial coordinates 0.53–0.71 at ±0.26–0.35, a generative fill 0.14 ± 0.04), and a verified repair reaches task success only where the corrected action is the sole remaining blocker (8/20 vs 0/15, p = 0.006; a flat 0/5 vs 0/5 where it is one of several) [T3] (Teach it to stop) — computer-use agents, carried for structure, not magnitude. And a loop that stops on the agent's own declaration is stopping on the trajectory's least reliable artifact: across 65 policy-governed tasks graded by 824 deterministic verifiers, nearly every failed trajectory ends with a confident claim that the governing document was followed, frequently citing the sections it violated [T1] (HANDBOOK.md). The replay above measures the same thing directly — self-report-only acceptance covers 99.8% at 29.5% risk (the-agents-final-report-is-its-least-reliable-artifact).

Part four: coordination between agents — still contested, now with a cheap instrument

The three T1s that started the contest

The sweep. 260 configurations across six agentic benchmarks, five architectures and three model families, with tools, prompts and token budgets standardized [T1] (Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems). Coordination's sign flips with task structure: +80.9% for centralized coordination on decomposable financial reasoning, −39.0% to −70.0% for every multi-agent topology on strict-ordering planning. The efficiency–tools interaction (β = −0.330, p < 0.001) is the dominant inhibitor, its worst case a 16-tool software-engineering task. Independent agents amplify errors 17.2×; a validating central orchestrator contains it to 4.4×.

The event-passing counterexample. icat-agent resolves 67.4% against 59.10% on SWE-bench Pro with the model held fixed — +8.3 points, from a baseline fourteen points above the ~45% threshold where coordination was supposed to invert, at $1.18 cheaper per instance — coordinating by event-based message passing, no shared pool [T1] (Adaptive Multi-Agent Scaffolding).

The shared-context counterexample. DeLM shares context and also wins: 65.7% avg@1 on SWE-bench Verified against 56.4% and 54.7%, at $0.12/task vs $0.25–0.26 [T1] (Decentralized Multi-Agent Systems with Shared Context). The store holds only verified, ~100-token gists written directly rather than routed, and its ablation puts a number on the discriminating property: remove admission-time verification and it drops 4.9 points.

Three T1s, same task class, and the shared-vs-private axis cannot separate the winners from the loser. That is what contested means here, and it has not changed.

OPEN TENSION: two candidate reconciliations, neither tested

(A) The substrate hypothesis. The tax is not coordination but raw, unvalidated state moving through a serializing router. Both winners avoid it: icat-agent sends small validated messages peer-to-peer; DeLM shares a pool of small units validated at admission with no router. It retro-explains the sweep's internal gradient — its worst performer, Independent at −70.0% and 17.2× amplification, is precisely the one that never validates the pool — and why git branch-and-merge in isolated worktrees was the critical mechanism for CAID (+26.7% PaperBench, +14.3% Commit0) [T1] (Async SWE Agents): a worktree is event-passing with a filesystem for a mailbox, and the merge gate is the validation.

(B) The regime hypothesis. The sweep measures intra-task coordination; production fleets are inter-ticket, parallelizing units a human already decomposed — the decomposable regime where the sweep's own theory says coordination should help. It rescues the production numbers without explaining icat-agent, which is intra-task, SWE-shaped, and wins anyway.

Unresolved. (A) explains more of the evidence with fewer moving parts, and everything below tilts it further, but nothing has isolated a single one of its three components. If (A) holds, coordinate freely but never through a raw shared window. If (B) holds and (A) fails, fleets pay off because a human did the decomposition. If neither holds, the production throughput numbers are counts of work a single strong agent would have done better serially — unchecked, because none of that evidence has a quality criterion (evaluation-integrity).

The counterfactual ran, and serial ties

This is the update that should change what you build. On 2026-07-25 this map named the arm nobody had run: take the admission discipline hypothesis (A) credits, and apply it with one strong agent, serially. A confirmatory study ran it as a fourth arm on 30 frozen feature pairs (15 conflict / 15 clean), four arms, three coder seeds, 360 executions, with the 60 planner declarations generated once under a freeze seed and reused across every arm and seed so planner variance cannot load onto the policy contrast [T1] (Claim Plane).

Static admission is a large win over unconstrained parallel: pair pass 23.3% → 50.0% (+26.7 pp, 95% CI [9.6, 60.0]), integration 65.6% → 96.7%, conflict stratum 6.7% → 60.0%. Always serial: 50.0%, 96.7%, 60.0% — identical within every cluster, and faster (181.4 s against 188.0 s on the modelled critical path). The reason is visible in the mechanism: effective serialization under static admission is 87/90 (96.7%), including 42/45 (93.3%) of the clean executions. Conflict sensitivity 100%, clean specificity 6.7%. The admission gate was, in practice, a serializer.

The selective dynamic policy — the one that classifies well enough to keep the concurrency — classified far better (66.7% of conflict serialized, 13.3% of clean) and lost anyway: 46 of 90 executions failed closed on undeclared scope, pair pass 22.2%, below unconstrained parallel. And the failure is a runtime design gap rather than a classification gap: 45 of 46 blocks were aimed at a file already present in the frozen declaration but outside its admitted regions, 15 of them within ten lines, with zero promotions formally rejected because there was no bounded amendment state between "contingent" and "refuse."

What this does and does not settle. The mechanism claim survives intact — validated-at-admission state beats raw shared state, and the vault has three independent instances of it now. What does not survive is the implicit corollary that the fleet is what buys the reliability. On this task class it was the discipline, and the concurrency it protected returned nothing measurable. Before you build a fleet, run the serial arm: it is one configuration, it is cheaper, and if it ties you have your answer. The open question this hands forward is where the tie breaks — task classes with genuine parallel headroom, or a runtime with a bounded amendment state that lets a well-classified dynamic policy keep its concurrency instead of failing closed.

The loop is a scored object, and it is a bigger term than the model tier

Everything on this page compares loops informally, because until 2026-08-07 nothing scored one. A benchmark now does, and it keeps completed work live — once a development unit's tests pass they stay enforced for the rest of the run, so the score has a term for breaking what you already finished [T1] (LoopsBench). 112 tasks from PR sequences, course labs and research evolutions, admitted only above 2.5 months of temporal span and 1,200 units of solution scale, with the implementation path stripped from recovered instructions and per-unit tests admitted under a solvability / non-triviality / discriminativeness check.

The headline for loop engineering: with the model fixed, sweeping the loop implementation moves resolve rate 18.75% → 7.14% — a spread comparable to or larger than the model-tier gaps this literature argues about — while the best configuration reaches 25.00%. Regression events persist across all four loop profiles despite an 8× spread in context renewal, and compaction sensitivity is measured directly (moving the trigger budget 0.75× / default / 1.25× shifts rounds by implementation). Two consequences: pin your loop the way you pin your model, and stop reading a resolve-rate delta as a model result when the loop was not held fixed.

The routing term, isolated for the first time

Remove the workers entirely: the planner emits only an assignment plus an explicit information transfer per cross-agent edge, and a deterministic simulator executes it — same plan, same result, every time. Across 240 DAGs at 10–100 subtasks, agent count's correlation with quality falls to −0.021 at n = 100 while transfer coverage holds 0.614–0.952 at every scale, and average missing transfers per plan widen from a [0.00, 0.43] range across models to [0.07, 22.70] [T1, strong inside the simulator] (OrchBench). At extreme scale the behaviour is divergence, not degradation: from 500 to 1,000 subtasks two frontier models fall from 0.981 coverage to 0.441 and 0.398 while a third holds complete coverage (transfer-coverage-beats-agent-count-in-orchestration).

Two things follow. The 2026-07-10 saturation finding gains a mechanism: raising the agent cap from 16 to 64 more than doubles the agents and leaves the score flat, because each added agent adds cross-agent edges and each edge is a handoff that can go undeclared. Parallelism saturates at the rate coordination failures accumulate, not at the rate tasks run out of independence. And at 1.3% of the tokens and 10.3% of the wall-clock of real execution, correlating with real Claude Code quality at r = 0.816, this is the first affordable way to run the decisive three-armed sweep with σ_eval zero by construction (simulate-the-plan-to-price-a-coordination-question) — which is also what makes the tripled cost of several implementations per arm affordable rather than prohibitive.

Bounds that matter, and they are large: no worker ever runs, so nothing here says a fleet beats a single agent; the sim-to-real correlation rests on six model-level points and one leave-one-out configuration collapses it to r = 0.421, p = 0.50; and the authors' own ablation shows the benchmark's discriminative power is almost entirely the missing-transfer penalty (a 0.247 quality gap becomes 0.011 without it), so this measures declared information routing under an assumed loss model and very little else. Task decomposition is a fixed input, and 34.3% of sampled generated DAGs were rejected on semantic faithfulness.

The two production instances that tilt (A) without settling it

A million lines, and validation that costs no model call. Two completed migrations [T2] (How Anthropic runs large-scale code migrations): 1M LOC Zig→Rust in under two weeks — 100% of the existing suite green pre-merge, 19 post-merge regressions, 5.9B input / 690M output tokens ≈ $165k — and 165,000 lines Python→TypeScript in one weekend across hundreds of agents and 8 phase gates. The shared substrate is the filesystem, the admission check is the compiler and the test suite, the queue is derived from disk state, and no orchestrator routes peer discoveries. Where DeLM paid an LLM to verify gists and lost 4.9 points without it, a compile error is admission-time verification at zero model calls. The one deliberate serialization is a build daemon batching patches — serialize the mechanical operation, never the knowledge.

Three practices travel with it and are worth more than the headline: fix the loop, not the file (on a recurring error, amend the rulebook and regenerate the batch — a third answer to the repair question alongside iterate and escalate, and the only lever that scales with throughput); build the judge first and validate it in both directions (it must pass the original and fail code you broke on purpose); and stress-test the contract before spending on execution — migrate three files, review adversarially, throw the output away and keep the refined rules (fix-the-loop-not-the-artifact).

The largest same-task substrate comparison anyone has published [T2] (Agent swarms and the new model economics). One task (SQLite from the 835-page manual), hundreds of agents, two substrates. The old one is (A)'s worst case built out: finest possible unit (a git commit), no admission check, no hierarchy — 68,000 commits in two hours, 70,000+ merge conflicts accelerating, a hottest file with 7,771 conflicts across 1,173 agents, 54 crates containing three parallel SQL implementations, ending at 64,305 lines. The new one coarsens the unit (~1,000 commits in the same window, ~68× fewer), adds compile-checked shared design docs as the admission gate — the second production system in six days whose validation costs no model call — and adds planner/worker routing: <1,000 conflicts over four hours, hottest file 47, 9 crates, 9,908 lines. Four-hour score 73–85% against 11–77%; every new configuration eventually passed 100% of the suite at roughly one seventh the code. Two mechanisms sit outside the hypothesis's vocabulary and deserve naming: neutral third-party agents resolve merge collisions, and an agent-curated field guide is injected into successors at startup (hierarchical-swarm-beats-flat-swarm-on-the-same-task). One infrastructure consequence: the substrate needed a purpose-built VCS.

Why neither settles it. Both are vendor accounts of their own products. The migration has no control arm — nobody ran the same rulebook and suite through one strong agent serially, or through the fleet with the compiler gate removed — on a task class that hands you three oracles for free. The swarm moved five mechanisms together with no isolating arm, one run per configuration, no paired noise floor, a test suite the vendor chose as the only quality criterion with "we have not done a deeper manual analysis" stated outright, and a losing arm its own authors had already decided to replace. They tilt (A). They do not test it, and the three-armed sweep is more valuable for their existence, not less.

The production accounts, unchanged and still ungraded

OpenAI's Symphony makes the ticket board the control plane — one agent per open ticket in an isolated workspace, an always-on orchestrator restarting stalled runs, PRs shepherded through CI. Landed PRs rose 500% in three weeks on some teams [T2] (Symphony). Cursor reports 40%+ of internal monorepo PRs from cloud agents at 50M+ actions/day [T2] (Cloud agent lessons). Both name the binding constraint as human attention: engineers cap out steering 3–5 interactive sessions. Note what that evidence is — a landed-PR count and a share of PRs. Neither is a quality measure.

Two smaller notes from the same limb. Flat delegation beat both worker-recursion and nested self-recursion (61.6 vs 57.6 / 57.9 mean partial) on a substrate whose shared state is the machine itself, offered by its authors as a design choice rather than a claim about hierarchy [T1, computer-use] (StateAct). And before you tune any loop's stopping rule, check how much of it is recovery from your own harness's stale state: between 4.9% and 37.3% of reasoning cycles disappear when the trajectory is synchronized with the repository rather than storing snapshots [T1] (CORVUS) — iterations spent re-reading files already in context, or recovering from edits targeting text that had already changed (re-derive-file-state-instead-of-storing-snapshots).

Why the academic deltas still are not bankable

Contamination. Cursor audited 731 frontier-agent trajectories and found 63% of successful SWE-bench Pro resolutions retrieved the fix rather than derived it — 57% via web lookup, 9% via git-history mining — with history-isolation and egress-proxying costing 14–21 points [T2] (Reward hacking is swamping model intelligence gains). icat-agent's signature behaviour on under-specified issues is to explore harder before patching, which is also a precise description of a better retriever, and it reports no egress constraint and no .git strip.

No measured noise floor. A paired protocol made two coordination mechanisms provably inert and they still disagreed by up to 18 pp on the same model and benchmark; the authors' own strongest single-seed result evaporated at the second seed, and they print the retraction. Detecting a 15 pp effect needs n≈110 paired; 5 pp needs n≈1,003 [T1] (A Paired Noise-Floor Protocol). It does not refute either counterexample — the floor is local — but nobody has measured a floor on the benchmarks where +8.3 and +9.3–10.5 point wins were scored.

Variance you did not budget for. A variance-components decomposition of agentic training found evaluation variance a measured zero, the training seed worth ≤10%, and the data draw dominant at 48% on the hardest cell, its share rising as effects become marginal [T3] (Teach it to stop). Its corollary: estimating variance from subsamples of one batch halves it and erases the catastrophic-failure mode entirely. Add the implementation-lottery result from Part one and there are now three separate variance terms — data draw, implementation choice, starting state — each larger than the seed, and none of them controlled in any comparison on this page.

The cost results survive all of this — $1.18/instance, half-price-per-task, and $165k for a million lines are real numbers whatever the resolution rates measure. The capability results do not.

The thread through both limbs: buy validation that costs no model call

The strongest single pattern across fourteen new entries is not architectural. It is that the checks that work are mechanical, and mechanical checks are cheap enough to run on every increment:

  • Compiler + test suite as a fleet's admission gate, at hundreds of concurrent workers [T2].
  • Compile-checked design docs, so contention surfaces as a build error rather than a merge conflict [T2].
  • A visible test gate in the stopping policy: prevents 248 false completions and introduces none, taking risk 29.5% → 2.4%, where the strictest freshness policy reaches zero only at 38.4% coverage and loses 311 sound completions [T1].
  • Adversarial mutants of the current patch as an admission criterion, at 9.5% of run cost, worth +8.0 Resolved over a same-backbone cogeneration baseline — necessary because a test and a fix emitted in one trajectory produce correlated errors (coupling factor 1.87), so the in-loop gate passes exactly when it should not [T1] (Beyond Fail-to-Pass, cogenerated-tests-and-fixes-fail-together).
  • Emitting the check before the artifact, worth +7.0 Resolved on its own, because a criterion written after the work anchors to the work [T1].

Every one of these is the same primitive: validation the loop can afford to run on every increment because no model is in the path. It is also the only place in this article where the evidence is consistent across academic and production sources.

And check what your containment experiment is handing the component

One more instrument, cheap and newly demonstrated. A model router credited with containing latent cascades showed recovery 0.83, a ~5.5× cut in cascade radius at depth 7, and an effect that grew with pipeline depth — the shape a real mechanism has. Re-run with the trusted upstream value withheld and the same licence to self-correct intact, recovery collapses 0.67 → 0.08, baseline level (paired drop 0.58 [0.33, 0.79], exact sign-flip permutation p = 5.19×10⁻⁴, n=24 pairs) [T1, single-study] (OrchestraBench). The router was never detecting the fault; it was repairing one the harness had already localized for it.

Generalize it before you next measure a monitor: ask what else in your setup already knows. A verifier handed the reference solution, a repair loop given the failing test's name, an attribution agent scored on traces where the injected step is the only anomaly — each will perform, and the performance belongs to the harness. What survives here is the useful half: the oracle arm still contains fully, so producing the signal is the open problem, not consuming it.

The same study supplies a cheap dispatch finding worth acting on directly. A router matching complexity flags and keywords scores 0% on cases where the flags decouple from intent — below a zero-signal baseline's 20%, because misleading metadata is worse than none — while a no-model TF-IDF router that merely reads the task description clears all of them (100%). The line is what the router reads, not how much intelligence it brings: if your orchestrator dispatches on tags, complexity scores or an upstream classifier's label, giving the existing router the request text is the cheapest improvement available. Caveat it properly — 26 author-labelled cases, no second annotator, and the diagnostic saturates for a capable model, so it isolates when keyword routing fails rather than grading difficulty.

Part five: the practice, with a denominator — and what it is missing

Every number above this line came from a study that built a loop and measured it. As of 2026-08-22 there is one that went looking for loops instead. It scanned 36,710 repositories already classified as engineered software projects — a frame defined without reference to loops, so it can carry a rate — and hand-inspected every one of the 256 its heuristics matched. 217 run confirmed autonomous agent loops, 0.59% of the corpus, at heuristic precision 0.868 [0.820, 0.907] [T1]. (Lulla, Nersesyan, Mohsenimofidi, Treude & Baltes, arXiv 2608.21884.)

That number is a floor, not an estimate, and the reason matters more than the number. Recall here is unmeasurable by construction — estimating false negatives would require repositories known not to run loops, and no such set exists. A loop driven by a tool-native scheduler, a local cron entry, or private infrastructure leaves no committed trace and is invisible. So read 0.59% as "committed-trace loops in public GitHub," and read everything below as a statement about what people version, not about what they run.

Two of those zeros are less damning than they look, and the study says which. Parallel isolation is genuinely untraceable — worktrees are local and never pushed, and the 3,507 repositories with agent-prefixed branches reflect ordinary agent use rather than loop isolation. And the empty goal row is partly a fixed-phrase artifact: manual annotation found a stop-condition mechanism somewhere in 55 of the 256 inspected repositories, mostly as terminal states or retry caps written into committed prompts, which a phrase matcher will not see.

The rest stand. And they land hardest on the one component this article has argued is load-bearing since it was written: the verifier. Zero repositories commit a verifier subagent referenced by a loop artifact. The maker/checker separation that the discourse calls the reason you can walk away — and that Part two spent a section decomposing into four distinct properties — has, in public, no artifact to inspect. Which is also why "verifier theater," the failure mode the practitioner literature names most loudly, is undetectable from outside: there is nothing to read.

The behavioral evidence agrees, independently. Of 1,334 plausible state files with at least five commits across 637 repositories, none shows the append-only run-log shape the discourse prescribes, and the cadence and authorship signals co-occur on exactly one file in the entire corpus. Since loops demonstrably run in these repositories, their state is either absent from version control or in files the candidate names do not cover — and the study is explicit that it cannot separate those two.

The shape of what is running is not what this article assumed either. Among the 217 confirmed: 21 run on a schedule only, 180 on repository events only — mostly automatic pull-request review — and 15 on both. The scheduled nightly loop that most of this page is about is roughly a sixth of the practice. The dominant form is an event-triggered reviewer, which routes the practice's centre of gravity into agentic-code-review rather than here.

Two method lessons worth more than the census

A green scheduled run is not evidence that an agent ran. One repository's hourly triage workflow logged 6,290 successful runs without a single agent execution, because its gating issue search matched nothing. The authors had to amend their codebook mid-study: a successful run counts only with a step duration long enough for real work, or with produced output. If you monitor your own loops on exit code, you are measuring the same nothing.

Fast automatic review is often a personal setting, invisible to the repository. In 95 of 159 repositories with measurable review timing, reviews arrived within minutes and clustered on individual authors' pull requests — the signature of an account-level standing auto-request. The study refuses to count it as project adoption, and any internal metric of "our repo has automated review" should apply the same test.

What the discourse agrees on, and how much that is worth

The same paper reviews twelve practitioner sources systematically and — this is the part that earns it a place on a page about evidence — discloses its own correlation structure. At least three of the twelve build explicitly on one origin author's posts, so the apparent convergence on building blocks partly reflects a single lineage rather than replication. Read the building-block consensus as one well-articulated design with adherents, not as convergent evidence [T1, suggestive].

The benefit claims are worse off. The two figures in circulation — roughly 1,300 agent-written pull requests per week at one company, 423 Firefox security fixes in a month — have no published study or dataset behind them, and the discourse's own synthesis note says the circulating numbers "should be treated as rough reference." Meanwhile the failure catalogue is documented in detail: runaway cost (~8M tokens in 48 hours from an over-eager CI-fixing loop), infinite fix loops, verifier theater, notification fatigue, drift between a loop's committed instructions and its state, and two named second-order risks — comprehension debt (the gap between what is in the repository and what anyone understands grows with loop velocity) and cognitive surrender.

The principle that survives all of it, and that this article should have stated three refreshes ago:

Raise autonomy only as far as you can check the result at acceptable cost.

Verification cost, not model capability, is what bounds delegation. It also separates two things a single "autonomy level" conflates — agency (how far one agent proceeds without intervention) and orchestration (how many run at once and who coordinates them) — which are independent axes and are chosen per task, not once per developer.

And the bottleneck named repeatedly in the discourse is not the agent: review capacity. Nightly runs open more pull requests than teams can review, so volume itself defeats the human guardrail the loop depends on. Review fatigue turns the gate into a rubber stamp "while the pipeline still reports green," and "the loops that stick are the ones where somebody was already paid to read the output." That is the verification-cost bound seen from the human side, and it now has a number attached to it from the other direction: 248,641 agent-authored pull requests received at least one AI-attributed review, with a 1.2-minute median from open to first review cross-product [T1]. (Selvanayagam & Ghaleb, arXiv 2608.21311.) The gate is being closed faster than a human could open the diff, and nothing in that dataset says a defect is more likely to be caught.

One more thing your loop is quietly losing

If your loop runs long enough to compact its context — and any unattended one does — the rules you gave it are not durable. Under the production compaction prompt, which explicitly orders safety rules kept verbatim, constraint recall is 0.53 after one round and 0.10 after five, and the decay reproduces across four LLM compactor families and every structural baseline [T1]. (Zerhoudi, Mitrović & Granitzer, CIKM 2026, arXiv 2608.22752.) The mechanism is that compaction is type-blind: a constraint and a debugging trace are summarized at the same rate, and only the constraint needed exact wording.

The fix is a decision made earlier, not a better prompt — classify items by type before compressing, pin the constraints, and run a deterministic presence check over the output. That last part is not optional: on configurations too dense to compress safely, the same pipeline without a verifier reported apparent 1.00 recall while silently dropping a mean 57% of the constraints it was told to keep. A transform cannot be its own receipt, which is Part two's independence argument arriving in the context pipeline.

What to actually do

  • Commit the stop condition, the verifier, the budget and the run log — or accept that your loop cannot be audited. [T1] Across 36,645 public repositories with 217 confirmed autonomous loops, triggers are committed in 253 and stop conditions, verifiers, budgets and cost logs in zero. A loop whose trigger is in git and whose governing artifacts live in a vendor UI cannot be reviewed, diffed, or reproduced by anyone including you in six months.
  • Set autonomy by what checking costs you, not by what the agent can do. [T1, suggestive] The one principle twelve practitioner sources converge on. Treat agency (how far one agent runs unattended) and orchestration (how many run at once) as separate dials, chosen per task.
  • Do not treat a green scheduled run as evidence of work. [T1] One repository logged 6,290 successful hourly runs with zero agent executions because its gating search matched nothing. Assert on produced output or on a step duration long enough for real work.
  • Re-present your rules at the decision they govern, and check the compacted context for them verbatim. [T1] Safety-rule recall under production compaction is 0.53 after one round and 0.10 after five, and the compactor's own fidelity report is worthless — without a separate deterministic verifier the same runs reported apparent 1.00 recall over a mean 57% silent loss.
  • Ask which tier your failure is in before you build a loop for it. [T1, single-study] Faults that raise are recovered ~1.0; silent semantic faults are recovered ~0.0 at every capability tier, and retry makes them worse by reproducing the fault while lengthening time to detection. Spend on detection and attribution for those, not on more rounds.
  • Run the serial arm before you build the fleet. [T1] Static admission and always-serial tie inside every cluster on this task class — same pair pass, same integration, same conflict stratum, and serial is faster. The discipline is what pays; the concurrency it protects has to earn its own line.
  • Pin your loop implementation like you pin your model. [T1] At a fixed model, swapping loop implementations moves resolve rate 18.75% → 7.14%. A resolve-rate delta measured without holding the loop fixed is not a model result.
  • Ask what your feedback measures, not just how noisy it is. [T1] With a perfectly reliable verifier grading the wrong quantity, iterative repair raises pass rate and lowers fault-catching power (87.54 → 92.77 the wrong way) while every reported number climbs. Grade the loop on the objective — kill rate, mutation survivors — or don't iterate.
  • Grade the reference before it drives the repair. [T1] Estimating reference reliability without labels cuts feedback emitted on already-correct code from 113.2 → 14.0 problems, upstream of any stop rule.
  • Put a small monitor on the trajectory and restart with the diff, not the story. [T1] A 0.6B classifier reclaims 20.4% of execution tokens at a 5% false-positive budget and transfers unchanged across policies including closed-weight ones; scaling it to 4B loses recall. On restart, carry the aborted run's diff as a removable overlay defaulted off and framed as an unverified lead (+5.2) rather than cold-resampling (+0.2) or replaying the narrative, which anchors.
  • Withhold the hint before you believe your containment number. [T1, single-study] Keep the component and its licence to act; remove the privileged fact the harness was feeding it. On the one system that ran it, containment fell 0.67 → 0.08.
  • Route on the request, not on flags attached to it. [T1, single-study] Keyword/flag routing scores 0% where flags decouple from intent, below a no-signal baseline; a no-model router that reads the description clears every such case.
  • Run the placebo arm before you credit your loop. [T1, ≤7B] The arm is the treatment with the payload blanked. If feedback does not beat a blank retry at the same budget, you are paying 2.5–5.5× the tokens for an extra sample you could have taken directly.
  • Check your resampling headroom first. [T1] Best-of-3 buys +0.10 to +0.21 on mid-tier agents and +0.007 at the top. If your generator is mid-tier, that is the number your loop has to beat; if it is saturated, no re-drawing technique has room to work.
  • Separate the four properties of "independent" before shopping for a verifier. [T1 ×4, reconciliation] Anchor present (original instruction, test suite, program state) → buy cheap independent weights, and a smaller model is fine. Anchor absent → you are buying capability, and a weaker reviewer is a measured net loss.
  • Measure error dependence at your operating point, not from a vendor diff. [T1] The cross-family verifier was the worst of three at matched coverage (0.240 vs 0.041/0.050), and every pair looked dependent once coverage loosened to 75%.
  • Run your reviewer over drafts you know are correct and count the regressions. [T1] Trivially cheap, and the only way to see a gate whose resolution rate looks fine while it rewrites correct work.
  • Report current correctness, not only ever-correct. [T1] Pass@k rises across exactly the interval where the submittable state degrades; 16.0% of trajectories produced a correct patch and lost it.
  • Stop on the state, not the budget. [T1] Continuation has negative expected value once q > α/(α+β); stopping on the sign of the marginal gain is worth +60.6 pp at 0.72 rounds instead of 5. The fixed iteration budget in every harness is a category error, not a tuning error.
  • Bind evidence to the state it describes. [T1] Carry it as (source-hash, suite-hash, payload) and act on it only on a match — stale-but-true evidence harms 34/135 correct starts against 4/135. Take the free half today: a fail-closed parser that maps an unparseable action to "keep."
  • Gate the revise action on machine-checkable predicates, and treat the trigger as the thing to tune. [T1, planning domain] The gated loop entered the model on 4.3% of turns; its per-board effects still spanned +0.140 to −0.148 F1 because the firing condition, not the repair, was miscalibrated.
  • Make discard-and-resample an available recovery action, and attribute before you rerun. [T1] Reflect is the worst fixed policy at 27.5%; attribute-then-rerun beats symptom repair 2–3× with a single rerun. Roughly 30% of failures are reached by no in-loop action at all.
  • Carry session state, and re-derive it before anything irreversible. [T1] Pinning eliminates drift and amplifies wrong actions 3.0× — worst on the strongest model, 16.6× in dependent chains.
  • Never stop on the agent's own declaration. [T1] Self-report-only acceptance covers 99.8% at 29.5% risk, and failed trajectories cite the sections they violated.
  • Buy validation from a compiler, type checker, test suite or mutant set wherever one exists. [T1, T2] Zero model calls, affordable on every increment. Where no mechanical oracle exists, build one and validate it in both directions before you spend on the loop it governs. And emit the check before the artifact — worth +7.0 Resolved on its own.
  • Don't route raw, unvalidated worker state through a serializing pool. [T1, hypothesis (A) — tilted twice, still untested] A shared store is fine if what enters it is verified and compact; a raw common window that every worker floods and nobody checks is the thing associated with 39–70% degradation.
  • Count declared handoffs, not agents. [T1, in simulation] Transfer coverage predicts plan quality at every scale; agent count correlates −0.021 at 100 subtasks, and 16 → 64 agents leaves the score flat.
  • Check how much of your loop is recovering from your own harness. [T1] Between 4.9% and 37.3% of reasoning cycles vanish when the trajectory is synchronized with the repo instead of snapshotted.
  • Still ask whether the task decomposes. [T1] The −39% to −70% degradation on strict-ordering planning is real, and has neither been shown to survive a validated substrate nor to vanish under one.
  • Stop using the ~45% saturation threshold as a design rule. [T1, retracted] Coordinators have won from 59.1% twice.
  • Do not trust a single-configuration delta to justify an architecture — yours or anyone's here. [T1, T2] Between contamination, an unmeasured noise floor, a variance budget dominated by which data you drew, and an implementation-choice term 5–10× the rerun term, a 5–10 point win is not yet evidence.

Where this connects

Worker isolation is context engineering by another name, and the pinned-versus-re-derived variable now runs through files, session state and verdicts alike (context-engineering). The adaptive-scaffold result — same weights, +8.3 points, structure alone — and the gated revise action are the harness thesis one layer down; where a mechanical operation exists, take the model out of the path (agent-harness-architecture). The verifier-property split is shared with agentic-code-review, which owns the reviewer side of it. Symphony's always-on daemon and the migration's disk-derived work queue are where loop engineering meets unattended-operation; the merge, compile and mutant gates ride the substrate in ci-cd-for-agents. The stopping decision is simultaneously a correctness control and a cost control (model-routing-token-economics). And because the pro-coordination case is scored entirely on proxies the vault distrusts — and because the null, the noise floor and the implementation draw all live there — this map is coupled to evaluation-integrity more tightly than to anything else.

Open and contested

  • Nobody has measured whether loops help, on either side. (Added 2026-08-25.) This is the gap the census makes impossible to ignore: the discourse's benefit claims are self-reports, the skeptics' cost claims are anecdotes, and the controlled experiment that would settle it — the same maintenance tasks run under interactive use, goal-driven runs, and scheduled loops — is proposed and unrun. Everything on this page about how to build a loop sits on top of an unanswered question about whether to.

  • Is loop state absent, or just somewhere nobody grepped? (Added 2026-08-25.) The census found zero valid loop state files and zero append-only run logs across 1,334 candidates in 637 repositories, while confirming that loops demonstrably run in those repositories. Those two facts are compatible with "the discipline is not practiced" and with "the discipline is practiced outside version control," and the study says plainly that its signals cannot separate them. The cheap resolution is a survey of loop operators rather than a better file-name heuristic.

  • Does the practice survive its own tooling? (Added 2026-08-25.) One strand of the discourse predicts loop engineering dissolves into the harness — that /loop and /goal shipping as built-in commands already absorb what the hand-rolled shell loop worked around, leaving explicit loop design as a concern only at the infrastructure layer. If that is right, the committed-artifact deficit measured here is not underinvestment but obsolescence in progress, and the census will read very differently in a year. The pipeline is deterministic and versioned, so re-running it on later snapshots is the test.

  • The map's headline contest is unchanged, but the burden has shifted. Hypothesis (A) — raw unvalidated state through a serializing router — now has two large production instances, a simulator isolating its routing term, and one controlled study. That study found the discipline paying and the concurrency paying nothing: always-serial ties static admission within every cluster and runs faster. So the live question is no longer only "is coordination the tax" but "what does the fleet buy once you have the discipline" — and on the one task class where it has been asked, the answer was nothing.

  • Where does the serial tie break? Candidates: task classes with genuine parallel headroom rather than 30 feature pairs; and a runtime with a bounded amendment state between "contingent" and "refuse," since the dynamic policy that classified well still lost, with 45 of its 46 blocks aimed at a file already in its own frozen declaration and 15 within ten lines of an admitted region.

  • Is the three-tier fault-recovery split real outside a controlled chain? It is currently one agent simulating orchestration over a synthetic verifiable chain, n=30 per mode, with the extreme cells as point masses by construction. Wire N real agents, inject the same five modes, and check whether tool faults still recover at 1.0 and semantic faults still at 0.0 — and whether the ~30% no-recovery-action floor from the attribution study is the same population.

  • Does the proxy-oracle trap generalize past oracle generation? One task, one language, one dataset, one repair system. The mechanism — a loop optimizing the signal it can see rather than the objective it stands for — should apply anywhere the free signal and the goal come apart, which is most places. Nobody has measured a second instance.

  • The decisive experiment is three-armed, paired, frozen — and now needs replicates. Raw shared pool vs event-passing vs verified-gist, everything else fixed, against a same-model configuration-equivalent noise-floor arm, from a committed frozen round 0 — and several independent implementations per arm, because implementation choice accounts for 33–45% of variation against 4–6% for rerun and reverses the winner a quarter to nearly half the time. At 1.3% of the tokens, the simulator makes the tripled cost affordable. There is no longer a cost excuse for not running it.

  • The four-axis verifier split is a reconciliation, not a result. It reconciles four T1 claims and predicts that anchor presence sets the sign of a cheap independent checker. Nobody has run the grid that would confirm or kill it: vary anchor presence × verifier capability × weight independence, and measure error correlation at a fixed operating point.

  • Does the placebo result survive a frontier agent with an executable oracle? The anchoring penalty is predicted by baseline pass@1 at r = 0.96 and is already non-significant at 7B, so it may vanish exactly where production loops live — or the informational payload may stay at zero while the penalty goes. Those are opposite conclusions and one cheap arm separates them.

  • How much of the review-loop literature's inverse-to-strength lift is resampling? The +29.4 pp weak / +3.2 pp strong shape has a pure-resampling arm of the same shape and order sitting underneath it, unmeasured on the same models.

  • Does the loop-destroys-work result survive a real fail-to-pass suite as the verifier? The non-absorption measurement is HumanEval-scale function repair on 27 tasks selected for repairability, with one of six pre-registered contrasts surviving correction. Take a frontier coding agent, an executable oracle, and measure the sign of the marginal gain per round: does the oracle raise discrimination far enough that unbounded iteration is safe, or does it only move the optimum outward?

  • Does the sole-blocker transfer boundary hold for code repair? If it does, every "our repair loop fixes X% of failures" is an upper bound with an unstated discount, and the ~30% of failures no recovery action reaches sets the ceiling.

  • Is the SWE-bench win a retrieval artifact? Re-run under history-isolation and egress-proxying; neither counterexample has been floored.

  • DeLM vs icat-agent is itself an unrun comparison — near-duals in design, never on the same field.

  • All production evidence remains output-count grade. The reconcile step's own failure modes — bad merges, forwarded-but-stale state — are named and unquantified, and nobody has measured spec → tickets, where fleets get their work.
