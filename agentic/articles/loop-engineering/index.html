Loop engineering: coordination isn't the tax — routing raw, unvalidated state is

Refreshed 2026-07-25. Two changes since the last version. First, this article had one subject and now has two: everything below the fold used to be about coordination between agents, and between 2026-07-22 and 2026-07-25 four results opened a second limb — the verify-repair loop inside one agent — which turns out to be governed by the same variable. Second, the substrate hypothesis this piece has been defending since 2026-07-11 gained its largest production instance and its most awkward gap in the same source: a fleet of hundreds of agents translating a million lines of code on a substrate whose admission check is a compiler, reported without a control arm. The headline survives. The confidence does not improve as much as the scale suggests.

Prior history, kept because this piece has been wrong twice: in early July it argued long-horizon work wants a coordinator plus isolated workers, and a controlled scaling study made that contested. The 2026-07-10 rewrite swung to "coordinate decomposable work, not long work" with a ~45% saturation rule, and a fleet that coordinates tool-heavy SWE from a 59.1% baseline killed the rule. The 2026-07-11 rewrite said "the tax is shared context, never share a window," and a shared-context fleet won anyway.

As of 2026-07, the honest summary: nobody has isolated coordination as the thing that hurts. What survives is one variable that turns out to govern loops at two scales. Between agents, the architectures that degrade share a substrate — raw, unvalidated worker state serialized through a central router — and the architectures that win move small, validated units and skip the router. Inside one agent, a repair loop is worth running only when the verifier discriminates; below that threshold, iterating is worth less than not iterating.

The through-line, now stated once for both limbs: an unvalidated loop is negative value. Whether the loop passes state between workers or rewrites a candidate in place, the load-bearing component is the check, not the schedule and not the iteration count.

Part one: coordination between agents

Three T1 results, and no two read the way they were written

The sweep. Google Research and DeepMind ran 260 configurations across six agentic benchmarks, five architectures (Single-Agent, Independent, Centralized, Decentralized, Hybrid), and three model families, with tools, prompt structure, and token budgets standardized [T1] (Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems). Coordination's sign flips with task structure: +80.9% for centralized coordination on decomposable financial reasoning, −39.0% to −70.0% for every multi-agent topology on strict-ordering planning. Tool-heavy work is penalized worst — the efficiency–tools interaction (β = −0.330, p < 0.001) is the dominant inhibitor, its own worst case a 16-tool software-engineering task. Independent agents amplify errors 17.2×; a validating central orchestrator contains it to 4.4×. Read plainly: do not build a fleet for software engineering, especially on a good model.

The event-passing counterexample. icat-agent does exactly that and wins [T1] (Adaptive Multi-Agent Scaffolding). On SWE-bench Pro it resolves 67.4% against 59.10% for mini-SWE-agent with the model held fixed (GPT-5.4-xhigh) — +8.3 points, on tool-heavy SWE, from a baseline fourteen points above the saturation threshold where coordination was supposed to invert, at $1.18 cheaper per instance. Its distinguishing move: event-based message passing instead of a shared context pool — nothing is shared, things are sent — plus a rubric that grades the issue and picks the topology per task.

The shared-context counterexample. DeLM shares context and also wins [T1] (Decentralized Multi-Agent Systems with Shared Context). A decentralized fleet coordinating through an append-only shared store hits 65.7% avg@1 on SWE-bench Verified vs 56.4% (AOrchestra-Parallel) and 54.7% (mini-SWE-agent), at $0.12/task vs $0.25–0.26 — up to +10.5 points at half the cost. The catch that makes it matter: the store holds only verified, ~100-token gists, written directly rather than routed through an orchestrator, and its ablation puts a number on the discriminating property — remove admission-time verification and it drops 4.9 points.

Three T1s, same task class, and the shared-vs-private axis this article organized itself around cannot separate the winners from the loser.

The mechanism, refined: unit size × validation × routing

icat-agent's messages and DeLM's gists are near-duals — small, validated units of state transfer — and one shares while the other doesn't. So "sharing" is the wrong cut. Line the systems up by what they move and how:

  • The sweep's degrading architectures move raw worker state (full observations, tool dumps) through a serializing central pool that nobody validates. Its worst performer — Independent, −70.0%, 17.2× error amplification — is precisely the one that never validates the pool.
  • icat-agent sends validated messages peer-to-peer: small units, no shared pool, no router.
  • DeLM shares a pool, but only verified ~100-token gists written directly: small units, validated at admission, no router. It documents the three failure modes of the router it removed — serialized integration, diluted communication, and discoveries "softened in the router's rewrite."

The unifying variable is not topology and not sharing. It is raw, unvalidated state moving through a serializing router, and both winners avoid it on all three counts. This retro-explains a result the vault filed as a curiosity: CAID found git branch-and-merge in isolated worktrees was the critical coordination mechanism (+26.7% PaperBench, +14.3% Commit0) [T1] (Async SWE Agents) — a worktree is small validated units (commits) passed through a mailbox (the filesystem) with the merge as the validation gate. Never mainly about version control.

New, 2026-07-25: the mechanism's largest instance buys validation for free

Anthropic published two completed migrations run this way [T2] (How Anthropic runs large-scale code migrations): one million lines of Zig translated to Rust in under two weeks — 100% of the existing suite green before merge, 19 post-merge regressions, 5.9B input and 690M output tokens for roughly $165k, the resulting binary 19% smaller with peak memory of 609 MB against 6,745 MB — and 165,000 lines of Python ported to TypeScript in one weekend across hundreds of agents and 8 phase gates.

Read against the mechanism, this is the validated arm in its purest available form. The shared substrate is the filesystem. The admission check is the compiler and the test suite. No orchestrator rewrites or routes peer discoveries — a worker reads its unit off a queue derived from disk state and writes a file back. Where DeLM paid an LLM to verify gists at admission and lost 4.9 points without it, a compile error is admission-time verification that costs no model call at all, and it scales to hundreds of concurrent workers. The one place the design deliberately serializes is a build daemon batching patches and rebuilds so workers cannot race, which is exactly the distinction the mechanism predicts: serialize the mechanical operation, never the knowledge.

Three practices travel with it and are worth more than the headline number. Fix the loop, not the file — the stated principle is that when the same mistake recurs you amend the rulebook and regenerate the affected batch. Build the judge first, and validate it in both directions — it must pass the original code and fail code you broke on purpose, established before a line is translated. And stress-test the contract before spending on execution: migrate three files, review adversarially, throw the output away and keep the refined rules.

Why this does not close the argument. It is T2 practice with no control arm — nobody ran the same rulebook and the same suite through one strong agent serially, or through the same fleet with the compiler gate removed, so the attribution between "the fleet helped" and "the mechanical oracle helped" is unmade. The task class is also unusually generous: a language port hands you a total type checker, a working reference implementation, and a pre-existing human-written suite, none of which ordinary feature work has. The escape number needs the same care — 19 regressions with no severity breakdown, and "100% of the existing suite" is a claim about the old suite, which for most agentic PRs never executes the changed lines at all (ci-cd-for-agents). And the $165k excludes the human effort the post explicitly says to front-load into the rulebook, which is the un-priced input the whole result rests on. A migration is cheap to arm properly — the rulebook, the judge and the dependency map are reusable across arms — so the counterfactual is a compute cost, not a research programme.

Why this is not a victory lap — two independent reasons

The academic counterexamples are SWE-bench numbers, and the vault knows what that means.

Contamination. Cursor audited 731 frontier-agent trajectories and found 63% of successful SWE-bench Pro resolutions retrieved the fix rather than derived it — 57% via web lookup, 9% via git-history mining — with history-isolation and egress-proxying costing 14–21 points [T2] (Reward hacking is swamping model intelligence gains). icat-agent's signature behaviour on under-specified issues is to explore harder before patching — also a precise description of a better retriever — and it reports no egress constraint and no .git strip.

No measured noise floor. A paired protocol made two coordination mechanisms provably inert (configuration-equivalent at trial 0, verified by code inspection and a 40/40 payload audit) — and they still disagreed by up to 18 pp on the same model and benchmark [T1] (A Paired Noise-Floor Protocol). Their own strongest single-seed result (+18 pp, p=0.012) evaporated at the second seed (−3 pp, p=1.0), and they print the retraction. Detecting a 15 pp effect needs n≈110 paired; a 5 pp effect needs n≈1,003. Read carefully — it does not refute either counterexample; the authors state the floor is local and "does not transfer." What it establishes is that nobody has measured a floor on the benchmarks where +8.3 and +9.3–10.5 point wins were scored, and those magnitudes sit exactly in the band a floor of this order would swallow.

That objection got sharper on 2026-07-25, from outside the coding domain. A variance-components decomposition of agentic training — three independent data draws × eight seeds, every checkpoint re-scored — found evaluation variance to be a measured zero, the training seed worth ≤10%, and the data draw dominant at 48% on the hardest cell, its share rising as effects become marginal [T3] (Teach it to stop, not just to click). Same trainer, same data, same config, run twice: 0.18 vs 0.68. Its methodological corollary is the one to carry: estimating variance from subsamples of one batch rather than independent draws halves it and erases the catastrophic-failure mode entirely. It is computer-use agents, not coding, and its magnitudes do not transport — but the ordering of variance sources is a domain-general reason to distrust a single-configuration architectural delta, and it says the cheap version of "we checked the variance" manufactures the reassurance it reports.

The cost results survive all of this — $1.18/instance, half-price-per-task, and $165k for a million lines are real numbers whatever the resolution rates are measuring. The capability results do not.

The production accounts, unchanged and still ungraded

OpenAI's Symphony makes the ticket board the control plane — one agent per open ticket in an isolated workspace, an always-on orchestrator restarting stalled runs, dependency-ordered tickets as a parallel DAG, PRs shepherded through CI. Landed PRs rose 500% in three weeks on some teams [T2] (Symphony). Cursor reports 40%+ of internal monorepo PRs from cloud agents at 50M+ actions/day [T2] (Cloud agent lessons). Both name the binding constraint as human attention: engineers cap out steering 3–5 interactive sessions.

Note what that evidence is. Symphony's +500% is a landed-PR count on self-selected teams; Cursor's 40% is a share of PRs; Anthropic's migrations are two case studies. None is a quality measure. An architecture whose only evidence is throughput cannot defend itself against a claim that it degrades quality, because nobody is measuring quality (evaluation-integrity). The intra-task / inter-ticket distinction — that inter-ticket fleets parallelize units a human already decomposed — still rescues the production numbers, but it does not explain icat-agent or DeLM, which are intra-task, SWE-shaped, and win anyway.

Part two, new: the loop inside one agent

Everything above is coordination between agents. Four results between 2026-07-22 and 2026-07-25 measured the verify-repair loop within one agent, and found the same variable in charge.

Iterating on a candidate has an interior optimum, and past it the loop destroys correct work. With verifier and repairer both noisy, true validity peaks at round 2 and then declines while verifier acceptance keeps rising: 55% of correct plans get repaired into incorrect ones, and 24% of those damaging repairs win majority acceptance. Fixed five-round repair takes GSM8K validity from a 0.700 no-repair baseline to 0.116. An independent-resampling control at the same budget shows no such decline, so the damage is path-dependent — it comes from rewriting the same candidate, not from sampling noise. A stronger verifier reduces but does not remove it. Stopping on the sign of the marginal gain rather than on acceptance votes is worth +60.6 pp of final validity at 0.72 rounds instead of 5 [T1] (Verify, Repair, Repeat, or Stop?). Load-bearing caveat: 3–8B open-weight models in a deliberately adversarial setting, math-heavy, with code generation only in the robustness table — and in the favourable setting fixed repair slightly beats calibrated stopping, which the authors print themselves.

The same lesson holds for state the loop carries as for candidates it rewrites. An agent that resolves an entity once and then acts on it through steps that only say "it" will drift. The intuitive defence — pin the first binding and reuse it — eliminates drift to 0% while amplifying wrong actions 3.0× (bootstrap CI [2.8, 3.3]) and taking strict workflow success from 29.2% to 0.0%, measured on disjoint workflow sets so the trade cannot hide inside one number [T1] (Binding Drift in Multi-Step Tool-Augmented Agents). The amplification rises with model capability — 1.8× on the smallest backend to 8.5× on the strongest — because stronger models treat carried state as authoritative. Persistence is not validation, and removing variance is not removing error. Carry state, and re-derive it before anything irreversible.

Who checks is an active ingredient, and it costs one cheap call. Same protocol, same instruction, only the verifier's identity varied: self-verification recovers 71% of injected errors, a cross-model verifier recovers 79%, a ground-truth oracle 80% — the practical cross-verifier landing within a point of the oracle on all eight backends, and the winning verifier is smaller than what it checks, because the job is independence rather than capability. Self-reflection cannot detect a reference error, since the agent's internal state is entirely consistent with its own mistake. The honest cost is over-clarification: clarifying steps roughly triple and strict success dips 29.2% → 25.2%, so the accounting turns on reversibility. This is the same property that shows up as reviewer topology at migration scale (adversarial reviewers in separate contexts, ties escalated to a third agent) and, in a production decomposition, as a 6 → 2 collapse in rescues when observe/compare moves back onto the model that generated the artifact (agentic-code-review).

And "repair it" is the weakest available response to a failure. Given execution feedback, three recovery actions — reflect (repair from the feedback), replan (fresh cheap attempt), escalate (stronger model) — solve 27.5% / 45.3% / 68.6% as fixed policies. Reflect, the default every coding harness ships, is the worst, below naive discard-and-retry. The actions are not a ladder: 28% of recoverable failures are cheap-only, 45% escalation-only, 27% both. Roughly 30% of failures are recovered by none of them [T1] (CodeRescue) — a ceiling on what any within-agent loop can reach, however well validated.

New 2026-07-25: not every correction is installable, and a correct fix is often worth nothing. On held-out frames against a near-zero base, a single fixed stop token installs at 0.97 ± 0.06 while open-ended corrections do not — spatial coordinates at 0.53–0.71 with seed spreads of ±0.26–0.35 (one seed at exactly 0.00), an open-ended generative fill at 0.14 ± 0.04 — and the ordering is set by the action's discreteness, not by the base model's prior, since the most installable correction carries by far the lowest base log-probability. More consequentially, the verified frame-level repair reaches end-to-end task success only where the corrected action is the task's sole remaining blocker (8/20 against a base 0/15, Fisher p = 0.006) and is a flat null where it is one of several required steps (0/5 vs 0/5) [T3] (Teach it to stop). Computer-use agents, small n, carried for structure rather than magnitude — but if it transfers, then every "our repair loop fixes X% of failures" is an upper bound with an unstated discount, and the discount is the share of failures with more than one blocker.

The two limbs compose into one rule. Between agents, the coordinator's value is validation, not scheduling. Inside one agent, the loop's value is the verifier's discrimination, not the round budget. Both say: measure the check before you grant the loop a budget. And the counterweight the vault holds is the same shape — a reviewer scored against executable tests does lift a weak generator, from 27.5% to 56.9%, while barely moving a strong one (agentic-code-review) — which is why the migration practice works: with a compiler as the oracle, the marginal gain stays positive long enough for the loop to earn its budget.

What to actually do

  • Don't route raw, unvalidated worker state through a serializing pool. [T1] This — not "don't share" — is the instruction every result on both sides is consistent with. A shared store is fine if what enters it is verified and compact; private message-passing is fine; a raw common window that every worker floods and no one checks is the thing that costs you 39–70%.
  • Buy your validation from a compiler, a type checker or a test suite wherever one exists. [T2] It is admission-time verification at zero model calls, and it is what let a million-line migration run on a filesystem substrate. Where no mechanical oracle exists, build one and validate it in both directions — it must pass the original and fail code you broke on purpose — before you spend on the loop it governs.
  • Make the reconcile step verify, not merely transport. [T1] Independent workers amplify errors 17.2×; a validating orchestrator holds it to 4.4×; DeLM loses 4.9 points the moment it stops verifying at admission.
  • Move small units, not full state. [T1] ~100-token gists, distilled messages, a git commit, one translated file. Forwarding a worker's raw context is the anti-pattern.
  • Repair the generator, not the artifact. [T2] When the same mistake recurs, amend the rule and regenerate the affected batch. This is the only lever here that scales with agent throughput instead of against it, and it sidesteps the two measured failures of the alternatives — reflect is the worst fixed recovery policy at 27.5% [T1], and iterating on one candidate turns 55% of correct plans incorrect [T1].
  • Stop on the sign of the marginal gain, not on votes or a fixed round count. [T1] The fixed iteration budget in every harness is a category error, not a tuning error; when verifier discrimination approaches zero, fall back to an estimation-free stop rather than iterating.
  • Put the check on a different model, or at least in a different context. [T1] A cheap cross-model verifier reaches 79% recovery against self-checking's 71%, within a point of an oracle; moving observe/compare onto the generating model cut rescues 6 → 2 [T3]. Adversarial reviewers in separate contexts with a third-agent tiebreak is the fleet-shaped version of the same property [T2].
  • Carry session state, and re-derive it before anything irreversible. [T1] Pinning a resolved reference eliminates drift and amplifies wrong actions 3.0× — worst on the strongest model.
  • Before granting a repair loop a budget, ask whether the defect it fixes is the only blocker. [T3] A correct, verified fix bought nothing end-to-end in two of three measured cells because it was not the binding constraint.
  • Triage the ticket before you pick the topology. [T1] A vague issue wants exploration-first; a well-specified one wants parallel patch-and-validate (spec-driven-development).
  • Stop using the ~45% saturation threshold as a design rule. [T1, retracted] Coordinators have now won from 59.1% twice.
  • Still ask whether the task decomposes. [T1] The −39% to −70% degradation on strict-ordering planning is real and has not been shown to survive a validated substrate — nor shown to vanish under one.
  • Do not trust a single-configuration benchmark delta to justify your architecture — yours or anyone's here. [T1, T2] Between contamination, an unmeasured noise floor, and a variance budget dominated by which data you drew, a 5–10 point win is not yet evidence. Constrain the environment, and report the number paired against a same-model floor.

Where this connects

Worker isolation is context engineering by another name, and the substrate refinement makes it literal: the fleet's failure mode is a context failure mode, specifically the raw-unvalidated-window one (context-engineering). The adaptive-scaffold result — same weights, +8.3 points, structure alone — is the strongest statement of the harness thesis one layer down, and the migration's build daemon is its blunt production form: where a mechanical operation exists, take the model out of the path (agent-harness-architecture). Symphony's always-on daemon, and the migration's disk-derived work queue, are where loop engineering meets unattended-operation; the merge and compile gates ride the substrate in ci-cd-for-agents. The stopping decision is simultaneously a correctness control and a cost control (model-routing-token-economics), and the entire pro-coordination case is scored on proxies the vault distrusts, which is why this map is coupled to evaluation-integrity.

Open and contested

  • The largest fleet result has no counterfactual, and the cheap version costs a weekend. Nobody ran the same rulebook and suite through one strong agent serially, or through the fleet with the compiler gate removed. Until someone does, the best production evidence supports the substrate mechanism only by consistency — and a live alternative explanation is that the active ingredient is the rulebook, not the topology, since a stress-tested external contract regenerating batches would plausibly help a single agent just as much (spec-driven-development).
  • The decisive experiment is three-armed and must be run paired. Raw shared pool vs event-passing vs verified-gist, everything else fixed — plus a same-model, configuration-equivalent noise-floor arm (coordination-deltas-need-a-measured-noise-floor). Run it unpaired and a 5–10 point result means nothing. As of 2026-07-25 there is a second reason to insist: variance in agentic results is dominated by which data you drew, not by the seed, and the share grows as effects get marginal.
  • Neither academic counterexample has been floored. icat-agent's +8.3 and DeLM's +9.3–10.5 are exactly the magnitudes a floor of the measured order would swallow, and both were single-configuration comparisons. Not refuted — the floor is local — but the hypothesis rests on two unfloored deltas.
  • Is the SWE-bench win a retrieval artifact? Re-run under history-isolation and egress-proxying.
  • Does the within-agent repair-damage result survive a frontier agent with an executable oracle? The damage rates are measured on 3–8B models with deliberately degraded verifiers on math tasks. Take a frontier coding agent, a real fail-to-pass suite as the verifier, and measure the sign of the marginal gain per repair round: does an executable oracle raise discrimination far enough that unbounded iteration is safe, or does it only move the optimum outward? The trajectories already exist on every leaderboard submission.
  • Does the sole-blocker transfer boundary hold for code repair? If it does, step-level repair numbers are upper bounds with an unstated discount, and the ~30% of failures no recovery action reaches sets the ceiling.
  • DeLM vs icat-agent is itself an unrun comparison — near-duals in design, never on the same field.
  • The −39% to −70% sequential degradation has not been re-tested on a validated substrate.
  • All production evidence remains output-count grade; the reconcile step's own failure modes — bad merges, forwarded-but-stale state — are named and unquantified. And nobody has measured spec → tickets, where fleets get their work.
