Grade the trajectory, not the answer: localize the first wrong step

As of 2026-07, the useful unit of agent observability is not the final answer — it's the first step that went wrong. A run can end "correct" while resting on a broken intermediate step, and a long run can be right for the first stage and useless by the last. What moved this topic to consolidating is that independent groups, working at every granularity from reasoning spans to pipeline stages to whole sessions, land on the same move: stop scoring the outcome, start localizing the break. What has happened since is that the move stopped being a principle and got numbers — the audit window is measured, the defect types have a shared vocabulary, and the session-level kill signal has a threshold. And a second half opened: the agent quietly degrades the observability of the code it builds, eroding the very substrate these methods depend on.

The through-line: an agent's own trajectory is the evidence; audit the run against it, find where it first diverged, feed that back to fix the system — and instrument the product the agent writes, because it won't.

Score the first wrong step, not the outcome

Deep-research agents are the clean demonstration. TELBench reframes evaluation around first-wrong-step localization across 1,000 instances and 2,790 annotated trajectories, and DRIFT localizes the break by auditing each of the agent's claims against the evidence its own run gathered — flagging claims that reach the answer path unsupported or contradicted — for up to a 30-point gain in localization accuracy over baselines [T1] (Where Do Deep-Research Agents Go Wrong?). The mechanism is the transferable part: don't judge each reasoning span for surface plausibility; track claims and bind each to supporting evidence in the log. The trade-off is cost — building and checking a claim→evidence map is extra work per run — and whether to do it always or by sampling is unsettled.

The field now also has a shared vocabulary for what a trajectory audit finds, not just a method. ProcCtrlBench ships an ontology of 11 process-defect types in 4 categories over a unified trajectory representation, plus a control-preservation axis (interpretable / interruptible / correctable / reversible), and finds that agents with similar pass rates differ widely on process quality [T1] (ProcCtrlBench) — a fourth independent group reaching grade-the-trajectory and the first to make the defects nameable and comparable across agents.

The audit window is measured, and wide enough to act in

The most useful new result is a measurement of the gap first-wrong-step localization is meant to close. The largest manual failure anatomy to date — 1,794 trajectories, 63k+ annotated steps, 7 frontier models × 3 scaffolds, inter-rater κ 0.78–0.94 — finds the error that decides a failed run lands at a median of step 7 out of ~27, while the first observable failure signal trails it by ~10 steps [T1] (Failure as a Process). So localization has roughly ten steps of headroom to catch a run before it visibly derails. Two more findings redirect the effort:

  • 57.9% of decisive errors are epistemic — the agent misused information already in its own trajectory (false premises alone are 30.7% of all failures). This vindicates the claims-vs-evidence substrate directly: the catching evidence was already in context.
  • 71% of successful runs also hit errors. Recovery, not error avoidance, separates outcomes — so the optimization target is the error→detection→recovery loop, not the error rate. Optimizing for fewer errors optimizes the wrong variable.

At the session level, the same move produces a kill signal. Grading an agent's whole run distribution (23,392 episodes, 396 tasks, 10 models) yields a Meltdown Onset Point — the step past which an unattended run is not recovering and should be killed and restarted rather than left burning tokens — and frontier models meltdown on up to 19% of long runs, which makes the signal load-bearing rather than nice-to-have [T1] (Beyond pass@1). Localize the first break to debug; find the meltdown point to know when to stop.

The same move at production scale

The failure a static leaderboard hides is serial dependence. RAMP runs agents on production-like workflows where each stage depends on the last and finds completion decaying 100% at the first stage to 20% at the last, with none of 15 frontier models finishing the full pipeline — so it builds staged recovery to localize the failing stage [T1] (Benchmarks are Not Enough: RAMP). Same principle as DRIFT at a coarser grain, reached by a different org on a different domain. And it has an operations counterpart: DeepMind runs trajectory monitoring across ~1M coding-agent tasks with trusted "supervisor" models that continuously review a working agent's reasoning, actions, and plans, graded on coverage / recall / time-to-response — the operating metrics observability-as-actuator had been missing — and reports the dominant flagged cause is misinterpretation or overeagerness, not adversarial intent [T2] (Securing the future of AI agents).

Observability as actuator — and its measured ceiling

The trajectory signal doesn't have to end at a diagnosis. Make a coding-agent harness observable — represent each editable component explicitly, distill trajectories into an evidence corpus, pair every proposed change with a prediction verified against outcome — and the same evidence that localizes failures can drive a control loop that improves the system. A self-evolving harness built this way beat a human-designed one, lifting Terminal-Bench 2 Pass@1 from 69.7% to 77.0% and transferring at 12% fewer tokens [T1] (Agentic Harness Engineering). A second, independent group runs the same loop with no labels at all, scoring candidates purely on execution-derived proxies distilled from the traces [T1] (TTHE).

But the loop is only as good as the signal it reads, and that limit is now measured. TTHE's trace-driven judge is "measurably miscalibrated": it commits a 50% harness when a 64% one was in the pool it inspected. Trajectory evidence can drive the loop; it cannot, by itself, tell the loop which way is up. So observability-as-actuator inherits the LLM-judge problem from agent-harness-architecture — the corpus quality, and the judge over it, become the new bottleneck, and in the label-free setting there is no ground-truth gate to fall back on.

The second half: the agent won't instrument what it builds

Every claim above is about observing the agent. The newest limb (2026-07-14) is about observing the product, and it is a negative. Across 4,550 agentic vs 3,276 human PRs in 81 repos, agents change logging less often than humans in 58.4% of repositories, and their apparent higher log density is mostly a PR-size artifact — control for size and it collapses to 1.64 vs 1.59 logs per 1,000 LOC [T1] (Do AI Coding Agents Log Like Humans?). The gap is not uniform: agents match human practice in try/catch (the mechanical, pattern-matchable case) and diverge most in conditional branches (46.7% similar) and loops (41.5% similar) — exactly where a human decides from context that a future maintainer will need to know what happened.

Two facts make this load-bearing rather than a style note. First, prose doesn't fix it: logging instructions appear in 4.7% of cases and are complied with 8.3% of the time from a repository context file, with instructed and uninstructed PRs statistically indistinguishable (p=0.25). The authors' conclusion is the vault's own — use deterministic guardrails (observability linters, CI checks), not instructions (runtime-action-governance). Second, humans silently backfill 72.5% of the missing instrumentation, off-review, so the debt is real but invisible in the merge record (agentic-code-review). The consequence lands on the rest of the vault: this map's own in-loop audit window and unattended-operation's operator surface both assume the code beneath the agent is instrumented — and there is a recursion nobody has closed. If the trajectory-audit techniques above depend on evidence being present in the run, an increasingly agent-authored codebase is quietly eroding the substrate those methods run on. That is a testable prediction — first-wrong-step localization should be worse in agent-authored code — and it is unrun.

What this half does not show is downstream cost: no incidents, no MTTR, no time-to-diagnose against agent-authored code. The gap is measured; that it hurts is inferred, and the vault should not over-claim it.

Where this connects

Trajectory grading is the review-side sibling of "don't trust the agent's 'done'" in agentic-code-review — first-wrong-step localization is what you do when the final answer looks fine but you don't believe it, and the merge/reject label is itself a mislocalized grade (only 35.7% of rejections are genuine agent failure). A harness evaluator that catches a broken step before it ships (agent-harness-architecture) is the constructive counterpart to localizing it after the fact, and the self-evolving harness is the seam where the two maps meet. The trajectory substrate is also a measurement instrument: it is what reveals that 57% of one agent's benchmark wins retrieved the answer rather than derived it (evaluation-integrity), and a reconstructability twin now asks whether a captured trace even licenses the decision a score claims — finding the replay precondition unmet in every real public trace it checked [T1] (Agent-Safety Evaluations as Load-Bearing Evidence). RAMP's cost findings (comparable quality, up to 1000× cost spread) spill into model-routing-token-economics: cost per stage is itself an observability signal.

Open and contested

  • The sources converge on the principle but span domains and granularities (reasoning spans, pipeline stages, harness components, whole sessions), so the mechanism replicates cross-org while no single localization metric is shared across them — "grade the trajectory" is settled, "measure it thus" is not.
  • Observability-as-actuator has a measured ceiling: the trace-driven judge is miscalibrated (50% committed vs 64% available), and label-free there is no ground-truth gate. How the loop keeps its own judge honest is open.
  • The map has two halves and only one is priced. Agent under-instrumentation is measured as a behavior; its downstream cost (incidents, MTTR, debugging time) is unmeasured, and the recursion — does localization degrade in agent-authored code — is a stated, unrun prediction.
  • The cost of always-on trajectory auditing / evidence-corpus distillation versus sampling is unquantified, and now doubly a tension: it is both the per-run debugging overhead and the self-evolving-harness bottleneck.
  • Effect sizes are each their setup's — DRIFT's 30 points on deep-research, RAMP's collapse curve on one workload family, the step-7/lag-10 window on Terminal-Bench. The durable claims are directional.
