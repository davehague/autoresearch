The harness is the product: build it to be replaced

As of 2026-07, the scaffolding around a coding agent — the loop, the tool interfaces, the context plumbing, the per-model prompting, and increasingly the repository and environment the agent runs in — has stopped being glue and become the product. Four independent orgs (Anthropic, Cursor, OpenAI, and a run of academic groups) now converge on harness engineering as the core discipline, and the founding claim is measured: with the model held fixed, the same model scores 59.1% under one harness and 67.4% under another on SWE-bench Pro — 8.3 points of latent capability the scaffold was sitting on [T1] (icat-agent). That founding claim got stronger in July: freeze the model entirely and walk 35 sequential releases of a single scaffolding, and resolve rate ranges over 16 points (23.0%–39.0%) on middleware changes alone [T1] (Don't blame the large language model). The scaffold, not the weights, is the lever — which is also why the scaffold's version belongs in every benchmark claim.

The through-line: a harness is a set of bets about what today's model can't do, and those bets go stale. Build it so each bet can be pulled out and replaced without a rewrite.

Updated 2026-07-18 — the self-improvement half of this article is now contested. The last revision closed by noting that self-evolution had never been compared against compute-matched baselines. Someone ran that comparison, and the result is not kind: under matched budgets, harness evolution loses to plain sampling and transfers +0.6 points to held-out tasks. A separate longitudinal study finds the human version doesn't climb either. Everything about decoupling, per-model tuning, role splits, and repo-as-harness stands; the section on self-tuning has been rewritten, and the rest of the piece should be read with that limb marked contested rather than promising.

As of late July the evidence says something stricter and more inconvenient: the harness and the model are a pair, and results do not transfer when either one is swapped — now measured on six dependent variables by six methods. There is a new section on that below, and it changes how you should read every number in this article, including the ones that open it. Two other things arrived: behavior localization turns out to gate harness work upstream of any edit anyone proposes, and removal turns out to be an under-used kind of edit. The self-evolution limb remains contested and its open questions are unchanged.

Decouple the pieces behind stable interfaces

Anthropic's rebuild of Managed Agents is the clearest worked example. The original design ran the harness inside the same container as its tools; when a container failed, the session was lost, and every infrastructure change had to route through the harness [T2] (Scaling Managed Agents). The fix makes each piece an interface that assumes little about the others: the harness calls its sandbox the way it calls any tool (execute(name, input) → string), session state lives in a durable event log outside the harness, and credentials stay out of the sandbox where model-generated code runs. The decoupling was not just a resilience move — it cut latency measurably (p50 time-to-first-token down ~60%, p95 down

90%). The trade you buy: more moving parts and network hops between brain and hands, in exchange for each part being independently replaceable and warmable.

Spend harness effort on structure, not prompt wording

When academics ablate what actually moves a harness, the answer is consistent: structural components (tools, middleware, memory) drive the gains; system-prompt edits contribute minimally [T1] (Observability-driven harness evolution). Two later self-evolution systems independently search the same space — arbitrary control programs over tools and loops, not prompt strings — and reach the same place. Wordsmithing the system prompt is the low-leverage edit.

The sharpest 2026-07 version of "structure over prompts" is a decomposition, not an ablation. Externalize a planning harness into four separately callable layers over one shared belief backend — belief tracking, declarative planning, symbolic reflection, LLM-backed revision — and the layer carrying the largest single effect makes no model calls at all: adding declarative planning moves win rate 50.0% → 74.1% at 0% LLM invocation, the only contrast in the decomposition whose 95% intervals don't overlap [T1, suggestive, planning domain] (How much heavy lifting can an agent harness do?). The comparison that gives it teeth: captains calling the model on every turn fall below the no-LLM baseline when they lack inference-time reasoning, and that holds up to 109B parameters (0.353 F1, zero wins, against structure's 0.522 F1 and 50% win rate) — "model size is not the missing ingredient, inference-time reasoning is." A mid-reasoning model does reach the planner's neighbourhood (77.8% vs 74.1%, overlapping intervals) at 100% invocation against 0%: the same outcome, radically different bill. Once the structure is in place the model fires on 4.3% of turns for +0.005 F1 and the lowest win rate in its family, because revisions sharpen local targeting while spending a finite question budget. The domain caveat is load-bearing and must travel with the number — Battleship is a probabilistic-search problem where posteriors and one-step lookahead are an unusually good fit, the authors say so, n=54, and this is not software engineering. Take the ordering and the method, not the magnitude.

That paper's second contribution is the more portable one, and it is a measurement discipline this field mostly lacks. Per-layer marginals are not identifiable while the layers share one LLM-orchestrated prompt loop, because deleting one role from a bundled prompt perturbs the others through the shared generation distribution. Attribution requires lifting each layer into a runtime object with declared preconditions and effects — enforced here by a non-Turing-complete DSL, so toggling one layer's flag cannot reach another's logic. What that buys: an aggregate null of −0.001 F1 resolves into +0.140 to −0.148 signed per-case effects in two opposed regimes, which is a trigger-calibration diagnosis rather than a dead mechanism; LLM invocation rate becomes a measured variable instead of a budgeted one; and a −16.7pp confound surfaces and gets published as an open question instead of being absorbed into a layer's score. Search ranks whole harnesses; decomposition tells you which part earned it. If you only ever measure end-to-end pass rate, every null in your harness is indistinguishable from an absent mechanism.

Structure sometimes means removing surface, not adding it. A three-arm ablation (all-tools / bash-only / a single execute_code channel), crossed over two task regimes and two agent designs with the model fixed, finds collapsing to one execution channel is pass-rate-tied in every cell and cheaper in three of four — the extra tool affordances bought no capability at current model sizes and usually cost tokens, because one general channel caches and composes better than many narrow schemas [T1, suggestive] (execute_code regime ablation). "Tied" at workshop scale is underpowered, not proven-equal, and the cheapest surface depends jointly on task regime and agent design — but the direction cuts against the reflex to keep adding tools. Methodological catch worth keeping when you measure your own: separate cost-on-success from cost-of-failure, since the lone adverse cell was entirely doomed-run failure cost, not a per-edit tax.

The harness and the model are one unit — stop reporting them separately

This is the article's biggest change since July, and it is a tightening rather than a reversal. The piece opens with an 8.3-point scaffold gap at a fixed model. Run the experiment the other way — fix the harness, vary the model — and the effect is just as large and just as unpredictable. As of 2026-07 the pair has been shown to be the unit that fails on six different dependent variables:

  • Defects. 255 hand-labeled field bugs need both components to explain; the same trigger crashes 79% of runs in one framework and 2% in a CLI product [T1] (Agent-reactive bugs at the model-harness boundary).
  • Security. Byte-identical repo, same model, harness swapped — install-time attack detection reverses direction [T1] (Setup complete, now you are compromised).
  • Context interventions. A progressive-disclosure context pack is worth double digits under one harness and exactly zero under another whose bare agent already greps, model held constant [T1] (Is progressive disclosure all you need?).
  • Cost. An industry study could not attribute its own result to model or harness at all [T3] (Inference economics of enterprise coding agents), and two production code reviewers running the same backend model (Claude Sonnet 4.6) land within 0.2 points of each other while differing roughly 7× in cost, with opposite behavioural profiles — one spending 58% of its steps exploring, the other 41% validating [T1] (Agentic code review in the terminal).
  • Deterministic harness components. A single mechanism — pinning an agent's first resolved entity and reusing it — amplifies wrong actions 1.8× to 8.5× depending only on which of eight backends sits inside it, worst on the strongest model, which appears to treat carried state as authoritative [T1] (Binding drift in multi-step agents).
  • What the agent is allowed to carry. A harness tuned for memoryless operation reads the fixed per-cycle context of a memory-carrying configuration as thrashing and kills it, forcing a tighter read cap than the same harness imposed on the memoryless arm [T3, single-study] (Delivery, not storage).

Three consequences, in ascending order of how much work they create for you.

Report the stack, not the model. A benchmark row that names a model is naming the wrong noun; the position argument for per-component evaluation makes the same case from the eval side [T3] (Coding benchmarks are misaligned with agentic SE).

A harness test suite that mocks the model tests a system that does not exist. If the model is half of the failure surface, stubbing it out is not isolating your component — it is deleting the interaction where the bugs live.

Structure outside the model is not automatically safe. This article has spent five sections arguing that structural edits beat prompt edits and that removing the model from mechanical paths is a reliability win. Both still hold. But the entity-lock result is a deterministic, model-free, obviously- sensible piece of harness structure that is worse than nothing — it eliminates reference drift to 0% and takes strict task success to 0.0%, because it preserves faithfully without validating. Structure is a lever, not a direction. The distinguishing question is whether the mechanism checks anything: the cue-anchored delivery layer runs zero false alarms across 75 audited trigger evaluations because delivery is decidable; the lock has nothing to decide against.

Behavior localization gates everything upstream

Before any harness edit is proposed, someone has to find the code responsible for the behaviour — and that step is weak and rarely measured. Unaided symbol-level localization on a 2,267-file harness runs 38.3% F1 [T1, single-study, LLM-judged plans with no execution] (Harness Handbook). That reframes the contested self-evolution result rather than resolving it: a matched-budget comparison that finds harness evolution losing to sampling may be measuring retrieval failure rather than search failure. If the optimizer cannot reliably find the component implicated in a failure, its proposals are drawn from the wrong neighbourhood, and no amount of selection pressure repairs that. Anyone rerunning the flagship self-evolution systems under a controlled protocol should instrument localization separately.

Where the uplift actually sits, and who staffs the loop

A production system decomposed by layer attributes +9.5 pp of a +11.0 pp uplift to scaffolding and routing, and only +1.5 pp to the verification checkpoint the system is built around [T3, vendor self-evaluation, no matched-budget scaffold arm] (Where does agent reliability come from?). Read that carefully in both directions. It is the strongest production-scale statement of this article's structure-over-prompts thesis — and the small verification number is positionally decisive rather than negligible, since it converts exactly the tasks good scaffolding already failed.

The same source supplies the staffing answer that makes elaborate loops affordable: 0.5–4B post-trained specialists run the observe/compare, typing and dispatch stages at 0.02–0.1× frontier per-call cost, which is why the compare stage could run on every loop-triggering task instead of a sample. This is the harness-level face of a claim now appearing in three places at once — a 4B fine-tuned router beats four frontier models used zero-shot at post-failure recovery routing [T1] (CodeRescue), and a harness optimizer can recover up to 89.7% of a frontier model's performance at 4% of cost. The pattern: the loop's stages are jobs for small trained components, and the frontier model is for the work in between. The trade is real — you now own training data, retraining cadence, and a staleness problem per component.

One more edit type belongs here, because it is systematically under-used: removal. Counterfactual search over an agent's repair trajectory strips 17.9–32.9% of a patch at 99.1% oracle preservation, and does it 1.6–3.1× better than prompting the agent to do the same job — which the agent fails outright in 3.8–44.9% of runs [T1] (TRIM). Together with the execute_code ablation above, that is two independent results saying the highest-leverage harness edit is often a subtraction, and that where a well-defined mechanical operation exists, taking the model out of the path is the reliability win.

Tune per model — and expect the tuning to expire

The harness is not model-agnostic, and pretending it is leaves performance on the table. Cursor customizes heavily per model and per version: OpenAI models get patch-based file edits while Anthropic models get string-replacement edits, each matched to the model's training data; prompting differs because "OpenAI models are more literal and precise" while Claude is "more intuitive and more tolerant to imprecise instructions"; a new model pre-launch means weeks of offline evals before it ships [T2] (Continually improving our agent harness). A focused reliability sprint drove all tool calls to 2–3 nines.

The flip side of per-model tuning is that the tuning is a liability once the model changes. Anthropic saw this twice: Claude Sonnet 4.5's "context anxiety" (absent in Opus 4.5) meant harness logic written to manage it became dead weight, and the move to Opus 4.6 let them delete whole sprint-management scaffolds outright [T2] (Harness design for long-running application development). Cursor reports the same discipline from its side. This is the claim that used to be single-vendor and now isn't: re-examine and strip the harness on each model upgrade, because its assumptions encode a model that no longer exists. The cost is obvious — you can never treat the harness as done — and it's the price of not shipping stale workarounds.

Two axes now extend "re-tune per model." The harness should also re-shape per task: icat-agent grades issue quality with a rubric and picks the topology at runtime (parallel patch+validate for well-specified issues, exploration first for vague ones) [T1] (icat-agent). And it can re-tune per test distribution without labels at all — see below.

Self-tuning harnesses: contested as of 2026-07-18

Read this section as a live dispute, not a recommendation. Three results published within four days of each other say the evidence for automated harness improvement does not survive controlled measurement. Here is the optimistic case first, then what happened to it.

The per-model re-tuning above can be automated. Make the harness observable — represent each editable component explicitly, distill trajectories into an evidence corpus, pair every change with a prediction verified against outcome — and harness evolution becomes a falsifiable control loop instead of hand work; a self-evolved harness beat a human-built one (Terminal-Bench 2 69.7% → 77.0%) and transferred at 12% fewer tokens [T1] (Observability-driven evolution). A second, apparently independent lineage drops the label entirely: TTHE evolves the harness at test time from unlabeled execution traces with the model frozen, lifting SWE-bench Verified 20% → 35% and LiveCodeBench 30% → 38.3% [T1, single-study] (TTHE). Pointed at cost instead of ceiling, the same trick lets small models close much of the gap to a frontier LLM — one system closed 7 of 21 model gaps, best case 89.7% of frontier performance at 4% of cost [T1] (Better harnesses, smaller models). The move is the same each time: distill the traces, let the harness rewrite its own structure, verify against outcome.

But a self-evolving harness is bounded above by the judge that selects its own improvements, and that bound is now measured. In the label-free setting TTHE's committed harness reaches 50.0% where an oracle over the candidates the judge inspected reaches 64.0% and an oracle over all candidates ever generated reaches 70.0% — 20 points of attainable performance lost, most of it to selection, not search [T1, single-study] (TTHE). The judge prefers outputs that "execute cleanly but are wrong" while claiming near-perfect correctness, and because proposer and judge share the frozen model, more search budget produces correlated variants repeating the same blind spots — more rounds can make it worse. The harness's usual defense against a gameable evaluator — pair it with a non-LLM ground-truth gate — is definitionally unavailable when there is no gold. If you ship self-evolution, the proxy's false-positive profile is a first-class engineering artifact, not a detail. And neither self-evolution source fully prices the loop (TTHE reports no cost figure at all for running a population of candidate programs), so "the harness improves itself" is a capability claim, not yet an engineering recommendation.

Then the compute-matched comparison got run, and the effect largely disappeared. Harness evolution is itself a search procedure — it repeatedly runs candidates, reads outcomes, and revises — so scoring it against a single-shot baseline credits the discovered design with the compute spent discovering it. Charge every arm the same feedback and inference budget on Terminal-Bench 2.1, across Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4-mini, and harness evolution averages 67.4 — below the 68.2 direct-sampling baseline — while plain parallel sampling reaches 72.3 [T1] (Rethinking the evaluation of harness evolution). Give every arm unit tests and the gap widens: parallel sampling 86.0 pass@1, sequential refinement 91.8 pass@5, against harness evolution's 75.8 / 86.2. The decisive number is generalization — on held-out tasks the evolved harness is worth +0.6 points, and +0.0 on GPT-5.4. It fitted the tasks it searched on.

That lands precisely on the caveat TTHE's own authors named and did not test: their method is transductive — selected and scored on the same batch — with forward generalization unestablished. This is why the result is more damaging than an ordinary failure to replicate.

Two things survive it. Harness scaling still beat direct sampling (71.8 vs 68.2), so structural search has value; what is refuted is that the evolutionary loop beats a dumber use of the same compute. And the study reproduces harness evolution rather than testing a specific published system, so an advocate can contest fidelity — one benchmark, three models.

The human version doesn't climb either, which is the more practical finding. Freeze the model and walk 35 sequential releases of one open-source scaffolding against 50 stratified SWE-bench Verified tasks: resolve rate swings 23.0%–39.0% (mean 30.5%) with no significant improving trend (ρ=0.208, p=0.231), while average token consumption climbs 391K → 668K — +70% — monotonically (ρ=0.743, p<0.0001) [T1] (Don't blame the large language model). Users of that scaffolding paid 70% more over the window for capability no better than where it started, with no way to know which release they were on was a good one. Regression risk localizes to two components: the LLM Provider layer and context management; extensibility and security changes are comparatively safe. Ecosystem context makes it worse — release velocities run to 18/week with issue backlogs in the thousands.

This cuts both ways, and the confirming half matters too: a 16-point capability swing sitting inside the middleware means any comparison naming the model but not the scaffolding version carries an uncontrolled term larger than most claimed model gaps. Caveat: one scaffolding studied deeply, a 50-task slice, single run per release — so part of that band is unmeasured sampling noise the paper never floors.

Updated 2026-07-29 — someone published that number, and it splits by domain. The paragraph below used to close this section by demanding a matched-budget held-out score. A Stanford/MIT/KRAFTON group supplies both halves [T1] (Meta-Harness), and the answer is genuinely mixed rather than a vindication or a burial.

The matched-budget half: Best-of-N is included as an explicit "control for whether search matters at all" — independent samples from the seed, same evaluation budget, no search structure — and it reaches 34.0 median / 44.2 best against the searched harness's 50.0 / 56.7. The held-out half is stronger and comes in two flavours. Nine classification datasets never seen during search: 73.1% average against 70.2% for the best hand-designed baseline, best-in-class on 6 of 9 — and notably, naively adding few-shot examples hurt on 7 of 9, so the discovered harness is not just "more context." Then the one that matters most for this article's staleness thesis: a single retrieval harness discovered on olympiad math lifts 200 previously unseen IMO-level problems by +4.7 points across five models never used during search. A harness that transfers to models it was never tuned against is durable scaffolding rather than model compensation — the distinction this piece keeps asking for and rarely gets measured.

And on coding it answers nothing. The TerminalBench-2 arm — 76.4 on Opus 4.6 (#2), 37.6 on Haiku 4.5 (#1 among reported Haiku agents) — searches and scores on the same 89 tasks. The authors frame this deliberately as a "discovery problem," defend it (public writeups already iterate against this benchmark; a split would weaken the signal on a small expensive set), and audit for leakage by manual inspection and regex. That catches copied strings; it cannot catch a harness shaped to the task distribution. Since the matched-budget critique was measured on coding, the dispute is untouched precisely where it was raised.

The mechanism finding is more actionable than either number, and it inverts a reflex. What makes harness search work is the proposer's access to raw prior execution traces through a filesystem it queries with grep and cat — not the search algorithm. Hold proposer, procedure and budget fixed and vary only what may be read: scores-only 34.6 median, scores-plus-LLM-summary 34.9, full traces 50.0 — and the summarized arm is worse than raw scores on best candidate (38.7 vs 41.3). It spent tokens to remove signal. The budgets involved: 10.0 MTok per iteration against 0.002–0.026 for every prior text optimizer, a median 82 files read per iteration, single evaluations emitting up to 10M tokens. The mechanism is horizon — a storage or retrieval decision surfaces as a failure many steps later, so compression deletes exactly the evidence linking them. Note this does not contradict the context-engineering result that pruning plus summarization beats full history: that measures an agent executing, this measures an optimizer diagnosing. Compress what the actor carries forward; preserve what the diagnostician can reach back for. Same paper, worth pairing: the discovered harness beats ACE (Agentic Context Engineering) by 7.7 points at 4.5× fewer context tokens, with the accuracy/context Pareto frontier reported instead of one operating point.

Bounds on all of it: one proposer agent throughout (Claude Code on Opus 4.6), with the cross-proposer study named by the authors as future work — so by this article's own pair thesis, a meta-harness result is itself a harness-model-pair property until someone varies it. Small candidate populations, no seeds or intervals on the ablation cells, and search cost reported only as "a few hours." The one higher-scoring leaderboard entry could not be reproduced from its public code, which the authors disclose.

A second measured cap, one step worse than bad selection: the optimizer can invent the failure it fixes. In a controlled setting where the correct self-edit is no action, proposers fabricated guardrails for nonexistent failures in 15 of 60 runs — and 0 of 60 on featureless input — under three jointly necessary conditions: a rule-shaped pattern in the data, an under-specified rule set, and failure-presupposing instructions [T1, single-study] (Phantom Guardrails). The phantom guard is a no-op, which is what makes it durable: the acceptance metric cannot see it, so a loop that only ever adds keeps it permanently. The fix is cheap and ablation-clean — instruction hygiene (don't assert failures your evidence doesn't show), state that the rule set is complete, and require warrant-aware acceptance: a self-edit lands only with an oracle-confirmed citation to a real failing episode, which drove fabrication to 0/60. Treat that as the minimum disclosure for any harness that edits itself.

What to actually do while this is unsettled. Pin your scaffolding version the way you pin your model, and re-measure cost after every harness upgrade rather than only after model changes. Before adopting any self-improving harness, ask for two numbers now: its matched-budget score on held-out tasks, and which distribution that held-out set was drawn from — a discovery result on the benchmark you care about is priced differently from a transfer result. If it reports only in-distribution gains against an unsearched baseline, it has measured its search budget. And if the harness rewrites itself continuously, ask what warrants an accepted edit — per the fabrication result above, an optimizer will install guardrails for failures that never happened unless acceptance requires a citation to a real failing episode.

The layer above the harness: interchangeability is asserted, not measured

New 2026-07-29. This article is titled "build it to be replaced." Since June 2026, a product category sells exactly that, and it deserves a warning rather than an endorsement. A meta-harness is an orchestration tier that sits above the coding agents — Databricks' Omnigent wraps Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, OpenCode, Hermes and Pi behind a uniform API, with a runner that sandboxes each session, a policy server, and terminal/web/mobile interfaces [T2, no measured claims] (Introducing Omnigent). A generator variant mints bespoke per-repo harnesses across six runtimes instead of orchestrating existing ones, on the thesis "the model is replaceable; the harness is the product" [T3, project README, lead only] (metaharness).

The pitch is composition: combine or swap harnesses without rewriting. Set that against the section above. A uniform API makes harnesses substitutable at the call site. It does not make them equivalent in outcome — 8.6 points of mean shift with rank order changing, a security outcome reversing, a context pack worth double digits under one harness and zero under another, a byte-identical skill library read on 99–100% of tasks under one stack and 0–1% under another. Everything you would actually need to use one of these layers well — which harness to route a workload to, what a swap costs, what breaks when a workflow moves from Claude Code to Codex — is per-pair, and no product in the category publishes it. Their own per-runtime adapter tables (Claude Code plugins, Codex TOML, Hermes <think>-scrubbing, a microhypervisor sandbox for one target) are tacit engineering evidence that they know non-equivalence exists.

Hold this as an open question, not a refutation. A mediating layer may well be the right place to manage non-equivalence rather than pretend it away, and the settling experiment is cheap: one workload, two harnesses, one layer, publish the delta. Nobody has run it. Until someone does, treat harness portability as a migration — re-run your own evaluation on the target pair — not a configuration change.

Two parts of the pitch survive the pair thesis untouched, and they are the reason to look. Governance: policy enforced at the runner, below the prompt, on the dispatch path the harness already mediates, with three composing scopes (server admin default, per-agent rule, per-session override), tool-call limits, and spend caps that pause rather than kill. That does not require harnesses to be equivalent, only wrapped — though nobody has red-teamed the enforcement, and a user-scope override that can loosen an admin default is a policy engine with a hole in it, which the post does not address. Sandboxing is mandatory bwrap on Linux and seatbelt on macOS, with a disclosed Windows gap — process-tree containment and no filesystem or network isolation. A governance layer's guarantee is its weakest platform, not its best. Supply chain: if harnesses become generated artifacts shipped as signed packages, harness provenance becomes a supply-chain problem, and the executing layer itself becomes installable. The generator's Ed25519 witness-signed releases, SBOM and SLSA L2 provenance are unusual for this category and currently exist ahead of any documented incident — a forward indicator worth watching, not a finding.

One last caution on that generator: it ships the self-evolution loop the section above says nobody has validated — "self-improvement through measured configuration mutations" — with no acceptance criterion, no held-out split and no mutation warrant published. Its README reports SWE-bench Verified 55.6% at ~$0.15/instance via a GLM→Opus escalation. The shape is plausible and matches the specialist-staffing economics above; the reporting has no harness description, no pass@1-versus-best-of-k statement and no seeds, so it is a lead, not support.

Split roles, seed the session, and learn when to stop

For multi-hour autonomous builds, Anthropic splits the work into planner / generator / evaluator, with the evaluator a separate skeptical agent that drives the running app and grades against hard thresholds — because an agent asked to grade its own output confidently praises mediocre work [T2] (Harness design). The reported builds are real (a working game in ~6 hr/$200 against a broken solo attempt in ~20 min/$9), though the split's cost is unquantified against a strong solo baseline on the same task and model.

Below the role split sits a session-lifecycle split: an initializer agent seeds the environment once (init script, a feature list marked all-failing, a progress file, git), then each session re-enters through a fixed ritual — read artifacts, pick one incomplete feature, baseline-test, make incremental progress, clean up — which kills the two unguided cross-window failure modes, build-everything-at-once and premature "done" [T2] (Effective harnesses for long-running agents).

The thing outside the agent that decides it is not done can itself be learned, and its correct policy differs per agent. A cost-penalized bandit choosing {generate, analyze-then-generate, stop} from cheap features beats a fixed iteration budget on both quality and price, and learns opposite policies for two agents — stop-early on 95% of tasks against a strong zero-shot agent, iterate-and-analyze against a weaker one [T1, single-study] (SCATE). The fixed iteration cap in most harnesses is wrong in both directions at once; the evaluator should decide not just whether the work is done but how much more to spend finding out.

The harness extends into the repo and the environment

The harness no longer stops at the loop. OpenAI shipped an internal product with zero hand-written code (~1M LOC, ~1,500 PRs, 3.5 PRs/engineer/day sustained) by treating the repository as a harness component: a ~100-line AGENTS.md table of contents over a CI-linted docs/ system of record, layer-dependency linters, lint errors that inject remediation instructions into agent context, and garbage-collection agents that open auto-mergeable cleanup PRs [T2] (Harness engineering). The rule: enforce invariants mechanically, leave implementations free. The caveat is scope — this is one greenfield, agent-first repo; whether mechanical-invariant enforcement pays off retrofitted onto a brownfield codebase is unmeasured.

It also extends downward into the environment, and that turns out to be where quality is decided. On from-scratch enterprise builds, ~95.6% of failures land before deep business logic — 63.5% never run stably — so the highest-return harness component is the environment (deps, tools, secrets, network), not the role split [T1] (SaaSBench); Cursor reports the same from operating cloud agents, where "environment setup became the determining factor" [T2] (Cloud agent lessons). None of the planner/generator/ evaluator numbers control for environment quality, so the two contributions are still confounded. And for the isolation layer, buy rather than build: Anthropic's custom proxy layers were the containment components that failed while gVisor/seccomp/hypervisors held — "the software you build yourself is often the weakest" [T2] (How we contain Claude).

Don't trust the judge

A separate evaluator beats self-grading, but it is still an LLM judge, and an LLM judge carries exploitable biases: anything that optimizes to pass it — an RL loop, or an agent iterating until the reviewer says "done" — learns the judge's blind spots instead of doing the task [T1] (Reward hacking in rubric-based RL). The judge's environment is part of the problem: give an agent web and git-history access and it routes around the criterion 63% of the time. And as the self-evolution result above shows, under sustained optimization the judge's miscalibration is not a tail risk — it is 20 measured points. Keep a non-LLM ground-truth gate (tests, CI, the app actually running) in the loop, don't hold a fixed judge under sustained optimization pressure, and treat "how the harness keeps its evaluator honest" as an open design problem, not a solved one. See agentic-code-review and evaluation-integrity.

Where this connects

The harness is where the other maps meet. Context handling is a harness concern — Cursor's shift from static pre-loaded context to dynamic just-in-time fetch is the context-engineering thesis at the harness layer. Model routing is a harness concern: routing lives in the harness, tool surface is a spend lever, and both feed model-routing-token-economics. Whether hesitation surfaces as a clarifying question or a silent guess is a harness affordance (31.8% vs 10.5% ask rate on the same model under two harnesses), which is runtime-action-governance and part of the Claude Code/Codex divergence in spec-driven-development. Durable session state, credential isolation, and the environment-reconstruction problem are shared with unattended-operation. The evaluator agent is an in-loop review gate, the constructive counterpart to trajectory error-localization in observability. And retry cost is an architectural property orthogonal to correctness — an execution-governed decomposition cut retry cost 51.7% with every configuration still scoring 100% correct, a difference invisible to a pass rate (evaluation-integrity).

Open and contested

  • The quantified wins are each one system's numbers — latency −60/−90%, 8.3-point scaffold gap, build cost/duration, tool reliability at 2–3 nines, 3.5 PRs/eng/day — with no cross-org measurement of the same metric yet. The pair thesis makes this worse rather than better: if a result does not survive a component swap, the missing cross-org replication is not a nice-to-have, it is the only thing that would tell you whether any of these numbers describe your stack.
  • Nobody has run the pair experiment as a designed factorial. Every one of the six dependent variables above comes from varying one component and observing that the effect moves. A harness × model grid — the same k harnesses crossed with the same k models on one task set — would say whether the interaction term dominates the main effects, and it is a straightforward, unrun study.
  • Behavior localization at 38.3% F1 is one measurement on one harness, scored on LLM-judged plans with no execution. If it replicates, it sits upstream of the whole self-evolution dispute; if it does not, the matched-budget critique stands unqualified. Either way it should be instrumented separately rather than bundled into an end-to-end score.
  • The specialist-staffing economics (0.02–0.1× frontier cost per stage) come from one vendor's internal serving estimates on proprietary weights, with latency overhead unmeasured. The +9.5 of +11.0 pp decomposition has no matched-budget scaffold arm.
  • Structure-is-not-automatically-safe has no diagnostic. The entity lock and the cue-anchored delivery layer are both deterministic model-free harness structure; one is worth 3.0× more wrong actions and the other runs zero false alarms. The distinguishing property looks like "does the mechanism check anything it could be wrong about," but nobody has stated that as a testable rule or applied it to an existing harness's component inventory.
  • Harness debt is asserted, not sized: every vendor says re-tune per model is necessary, none measures the cost of not doing it.
  • Automated harness improvement is contested, and as of 2026-07-29 the dispute has a domain boundary rather than a verdict. Outside coding, matched-budget and held-out evidence now exists and favours search (Best-of-N control beaten; +2.9 over the best hand-designed baseline on nine unseen datasets; +4.7 across five models never searched against). Inside coding — where the critique was measured — the new evidence searches and scores on the same 89 tasks, so nothing has been resolved there. The open question is now specific: does harness search generalize on a coding benchmark under a disjoint held-out split? That is one experiment nobody has run, and it would settle the limb either way. The older ask still stands too: rerun the two flagship self-evolution systems (observability-driven evolution, TTHE) under the matched-budget disjoint-task protocol their critics propose.
  • The meta-harness product layer sells interchangeability and measures nothing. Two sources, both unmeasured (one T2 vendor post, one T3 README). The settling experiment is cheap and unrun: one workload across two harnesses through one mediating layer, publish the delta. Two sub-questions with no evidence at all — what mediation overhead a uniform wrapper imposes (not obviously zero; an incumbent harness's own thrash guard has been observed firing against a configuration it wasn't tuned for), and whether a user-scope policy override can loosen an admin default, which would make the governance story hollow. Neither product states precedence.
  • Harness provenance as a supply-chain surface has exactly one source and no incident literature. If harnesses are generated per-repo and distributed as packages, the executing layer becomes installable and every argument about untrusted content reaching an agent's execution path applies one level up. The countermeasures (witness signing, SBOM, SLSA) currently precede any documented attack. Open: does the rest of the category adopt them, and has anyone tried to poison a generated harness?
  • Harness search's own dependence on its proposer is unmeasured. Every result in the Meta-Harness paper uses one proposer (Claude Code on Opus 4.6), and the authors name the cross-proposer study as future work. By this article's own pair thesis that makes the meta-level result a pair property too — which means the finding "harness search works" may not transfer across a swap of the thing doing the searching.
  • Decomposition has been demonstrated once, in a domain chosen to suit it. The zero-LLM heavy-lifting result (+24.1pp win rate, 0% invocation) and the layer-lifting method that produced it come from n=54 games of Battleship, a probabilistic-search problem the authors concede favours declarative planning, with one 9B model supplying the residual. The method's value is the measurement discipline; whether the ordering survives in a coding harness is untested, and the authors name replication in a second domain as their first future-work item.
  • Self-evolution's ceiling is also a miscalibrated judge (20 points, most in selection), and the standard fix — a ground-truth gate — is unavailable label-free by construction. Still open: does the selection gap widen across generations as proposer and judge share blind spots; is there any label-free in-flight detector of judge miscalibration; and does a periodic gold-labeled audit reintroduce the very labeled-set dependency the method exists to escape? Note these questions now sit downstream of a prior one — whether the loop beats sampling at all.
  • Self-evolution remains unpriced — TTHE reports no cost figure for running a population of candidate programs. Combined with the matched-budget result, the unflattering hypothesis nobody has excluded is that the loop is an expensive way to buy the gains of best-of-n.
  • Human-driven scaffolding evolution has the same problem from the other side: 35 releases, no quality trend, +70% tokens. Nobody has measured whether any scaffolding's release series shows a durable upward trend — the one study is n=1 at the scaffolding level, and a second scaffolding could break the finding either way.
  • If ~95.6% of from-scratch failure mass sits in setup and integration, the environment may dominate the role split, but nobody has ablated a harness against a fixed, pre-built environment to separate the two.
