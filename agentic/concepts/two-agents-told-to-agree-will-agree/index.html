The two-reviewer setup that looks like independent validation is the one that produces false consensus

This map already holds that model-family diversity is not verifier independence (verifier-diversity-is-not-verifier-independence) and that a verifier sharing the generator's interpretation shares its error (a-verifier-sharing-the-interpretation-shares-the-error). Both are about what the checkers know. This is a failure of a third kind, and it is caused by the protocol rather than by the models: when two agents are asked to produce a joint verdict, the pressure to converge is itself the defect.

The setup is minimal and looks like good practice. A reviewer subagent reviews a frozen artifact; a critic subagent audits that review and may challenge it; they exchange review text until consistent; only then does the main agent edit. On code generation it is the best of six methods tested — and the progression it was built through is itself a result this map should keep: zero-shot 77%, self-refine 77%, single external reviewer 77%, two independent reviewers 75%. Four methods, one indistinguishable cluster. Adding independent reviewers does not move the number. Interaction does: the reviewer-critic pair reaches 87% with three agents where a five-agent parallel-review-plus-meta-reviewer system reaches 82%.

Point the same protocol at review quality and it comes last. F1 0.457 against 0.495 for a single reviewer, 0.501 for the five-agent system and 0.503 for two independent reviewers. Worse than the thing it was built to improve on, and worse than doing nothing extra.

The traces name the mechanism, and there are two shapes of it. Over-decomposition: the reviewer writes one real bug plus two or three hedged concerns, the critic agrees with all of them and adds a speculative bug of its own, and a formatting step turns each into a separate comment — the judge marks 3 of 5 fabricated because they are too thin. The loop adds findings and never filters them. Yielding: the reviewer approves, the critic raises a concern that the authors manually verified as real, the reviewer rebuts with a file-level argument citing no code, and the critic gives in. The verdict flips to AGREE and the real bug is dropped from the final review — on a task where the non-interacting baseline catches it at more than double the F1. Both failures have the same structure: the agents reach agreement, and the agreement is not supported by enough evidence. The authors' name for it is false consensus, and their sharpest observation is that it is dangerous precisely because it looks like independent validation.

Two agents agreeing is not two pieces of evidence. This is the operational consequence for anyone running a reviewer plus a second-opinion agent in a pipeline: the second agent's concurrence carries information only if the protocol made disagreement cheap and legible. Absent that, concurrence is conversational pressure with a confidence score attached. It also means the failure is structural rather than a model-quality problem — the same three agents, unchanged, go from worst to best on a prompt change alone (type-the-disagreement-or-it-collapses-into-agreement).

And the generation win does not license the review claim. The paper's own framing is the useful part: performing best on one benchmark (code generation) did not transfer to a related task (code review), and the reason cooperative AI systems need to be audited rather than merely benchmarked is that the audit is where the convergence path becomes visible. Related: the-agents-final-report-is-its-least-reliable-artifact; a-review-agents-default-failure-is-volume-not-blindness — over-decomposition is that failure produced by a second agent rather than by the first; reviewers-underplan-and-overvalidate.

Bounds. One model family throughout (Claude Sonnet 4.5, medium reasoning, every agent and subagent on every benchmark), so nothing separates protocol from model. The review-quality metric is an LLM judge matching generated comments against human PR feedback — useful for comparing methods, and liable to penalise valid comments that differ from the human's or reward surface-form matches that do not improve the patch. Two execution modes (Python orchestrator for LCB and SWE-PRBench, pure-text skill on SWE-bench Verified) answer different questions and are not interchangeable. Claims are empirical, not formal: the paper does not claim reviewer-critic loops are universally better or worse than the alternatives.
