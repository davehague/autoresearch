Two reliability curves, and only one of them moves

The measurement is a clean two-by-two that nobody in this vault had run: hold the skill suite, the tasks, the authoring surface and the interface fixed, vary model generation and prose-versus-compiled, and watch the two curves. Prose procedure execution improves with the model — 56% to 68% — because in that arm reconstructing the control flow on each run is a capability, and capabilities improve. The compiled harness does not move at all: 86% on both, because the graph carries the procedure and the graph is byte-identical across the two sweeps. The model still authors the content of every model-owned slot; it no longer decides which steps happen.

Three consequences, in descending order of how much they should change what you do.

Structure pays most where models are weakest and cheapest. The 30-point advantage on the older model narrows to 17 on the newer one. That inverts the standard argument for waiting — "this will be fixed by the next model" — for this class of mechanism specifically: the gap closes from the prose side, slowly, and the cheap-model deployment is exactly where the structural investment returns most. Anyone deliberately running a small model for cost or latency reasons is choosing the regime where compiling the procedure is worth the most, and the token cost runs the same direction (0.58× median, see compile-the-procedure-into-the-harness).

A prose-compliance number expires. Any measurement of the form "agents follow instruction X in N% of runs" is a snapshot of one model generation and a lower bound on the next, which is a specific instance of this map's standing complaint that a measurement belongs to the whole stack rather than to one component (attribute-performance-to-the-stack-not-the-model, harness-model-assumptions-go-stale, re-examine-the-harness-when-the-model-changes). It also means the vault's own prose-compliance figures — the 8.3% logging compliance (instructions-fail-to-govern-non-functional-requirements), the 17–40% baseline procedural rates (re-delivery-governs-procedure-not-permission) — should be read as dated rather than as constants. Whether the permission half of that partition also lifts with capability is untested and is the more interesting question, since it has not moved for anything else yet.

The comparison itself is unstable, which is a measurement hazard rather than a finding. Two arms whose capability sensitivity differs cannot be ranked by one number: report the delta at each model, or the ranking is a property of the model you happened to pick. The losses tell the same story from the other end — on the stronger model the harness loses on 4 of 30 skills, all judgment-heavy, where there was little determined structure to compile and a capable model executed the prose well. The predicted behaviour, not an anomaly: the benefit tracks a skill's mechanism-to-judgment ratio, so a suite weighted differently would move the average.

Caveats, and they bound this tightly. Two points from one provider establish that one curve is flat and the other is not; they do not establish the shape of the prose curve across a wider capability range, and in particular do not say whether the remaining 17 points close, plateau, or invert. The model-independence claim is about the substrate — the same compiled artifact selects its model at run time — and is strongest for the mechanical share of a procedure; model-owned slots retain whatever capability sensitivity their content has. Compliance is scored by a model judge. Nine runs per skill per arm, 30 skills weighted toward document, process and compliance work.
