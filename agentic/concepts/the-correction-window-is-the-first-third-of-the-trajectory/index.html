The headroom this map measured is real, and it is spent early

This map's window result says the error that decides a failed run lands at a median of 7 steps into trajectories averaging 27, while the first observable failure signal trails it by about 10 steps (decisive-errors-strike-early-and-surface-late). That was an argument that in-loop auditing has room to act. It had never been checked against an intervention that actually recovered runs.

It checks out, and tightens. Across all 244 recovered runs, the first intervention landed within the first third of the trajectory — not most of them, all of them. Nothing in the study design forced this; the monitor is free to fire at any step, and on the runs it rescued it always fired early. The complement is the useful half: a supervisor that first speaks after the first third did not, in this study, save anything.

Recovery is also repeated rather than single-shot. 182 of 244 (74.6%) required more than one intervention, averaging 3.70 across all recovered runs. So the operational shape is not "detect the decisive error, correct it, done" — it is a sustained low-rate correction over the first phase of the run, which is consistent with repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum's finding that repair is a process with a peak rather than an event, and with the fact that the monitor's own value comes from suppressing interventions rather than issuing them (intervene-on-a-trigger-not-on-a-schedule).

Two practical consequences for anyone building the detector. It sets a latency budget: a check that requires enough trajectory to be statistically confident — a learned monitor needing post-onset horizon, for instance (a-detectors-operating-point-belongs-to-its-deployment) — may not fire inside the window where correction works, which is a reason to favour the checks that fire on the first violating step. And it argues for cheapness over precision at the front of the run: with drift present in 78–86% of unmodified trajectories, an early detector is operating in a population where something really is usually wrong.

Carried as suggestive rather than strong: "first third" is a post-hoc description of the recovered set, not a pre-registered threshold, and there is no matched analysis of unrecovered runs asking whether their first intervention was late or merely unheeded — the authors' own regression analysis says the dominant failure is the executor not following correct guidance, which would make timing incidental in those cases. One harness, one language, no intervals.
