The cheap judge in the cascade did none of the work, and the cascade still won

This map holds that routing pays when the router reads something real — the session rather than the query (route-the-session-not-the-query), the repository rather than the issue text (route-after-scouting-the-repository-not-from-the-issue-text), live traffic rather than a static taxonomy (route-with-a-classifier-trained-on-live-traffic) — and that solve sets are nested, so route for cost rather than accuracy (solve-sets-are-nested-so-route-for-cost-not-accuracy). This adds a routing signal none of those use: the model's own calibrated confidence in the answer it just produced, with the threshold set by conformal risk control against expert labels rather than chosen by hand.

The economics come out ahead but not the way the design implies. At the balanced operating point the cascade reaches CPP 3.49 against 3.61 for the mid model alone and 5.11 for the expensive model alone — 31.7% cheaper than the strong single judge at higher accuracy (98.8% vs 90.0%). That is a real win and it is the headline.

The judge distribution is the more interesting number. At α = 0.14 it is 0 / 72.1 / 27.9: the cheapest judge, the whole point of tier one, handled nothing. Its confidence never cleared its own calibrated threshold, so every case escalated immediately, and the cascade's actual shape was mid tier, with a quarter escalated to strong. Read against solve-sets-are-nested-so-route-for-cost-not-accuracy, this is what happens when the cheap arm sits below the competence floor for the task: it contributes only latency and its share of the token bill. Tuning α up until it does absorb the work (α = 0.36, distribution 99 / 1 / 0, CPP 1.65 — less than half the cost of anything else here) collapses agreement to 62.0%. So the cheap tier is not usable at this task, and the cost curve says so honestly rather than hiding it.

The practical instruction for anyone building a verification cascade: measure your tier distribution, not just your blended cost. A tier that never fires is a tier you can delete, and a tier that fires for everything is the model you should have picked outright. The lesson generalises past judging — this is a-routing-claim-needs-a-no-router-ablation applied to a cascade, where the ablation is free because the distribution is already instrumented.

One thing this buys that a single-model choice cannot: the cascade can decline. Cost per performance here is computed over accepted verdicts, and the 14% abstained cases go to a human whose time is not in the CPP figure (price-the-checkers-abstention-as-lost-coverage). So the 31.7% saving is against the model cost of a judge that answers everything, and the true comparison would need the human review hours priced in — which nobody has done.

Bounds. One embedded case study, one domain, 100 evaluation verdicts, one cascade ordering. Cost tiers are official API list prices at a moment in time, and the whole ranking would move with a price change or a model release — this map's standing caution (harness-is-tuned-per-model-and-version). Confidence is estimated by prompting each judge against five simulated annotators at temperature 0.7 and reading empirical label frequency, an approximation forced by black-box APIs having no log-probabilities, and its quality is not separately validated. Per-tier conformal bounds are proven individually and the authors state they do not compose into a system-level guarantee under adaptive routing. And nothing was ablated: no arm runs the same cascade with uncalibrated thresholds, so the contribution of the calibration versus the escalation is unmeasured.
