Separate proposing the answer from authorizing the stop, and make the authorization mechanically checkable

This map's standing lesson is that validation quality, not iteration count, is the operative variable in a loop. That lesson has been measured on the repair half — a weak verifier destroys correct work, and repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum puts a shape on it — and on the coordination half. This measures it at the termination boundary, which is where an unattended loop actually spends its authority: continuing indefinitely wastes calls and compounds risk; stopping early turns an unsupported intermediate state into a final answer.

The design move is a separation of powers. The agent proposes a certificate; a deterministic verifier checks it against a trusted contract and an immutable ledger. The agent does not author the contract during a run. Six check families run in order — descriptor digest, ledger binding, slot coverage, evidence validity, scope and cardinality, transform authorization, exact replay — and internal exceptions fail closed to continue. The transform language is closed and non-executable (aggregation, difference and ratio, extrema, set union, top-k, grouped-sum top-k, all-null abstention, windowed sum difference), which is what makes "replay the claim" a decidable operation rather than another judgement call.

One distinction is worth carrying into any loop design: stuck is a separate controller decision requiring its own recovery-availability rule. Verifier failure alone neither licenses completion nor proves recovery is impossible — which is exactly the collapse most stop heuristics make, treating "I cannot verify this" as either "so I'm done" or "so I'm stuck."

The ablations say which check is load-bearing. Claim coverage and value replay each carry 36 of 36 tasks; scope carries 12. Two checks ablate to zero additional unsafe completions — and the author reports that as residual containment by another check, not as evidence the check is unnecessary. The exact-transform ablation has no matched fault in the frozen grid at all, and is reported as a coverage limitation of the benchmark rather than quietly dropped. That discipline is why the whole result is worth grading strong.

For unattended fleets this is the missing piece of the maker/checker argument. verification-cost-is-the-bound-on-delegation says the reason you can walk away is a checker that can refuse. This gives that checker a concrete, cheap, deterministic form at the one boundary that matters most — and prices it: under a cent and one extra turn per trajectory. It is also the direct constructive answer to the field-side observation that zero public agent loops commit a stop condition: the artifact nobody versions now has a specification.

Bounds. Six synthetic task families, not deployment traffic; the eight faults are an intervention design, explicitly not an estimate of natural prevalence. One planner/model stack (a disclosed substitution for an unavailable source-default model) and 24 closed-loop tasks do not establish cross-framework generality; the confirmatory analysis rests on 22 task clusters. The soundness statement is relative to a trusted contract and an honest adapter: hash binding is not a signature or remote attestation, a malicious adapter can fabricate an internally consistent ledger, and a contract can omit a requirement. The static faults are deliberately aligned with the certificate's declared obligations, which favours a verifier implementing them and says nothing about unknown error classes.
