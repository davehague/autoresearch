The floor is the finding: a resolvable-magnitude bound turns "we can't tell" into a stated number, and an arm-correlated stop rule shows how a downstream analysis can be biased by a control that was never hidden

This map's single argument is that a delta is not a result until its own null — or, here, its own resolvable floor — is measured and stated alongside it. SaltBench is a worked instance of exactly that discipline applied end-to-end, and it goes further than most sources this map carries by publishing the floor as a number (2.0072x at n=3) rather than a qualitative caveat, then applying it mechanically: three of five observed premiums fall below that floor, so the paper states plainly that no ratio, range, or percentage may be quoted as "the cost" of the method, only the cross-problem sign. The gold-arm extension is a second, sharper instance of the same discipline: the paper computes, before the extension's cells fire, that the sample size (k=4) makes p=0.0625 the best achievable outcome under any possible result, and reports the resulting non-significant p=0.3125 as "no verdict" rather than as evidence against the treatment — refusing to let a null read as a negative finding when the design was never powered to produce a positive one.

The instrument-bias finding is the piece most worth carrying past this specific paper. A per-episode budget cap (registered, published, never hidden) turns out to be correlated with the arm under test — because the treatment is the arm that spends more, it disproportionately reaches the cap, and it reaches it specifically on the hardest, slowest problems for that arm. When a correctness pass is run afterward over the surviving (uncapped) cells, the treatment's population has been silently filtered down to its easiest instances relative to the control's unfiltered population — a selection effect the pass rate cannot reveal on its own, discovered only because the paper checked whether capped cells were evenly distributed across arms rather than assuming it. This generalizes past this paper: any evaluation with an arm-differentiated stop rule (a timeout, a retry cap, a cost ceiling) needs the same check before its downstream pass/fail numbers are trusted.

Interaction edges: spec-driven-development — a directly-priced instance of the "front-loaded structure" enforcement pole this map already tracks (spec-then-build vs prompt discipline vs immutable controls): specifying-and-verifying against a formal statement costs more, with the premium scaling with component size, though this source cannot say whether that cost buys correctness (its own post-hoc pass didn't separate the arms). ci-cd-for-agents — the referee-gated, agent-blind isolation protocol (network-walled, probed before every scored run) is a verification-gate design pattern directly relevant to this map's contest over how strict a commit or merge gate should be, independent of the specific cost finding.

Bounds: the five-component population is authored by the same people who wrote the treatment method being tested, so it carries no independent authorship and no public-contamination check of the kind a drawn benchmark population allows. The paper measures a "dieted" (reduced, solo-agent- renderable) rendering of the underlying method, explicitly not a test of its full multi-agent-tier form. Three of five problems reach the registered n=3 on the plain arm only by counting a cell fired in a separate campaign root under an earlier harness export; the paper discloses and bounds this dependency rather than hiding it, and the sign result is confirmed to hold with or without the borrowed cells, but two of five magnitudes shift when they're excluded. No cell in the reported cost matrix carried a referee verdict at the time it was priced (correctness was assessed only in the separate post-hoc pass), so the cost finding and the correctness finding cannot be causally joined — the paper states explicitly that it does not claim the treatment "costs more and produces more correct code."
