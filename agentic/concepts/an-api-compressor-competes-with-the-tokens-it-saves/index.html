The compressor's bill scales with the context it reads, so it is most expensive exactly when compression matters most

This map already holds that cost is a first-class design variable and that cheap inference relocates rather than removes spend (cheaper-inference-relocates-cost-into-repair-labor, agent-cost-spans-orders-of-magnitude). This adds a case where the accounting inverts the ranking of the options entirely, and it needs no experiment to establish — it follows from published prices and measured rates.

The structure is the finding. An API compressor reads the uncompressed context — the very tokens the exercise exists to avoid paying for — and writes the compressed one, both metered. So its cost is a fixed multiple of the input, which means it is expensive precisely in the situation compression is for. A self-hosted compressor's cost is amortized GPU time: independent of upstream token prices and falling as utilization rises. Those are not two points on a quality-cost curve; they are two different cost structures, and the better-quality option is on the wrong one.

The frontier row is the one to internalize. gpt-5 retains 93.6% of solve quality as a compressor, the best number in the table — and running it costs $7.44 to save $1.14. Its quality retention "is not a favorable point on a trade-off curve; at these prices there is no saving to trade against." A quality metric with no cost denominator would have ranked it first.

The caching caveat is stated by the authors and cuts toward caution, not comfort. Real agents re-send an accumulating prefix that is largely cache-eligible at roughly a tenth of base rate, which shrinks the downstream saving every compressor is competing for — so the mid-tier compressor's 10% margin is an overestimate for a realistic agent, not an underestimate. Compression competes against the cached price of the tokens it removes, not the list price.

The self-hosted row is worth copying as a reporting norm as much as a result: it is stated as a threshold rather than a number, because the authors did not measure their own hosting cost and decline to invent one. "It beats raw context when your amortized cost is under $2.23 per million tokens; here is the tokens-per-second that implies on a rented GPU; check it against your hardware."

For a fleet running scheduled agents, this is a routing question with an unusual answer: the model you would pick for the compression task is not the model you should pick for the compression role. Pick the one that does not bill per token, then ask whether its quality is adequate — which is the reverse of how model selection is normally done.

Bounds. Single-turn, uncached list pricing, one upstream price point; a full treatment of cache pricing, per-turn prefix growth and where the token bill actually concentrates in a multi-turn agent is explicitly deferred to a companion paper. Prices are a moving target — the arithmetic is durable, the specific dollar figures are dated August 2026. The self-hosted threshold assumes an unmeasured throughput; the authors say so.
