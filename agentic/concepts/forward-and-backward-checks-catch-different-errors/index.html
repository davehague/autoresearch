Two checks with the same score are not the same check

The ablation is the interesting part of this system, and it is the kind of result that only shows up when someone reports overlap instead of totals. Forward-only and backward-only score identically — 68 of 120 each. If you were choosing a verification stage on the leaderboard you would call it a tie and ship whichever was cheaper. But each rescues 6 issues the other misses, and combining them adds 7 more that neither finds alone.

The mechanism behind the disjointness is legible, which is why it should generalize past this system. Forward reasoning acts before the patch exists: it reorganizes context into an explicit rationale and thereby corrects flawed editing decisions at the point of generation. Backward reasoning acts after: it catches patches whose effect diverges from the reported problem, an error class that is invisible while you are still deciding what to edit. One is a planning intervention wearing a verification costume; the other is verification proper. They cannot fail the same way because they do not run at the same time.

This is the within-agent repair limb's sharpest structural datum since an-independent-checker-beats-the-same-model-checking-itself. That map's running theme is that validation quality rather than iteration count is the operative variable — repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum, a-verifier-sharing-the-interpretation-shares-the-error, spend-the-budget-on-context-not-repair-rounds. This adds a dimension the theme was missing: validation quality is not scalar. Two checks can be equally good and differently blind, and the aggregate metric hides exactly the property you need to compose them.

The design rule that falls out is cheap to apply. When you are choosing between verification strategies with comparable scores, measure the overlap of what they rescue, not just the totals — the same discipline localize-first-wrong-step-not-final-answer applies to attribution and report-judge-agreement-per-condition-not-per-panel applies to judges. If the sets are nested, take the better one; if they are disjoint, both are cheap relative to the failure they prevent.

Bounds. One 120-issue subset, one model, one benchmark, no repeated seeds — the 6-and-6 disjoint sets are small enough that run-to-run variance is a live alternative explanation for the size of the disjointness, though not for its existence. The full system's reconciliation stage is not itself ablated against a naive union of the two verdicts, so "reconciliation exposes 7 more" could be reconciliation reasoning or could be one more pass at the material. And this measures rescue on a benchmark with executable tests; nothing says the two directions stay complementary where the oracle is a judge.
