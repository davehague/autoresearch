Nothing in the contract describes a service that opens a branch and commits

This map is about what an unattended fleet needs when nobody is watching — durable state, an operator surface, credential and identity governance. It has always treated accountability as an engineering property: who may write (a-lease-is-not-write-authority), what the fleet may believe about its own state, which credential the action ran under. This is the layer above all of that, and it is in worse shape than the engineering.

The contractual vocabulary predates the thing it governs. Output is defined as what a service returns to the user. No term in eighteen documents across seven providers describes a service that opens a branch, authors a commit, and pushes it. Where "agent" appears at all — in one provider's terms effective March 2026 — it means an agent the customer builds, not the agent operating inside the customer's repository. The terms are not wrong so much as aimed at a different product.

And the verification duty thins exactly where the autonomy concentrates. The same provider expressly obliges individual users to review, test and validate output, and states no such duty for volume-licence customers anywhere in the agreement governing them: express verification vocabulary drops by a factor of seven, and to 0.00 occurrences per thousand words in the instrument governing generative AI services specifically — against 10.82 in a comparable-length AI-specific instrument elsewhere in the collection. The obvious alternative explanation is controlled: a second provider makes the same consumer-to-business transition and its commercial terms retain verification vocabulary in the middle of the range. The author is careful that this is a fact about drafting, not about legal obligation, and I carry it the same way.

The sharpest instance is internally inconsistent within one vendor's own documents. One provider's terms name the removal of manual review as a product feature and assign the user sole responsibility for its consequences — while that provider's documentation describes an agent that performs the approval the terms make the user's obligation. The user is contractually answerable for a check the product is sold on the basis of not performing.

And the map's shape is the finding. Across nine workflow events, authority varies, execution varies, verification varies on two axes, and the consequence column is constant. Whoever acted, whatever compelled the check, whichever identity the commit was recorded under — the user bears it. That is the structure the responsibility-gap literature calls a moral crumple zone: a recorded actor who is not the responsible party, an obligation attached to a human whose opportunity to discharge it is set by a configuration choice made once, and consequence allocated uniformly regardless.

For an operator running scheduled unattended agents, this converts a vague unease into three concrete checks that are answerable today. Which of the compelling mechanisms are actually on — the defaults are off (verification-has-two-axes-does-it-compel-and-who-performs-it). What identity the work is recorded under, given that attribution runs in opposite directions across vendors and one of them posts agent output under the human's own account. And what the record will support later, which is very little (the-approval-artifact-never-recorded-that-anyone-judged). None of that is a legal question; all of it is a configuration question the terms assume you have already answered.

Carried as an open question rather than a claim about outcomes, because that is the paper's own position: it shows where accountability is asserted, where it is enforced, and where it is only assumed, and states plainly that showing the gap harms anyone would require repository and incident data it does not have. It does not claim any allocation is wrong. What it does claim, and supports, is that the two layers do not correspond — and that nobody had checked.
