The parameter you assumed is a measurement you skipped

This map catalogues the ways a reported difference fails to survive its own null. Most entries concern the comparison — a missing placebo arm, an unmeasured noise floor, a single initial draw standing in for a distribution. This one concerns a component parameter inside the system being reported, and it is the cheapest failure in the catalogue to commit and the cheapest to fix.

The setup is ordinary. A cost-optimal escalation policy routes suspicious steps to an LLM judge instead of judging every step, and the headline is that it recovers 83% of judge-every-step detection at 8% of the judge calls. That number cannot be computed without a judge quality, and the judge quality was stipulated — a noisy oracle at p_detect 0.90, p_false 0.02. Reasonable-sounding, unmeasured.

Then the author measured it. A real gemini-2.5-flash judge on a labelled subset of 161 distinct prompts scores p_detect 0.548 (95% CI 0.44–0.65) and p_false 0.052. Both stipulated values lie outside their measured intervals — the assumed detection rate is not merely optimistic, it is excluded by the data. Substituting the measured rates and re-running all five master seeds with everything else held fixed: detection recovery falls 82% → 43%, and the hypothesis that held at four of five seeds under the stipulation is supported at zero of five.

Three things make this worth a concept rather than a footnote.

The failure is compositional, and the survivors identify themselves. The cost claim — 6% of judge-every-step calls against the stipulated 8%, cost ratio 0.63 against 0.61 — barely moves, because the escalation policy's call volume does not depend on how good the judge is. So the substitution acts as a diagnostic: it partitions the paper's own claims into the ones that rested on the assumption and the ones that did not. Any pipeline result can be audited this way, and almost none are.

LLM-as-judge parameters are exactly the class of assumption most likely to be wrong in the optimistic direction. This vault already holds llm-judges-have-exploitable-biases, verifier-catch-and-false-alarm-rates-are-measurable, and validate-the-judge-before-you-trust-the-migration — validate the grader in both directions before the run. This is that rule applied to a simulated grader, which is a case the rule did not obviously cover: nobody thinks of a number in a cost model as an instrument needing validation. The measured judge lands at 0.548 detection, which is close to the 0.5–0.6 band this vault keeps recovering for LLM checkers (a-verifier-sharing-the-interpretation-shares-the-error at ~90% blind, the review-agent resolution figures at 54.8–72.9%, PRWeaver's auditors at 42–79%). 0.90 was never a plausible prior for an LLM judge on this vault's evidence.

The correction preserves the architecture and demotes the claim. The escalation layer stays worth building, and for a reason unrelated to the number that collapsed: judge and monitor fail on different classes — the measured judge is perfect on goal drift (21/21) and 0.18 on context corruption, where the monitor is 1.00. That is a complementarity argument, not a cheaper-approximation argument, and it survives the substitution because it was measured rather than assumed.

The rule. Any quantity in a pipeline result that was chosen rather than observed is a claim, and it should be reported as one — with the sensitivity of the headline to it. Where the quantity is another model's accuracy, measure it on your own distribution before you build a number on it, because the field's published rates for LLM checkers cluster far below where cost models tend to assume they sit. And when the measurement contradicts the assumption, the model to imitate is this one: re-run every seed, publish the collapsed number, and keep the claim that survived.

Bounds. One incident inside one single-author study — a receipted demonstration of a hazard, not a prevalence estimate for stipulated parameters in the literature. The measured judge is one model on one labelled subset of 161 prompts from this study's own task distribution, so 0.548 is that judge on that workload rather than a general figure. The re-run holds everything else fixed, which is what makes the attribution clean.
