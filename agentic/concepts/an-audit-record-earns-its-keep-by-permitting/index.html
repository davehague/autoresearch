The half of the record that says yes

This map's sources overwhelmingly evaluate a record by what it lets an investigator conclude after the fact — can the trajectory support what the agent claimed (a-passing-rerun-is-not-evidence-of-a-repair), can any signal identify which step mattered, does the audit line resolve to the write it used (an-audit-line-must-resolve-to-the-write-it-used). Every one of those is a retrospective use. This is the first measurement in the vault of a record's prospective function, and the sign is not what the framing predicts.

The setup is a runtime authorizer holding two stores: negative state (which arguments and actions originated in untrusted observations, carried persistently across steps) and positive state (evidence from calls that were previously authorized and executed successfully). Ablate them one at a time and they behave as opposites. Remove the negative store and attack success rises 20× and 18× while utility barely moves — suspicion is cheap security. Remove the positive store and attack success falls to 0.00% and 0.12%, while utility under attack drops around 10 and 13 points and benign completion on the dynamic benchmark falls 56.03% → 49.17%.

The mechanism is the useful part. Open-ended tasks produce values that do not come from the user: file identifiers, object IDs, paths resolved by a search. Those values are necessary to progress and, by construction, arrived through an untrusted channel. Without a record of prior authorized successful executions to bind them to, the authorizer has no legitimate source to point at, so every dynamic binding is treated conservatively and the task stalls. The paper's own summary is the sentence to keep: the origin state preserves external action-inducing origins while the evidence store provides positive support for legitimate runtime dependencies, and together they prevent persistent review from degenerating into indiscriminate rejection of dynamic execution.

Why this matters for how this vault argues about logging. The map keeps finding that agent records are thin in ways that only hurt after an incident — an approval stored as the same event type as a comment (the-approval-artifact-never-recorded-that-anyone-judged), a provenance field that resolves 3,478 occurrences to 62 identifiers, a configuration represented nowhere. Those are all arguments a team has to accept on faith, because the cost is paid later and by someone else. This one has an operating cost attached in the same run: the record is not a tax on the loop, it is a precondition for the loop doing dynamic work under any policy stricter than trust-everything. That reframes the pitch — an execution record is not only what you owe an auditor, it is what buys back the utility that governance takes.

It also puts a floor under the map's harder question. no-harness-produces-a-record-an-outsider-can-check finds nothing produces externally verifiable records; this finds that the internally verifiable one already earns its cost at runtime. The cheap version of the argument for building the record properly does not have to appeal to an auditor at all.

Bounds. A component ablation within one system, not a general claim about audit trails: what is measured is a specific store of authorized-and-executed tool interactions inside a specific authorizer, on two benchmarks and one backbone family for the ablation table. Because authorization decisions alter the agent's subsequent planning path, all ablation results are end-to-end effects rather than isolated mechanism contributions, which the authors state. The direction — safer-but-less-useful — is what removing any permissive component would predict; what the ablation adds is the size of the utility loss and the identification of which values fail without it. Nothing here measures whether such a record helps a human investigator, which is the use this map usually cares about.
