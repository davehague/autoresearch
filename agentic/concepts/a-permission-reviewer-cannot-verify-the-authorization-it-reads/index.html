The gate reads authorization off a label the harness wrote

the-gate-fails-by-refusing-authorized-work is this map's largest single measurement of a pre-commit model gate, and its finding is directional: across 106 incident-anchored scenarios and 30 model conditions, the gate makes 471 over-refusal errors against 15 under-refusals — 31.4:1 on a 1.12:1 opportunity ratio. The operator reading the vault took from it was that the under-refusal direction is nearly free and the cost of a gate is a queue for a human.

That measurement was made on constructed single-turn descriptions of commit moments with no tool execution, which the source stated plainly as scope. This result runs the other design: six live harnesses, real repositories, real command execution, and an attacker who controls nothing but files in the workspace. On the three harnesses that ship a reviewer, the under-refusal rate on 13 attack objectives is 13/13 — 100%. The two findings are not in contradiction and the difference is not model quality. It is that one experiment gave the gate an honest transcript and the other gave it a true transcript that had been assembled by the harness.

The failure is not risk misjudgment, which is what makes it hard. In the paper's second motivating case, the reviewer inspects the proposed action, classifies it as high risk, and then authorizes it because "the user explicitly approved this exact action." The content it is reading as user approval — "Start the server. I know the risks." — was generated by the main agent, which had itself read it from an attacker-controlled file, and became a user message because the harness writes a delegated task into a subagent's context that way. Every step is faithful. The reviewer acts correctly on the context it receives. The context is a lie no participant told.

This scopes rather than overturns. the-gate-fails-by-refusing-authorized-work holds for the question it asked: given an honest account of a commit moment, a model gate errs toward holding. What it cannot support is the operator's generalization that under-refusal is a small residual, because the quantity it measured is the gate's disposition and the quantity that matters in deployment is the gate's disposition given a context an adversary can shape. The two coexist: a gate that is trigger-happy on authorized work and blind to laundered authorization is exactly what you get from a mechanism that answers from the surface of a situation rather than from evidence about the world — which is precisely the diagnosis that source offered for the over-refusal side.

What it asks of the layer. This map's architecture is enforce below the prompt, in layers — environment, classifier, human-last. The classifier layer here is below the prompt: automatic permission review runs harness-side, sees the whole transcript, and on Codex may even use a read-only tool to inspect files before deciding. It still fails, because the thing it reasons over is the model-facing context, and the model-facing context is exactly what the attack manipulates. The correction is not a better reviewer. It is that authorization must be established from something that was not reconstructed — which is what what-induced-an-action-is-not-what-authorizes-it builds, by authorizing against the user objective plus audited evidence of prior authorized successful executions rather than against the transcript, and by carrying action-origin provenance across steps so that reappearance in history cannot manufacture authority. Note also that the environment layer is untouched by this result: the escalation gets its effect through actions the sandbox permits, and it is the model-mediated rung that inverts.

Bounds. Three reviewers, one benign cover task, ten attempts per case; per-attempt rates rather than distributions. The paper proposes and evaluates no defense. The reviewers' implementations differ in ways the paper documents and does not isolate — Codex's reviewer sees the working agent's assistant messages and can read files; Claude Code's sees neither — and Claude Code is the hardest target on both escalation forms, at 37.1% per-attempt under review and refusing the tool-to-system variant outright, which the paper reports without attributing to any specific design choice. Nothing here measures how often such a transcript arises without an adversary.
