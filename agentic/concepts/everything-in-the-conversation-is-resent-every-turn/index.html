The vendor says out loud what the token census measured

This map's largest arrival to date was a production census of 760.5M calls finding that the biggest term in the agent bill is whether the prefix cache survived (the-agent-token-bill-is-a-cache-lifecycle-problem). That was telemetry, inferred from outside. Here the vendor operating the harness states the mechanism directly, and the two accounts agree on every structural point.

The rule. "Everything that ends up in the conversation gets sent again on every turn after it, for the rest of the session." Files read, command output, prior tool results — none of it is consumed once. A session is a monotonically growing prefix, re-sent in full on every request, which is why the median call in the census carried 68K prompt tokens of which 63K were cached, and why an independent cost study's own eight days of agent work ran 97.1% cache reads with 6,805 uncached input tokens in total and an input:output ratio of 437:1 (the-scaffolding-outspends-the-tool-interface). Three sources, three vantage points, one shape: agentic spend is a context problem, not a generation problem.

The prices that make it survivable, and the one that does not. A cached read costs 0.1× input, so the marginal cost of a turn is roughly the new material. Writing to cache costs 2×. Output costs about 5× input, for a stated mechanical reason — decode requires one full model pass per token while prefill processes all input in parallel — which is why the 5× multiplier applies to a quantity that barely varies across harnesses and the 0.1× applies to the quantity that varies by a factor of twenty.

The operational half is that the cache key is a prefix. Requests assemble as tool definitions → system prompt → conversation with the project rule file at its front, and matching runs from the beginning forward, so any change in that prefix invalidates everything behind it. The invalidating actions are ordinary product controls: switching model, changing effort, toggling fast mode. Cache lifetime is one hour on subscription and five minutes on API keys. This is the vendor-side confirmation of the census's most consequential number — hit rate collapsing to 8% after a model switch — and it converts that from an observed correlation into a stated design property. A mid-session router is spending a cache, and this map's standing warning that nobody has priced that term (the-agent-token-bill-is-a-cache-lifecycle-problem) is now a warning about a documented mechanism rather than an inferred one.

The practices the post derives are unmeasured and mostly obvious once the rule is stated — clear between tasks, fix model and effort at session start, attach files rather than making the agent read them, quiet the noisy commands or push them into a subagent whose output never enters the parent window, inspect what is loaded before starting, compact while the cache is still warm. The one with real architectural content is the last consequence: one long session costs meaningfully more than the same work split across several short ones, because context that is cheap to re-send is not free to re-process. For scheduled and unattended work, that argues for many bounded sessions over one long-lived agent, which is the opposite of the direction persistent-agent designs pull.

Bounds. A vendor engineering post about the vendor's own product — no experiment, no workload, no comparison. The multipliers and TTLs are authoritative for this stack because the party stating them sets them, and transfer to no other. None of the six practices is measured, so nothing says how much any of them saves or where the advice reverses. The 30,000-character output threshold and the request-assembly order are implementation constants subject to change.
