Both errors were plausible, both were arithmetic, and neither shows up in the ratio

The routing literature this map tracks assumes the cost axis is the solid one — quality is contested, dollars are dollars. This is the first source here to audit its own cost measurement and find it wrong by roughly 2×, in a direction that flattered AI both times.

The first error is the one that generalizes. They estimated AI spend by counting tokens and multiplying by published per-token rates for the model they believed the coding agent was using. The agent ran under a flat monthly subscription, where token volume has no effect on spend at all. The resulting US$22.74 was, in their words, "a plausible figure computed from a pricing model that did not apply" — and it was concentrated enough in one phase (68% of the apparent AI budget) to support a headline conclusion, model selection dominates the economics of AI-assisted development, which the billing records dissolve. Real tooling cost for the whole project: US$69.

That failure mode is live for anyone reading this vault's own numbers. Half the cost figures in circulation — including some this map carries — are token counts multiplied by a rate card, computed by people whose agent is on a subscription. agent-cost-spans-orders-of-magnitude and price-agent-work-per-commit both quantify spend without stating which billing regime produced it. The ordering rule the authors extract is blunt and correct: establish whether each tool is flat-rate, metered or hybrid before optimizing anything, read the billing dashboard rather than inferring from tokens, and record metered charges as they occur. Under a flat plan, every token optimization in this map's allocation strand is worth exactly zero.

The second error is the denominator. The human counterfactual was priced with national salary surveys skewed to the largest metropolitan markets — about 85% above the local rates — moving the counterfactual from US$5,678 to US$3,005. Combined, the two corrections take 19.4× to 9.9×, and the authors argue 9.9× is the better number not because it is smaller but because every term now traces to a billing record or a stated rate.

The honesty extends to the remaining softness: 329 counterfactual hours are still an estimate of work never performed, by students rather than the professionals used to price it, which is the same class of error that produced the 19.4×. Their own conclusion is that even 9.9× may not be final.

One secondary observation worth keeping, because it is a routing fact rather than a cost fact: delegation was steeply uneven and stayed uneven — ~95% AI on code and test writing, ~80% on debugging, ~40% on prompt design, ~20% on architectural decisions. The tool absorbed implementation and left the decisions that shape the system with the humans, which is the same boundary cheap-code-costly-judgment draws from the other side of the ledger.

Bounds. A single ongoing case study, six students, self-reported effort subject to hindsight bias, a retrospective counterfactual. Not a controlled productivity measurement; offered as an order-of-magnitude signal from one case with a released replication package. Graded T3.
