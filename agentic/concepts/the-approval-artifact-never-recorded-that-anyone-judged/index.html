The record says a party with authority clicked, and it has never said more than that

This map's method is to audit from artifacts rather than from claims. This is the case where the artifact was never load-bearing in the first place, and nobody noticed because a human was standing behind it.

An approval records that a party holding authority performed an action the platform stores as approval. It does not record what that party understood. And the platform makes even less of a distinction than that: approval is logged as submission of a review — the same event type as a comment or a request for changes — so the record does not separate endorsing a change from remarking on it. The audit log does carry fields for whether an actor is an agent, whether an actor is a bot, and an agent session identifier, and the documentation defines none of them. The fields exist and their meaning does not.

The paper's thesis is deliberately smaller about agents and larger about the arrangement they entered. The thinness of the approval artifact was measured a decade ago: only about 15% of reviewer comments indicate a possible defect while at least 50% is maintainability feedback; over 80% of changes involve at most one comment-resolution iteration and 70% are committed within a day of going out for review. Reviewers were forming substantive judgements and the artifact recorded none of their content. What agentic tooling changed is that this thinness moved from a contingent property of how people work to a documented property of what a product does. A reviewer who approved in twenty seconds in 2018 could have read the diff and did not; a vendor now documents a product that stands at the approval event and emits the same artifact under configured thresholds, with no party capable of reading the diff present at all (verification-has-two-axes-does-it-compel-and-who-performs-it).

Attribution is worse than absent — it is inconsistent in direction. One provider records the agent as commit author with the assigning human as co-author, signs the commits, and links each commit message to the agent session logs. Another posts agent output under the human's own account, distinguished only by a label in the body of the reply. Both are current, documented, and applied to the same artifact type on the same platform. There is no standardised trailer for machine authorship anywhere; one provider repurposes the co-authorship trailer — documented elsewhere purely as a multiple-human mechanism — and that attribution can be turned off by configuration.

For this map the consequence is direct and unwelcome. Every result here assumes the trajectory or the artifact answers a question later. agents-under-instrument-the-code-they-write is the same erosion inside the code; govern-the-agents-configuration-as-a-versioned-object is the same hole one layer up, where no tooling represents the configuration a trace was produced against. This is the hole at the commit layer: an increasingly agent-authored repository whose provenance record is optional, inconsistent across vendors, undefined in its own field documentation, and unable to distinguish a comment from an endorsement. A first-wrong-step analysis that reaches back to "who approved this and on what basis" runs out of substrate before it gets there.

The constructive form is a schema requirement rather than a practice: an event type that distinguishes approval from comment, a defined and non-disableable machine-authorship marker, and a record of which party performed a compelled check — none of which any vendor in the collection currently emits. Adjacent work designs artifacts that would attest an agent stayed inside its authorisation; the paper is precise that this is a different object, quoting that such a certificate is "silent on whether the contract was wise." What is missing is not a record that the agent behaved, but a record that the party bearing the consequence formed a judgement.

This is a reading of published documentation, not of repository data. It establishes what the record can carry, not what teams do with it, and the author states plainly that showing harm would need incident data this study does not have.
