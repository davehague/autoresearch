The failure direction depends on which chair the model is sitting in

Nearly everything this map holds about permission is measured on the acting agent, and it says the same thing every time: the agent acts when it should not. underspecified-targets-drive-unsafe-actions has 55.8–67.8% of acted runs violating a boundary; agents-do-not-refuse-a-prohibited-contribution has native refusal at 0% for four shipped agent-model pairs even after the violated clause is quoted back; permission-prompts-fail-open-under-fatigue has the human approver rubber-stamping. The map's whole architecture — enforce below the prompt, in layers — follows from under-refusal being the failure mode.

Move the same models into the gate's chair and the sign flips. Given the request, the proposed action and the evidence on hand — information sufficient, authorization present — 30 conditions produce 471 over-refusal misses against 15 under-refusal misses, on denominators that differ by only 12%. That is not a corpus artifact and the authors bound it three ways rather than asserting it: excluding the 17 rows with an inferred risk flag still gives 19.4% against 1.25%; relabelling against an unadjudicated three-rater majority widens it to 41.1% against 0.36%; excluding two arguable proceed rows gives 25.5% against 1.0%.

So the two results are not in contradiction and the reconciliation is the finding: an agent asked to do work under-refuses, and a model asked to authorize work over-refuses. Both failures are the same underlying behaviour — the model answers from the surface of the situation rather than from the evidence in front of it — pointed in opposite directions by the role. That is a scope condition this map did not have, and it constrains the map's own remedy: the layered enforcement it recommends is largely built out of model judgements, and this is the first cross-vendor measurement of how those judgements fail when they are the layer.

Where they fail is the sharp part. Models score 98.5% on the famous public-harm anchors and 83.3% on a risk-resolved commit, but the hardest rows are the ones where the risk was real and the evidence resolved it: a benefits notice whose statutory-basis, eligibility-match and due-process services have all signed; a credit limit increase with completed and signed fairness controls, held wrongly by every condition in the grid. Two degenerate baselines frame how weak this is — always-hold scores 47.2% and always-proceed 52.8% — so the grid is genuinely doing work, and still spends its entire error budget on one side.

For an operator this is the cost of autonomy stated as a number. A gate that holds 28% of authorized work does not fail loudly; it converts an unattended agent into a queue for a human, which is permission-prompts-fail-open-under-fatigue arriving by the other road — approval fatigue caused by the gate rather than by the agent. And it re-prices gate-actions-with-a-contextual-classifier-dial, whose ~4% block rate is measured on organic traffic rather than on a proceed-labelled adversarial set: the two numbers answer different questions, and nobody has run the Cursor-style classifier against a set constructed so that holding is wrong.

The training target the authors name is the right one and worth stating in this map's vocabulary: not "be less safe" but "detect the risk trigger, then condition the hold on whether the risk remains unresolved." A gate that cannot tell a resolved risk from an unresolved one is not a safety control, it is a keyword filter with a confidence score.

Scope, stated by the authors. The scenarios are constructed, single-turn descriptions of commit moments — the model reads the boundary, it does not execute tools, so live-harness behaviour can differ. Nothing here measures long sessions or what an agent does after a denial. The proceed and hold pools are matched on opportunity count but not on authored difficulty, and no per-item difficulty rating exists to test it, so the asymmetry is robust to the composition objection but not to an item-difficulty selection effect. Model scores are snapshots of dated endpoints frozen 2026-06-08, and the released 106 starts a contamination clock on publication.
