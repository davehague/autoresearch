Look at what your agent gets wrong before deciding what to build

This map's standing position is that the harness is a large term — the-loop-implementation-is-a-larger-term-than-the-model-tier, move-task-difficulty-from-the-model-to-the-harness, decompose-the-harness-into-separately-liftable-layers. What it has not had is a cheap prior test for whether harness work will pay off on your particular stack, and teams have been answering that by building something and measuring after.

The instrument here is nearly free. Failure attribution runs on two evidence channels the run already produces:

  • Verifier-side — the scored submission, the resulting environment state, and a stage-level execution record including stages that did not run. That last clause is the one people omit and the one that matters: it surfaces a patch colliding with a held-out test patch, which appears in the transcript only as an ordinary failure.
  • Budget-side — termination reason and resource use, which distinguishes an attempt cut off at a ceiling from a completed-but-wrong submission. Two failures that look identical on a pass/fail metric and need opposite fixes.

No labels, no human review, no separate benchmark. Run the agent, read the rollouts, count the defects.

What the counts tell you. Composition varies enough to invalidate any default: non-compiling submissions are 70% of C++ rollout defects and under 10% of Go's. A static scaffold has to pick one guess and be wrong most places, which is the concrete reason part-of-the-harness-term-is-task-shape-fit holds. Total mass tells you whether to bother at all: Python's detector-covered rate is 5.6%, the lowest of eight, and Python is precisely the cell where evolution returns nothing under any model. Where the mass is near zero, so is the gain.

And read the model axis too. GPT-5-mini's evolved memory contains no test-file rule on languages where every other model writes one first — the loop diagnosed independently that this model does not commit that defect. Yet GPT-5-mini is the weakest of the three under a static scaffold. So a low defect profile does not mean a strong model; it means the failures are elsewhere (localization, synthesis) and no rule will reach them. This is the diagnostic form of harness-asymmetry-manufactures-model-differences: two models can need entirely different harnesses without one being better, and a shared scaffold quietly advantages whichever model's defect profile it happens to match.

The rule. Before writing a harness rule, spend one rollout batch counting defects. The profile tells you whether harness engineering has headroom on your stack, which rule to write first, and — if the mass is near zero — that the money should go to the model, the task decomposition, or the retrieval layer instead. This is grade-process-defects-not-just-outcomes used as a planning instrument rather than as a scoring one.

Bounds, and the authors are careful about them. The detectors cover only part of the recoverable space, so defect mass is an indicator, not an estimate of headroom — a cell with low measured mass may still have recoverable failures nobody has built a detector for. The three automatic detectors are specific to code repair with hidden tests (test-file collision, non-compiling submission, unrun target test); porting the method to another domain means finding that domain's equivalents, and nothing here says how. Attribution is advisory rather than authoritative: a misattribution costs one iteration because the resulting edit fails its own falsifiable prediction and gets reverted — an elegant property that depends on the loop having that validation step at all.
