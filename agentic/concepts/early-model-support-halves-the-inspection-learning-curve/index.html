The measurable cost of assistance showed up in the second session, not the first

The sibling finding in this source — that assisted detection was about 8% worse — is the one with the headline and the overlapping credibility intervals. This one is the one the design was actually built to detect, and it is the one the authors treat as clear.

Crossover experiments carry period, sequence and carryover as known threats, and the standard practice is to model them so they can be discounted. Here they carry the result. Everyone improved between sessions, which is ordinary maturation. But the group that met the task for the first time with a model in the room improved by roughly 7% where the group that met it unassisted improved by roughly 12%. Same task, same documents, same total exposure. The difference is which session had the assistant in it.

Why this outlives the −8%. A one-session detection penalty is a fact about a tool and a task, and a better tool or a better prompt could plausibly erase it. A halved learning rate is a fact about what the person retains after the tool is gone, and it does not have an obvious tooling fix. The authors' account — early reliance short-circuits the reflective process through which a novice internalizes inspection strategy and quality heuristics — is a hypothesis, but the measurement is a before/after on the same people.

Where this bears on how a team uses this map. Everything on this map assumes a human who can tell a good specification from a bad one: agents-are-weak-at-spec-critique makes that human the gate, converge-the-spec-against-the-code-before-freezing-it makes them the arbiter, emergent-specs-corrupt-the-artifact makes them the one who notices the artifact rotting. This is a measurement of the pipeline that produces that human, and it says the pipeline runs slower when assistance arrives before the skill does. For a team that is currently onboarding engineers into an agent-assisted workflow, the sequencing is the actionable part: manual first, assisted second — the authors' explicit teaching recommendation and their explicit practice recommendation (restrict model use to post-inspection review rather than initial detection) are the same rule stated twice.

There is a wider pattern this belongs to and the authors name it: programming students given ChatGPT frequently entered generate-copy-submit cycles without deeper reasoning, and increased interaction time did not translate into improved performance — despite longer time on task and high perceived usefulness. The perception/measurement gap is the same one recorded on this atlas's own maps for productivity (faster-ai-review-is-not-better-review) and it keeps reappearing wherever someone measures the human rather than the artifact.

Bounds. One cohort of 34 bachelor students at one university, two sessions, two documents; there is no third session, so what is measured is the difference between the first and second exposure and not a learning curve. The effect is an interaction term in a Bayesian regression reported as an approximate percentage without an interval in the text. Skill and motivation were deliberately not collected, so a group-level difference in either could in principle produce the asymmetry despite the Mahalanobis matching on inspection and LLM proficiency. Nothing establishes that the deficit persists — a third unassisted session might close it — and the authors advance this as an implication for teaching rather than a measured durable harm. And it is a claim about novices acquiring a skill, with no bearing on practitioners who already have it.
