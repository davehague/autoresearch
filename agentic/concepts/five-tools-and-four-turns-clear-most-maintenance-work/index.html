The floor, finally written down and executable

This map has spent months measuring what harness components add — evolved scaffolds, tool-surface restrictions, compaction strategies, routing — almost always as a delta against an unspecified baseline. It has not had a floor: a minimal agent someone published, with the code, that you can point at and say "below this line is not an agent, and above it is what you are paying for." Ark is that artifact. Eighteen lines of loop, five tools, a four-turn history window, and it clears 8 of 10 small maintenance tasks on gpt-5.4-mini for under fifty cents total.

Read it as a floor and not as a result, because it is a toy benchmark and a single run, and the number that matters is not 8/10. Three things transfer.

The context strategy is aggressively destructive and it did not obviously cost anything at this scale. Ark keeps the last four iterations and throws the rest away, where Codex CLI and OpenCode both run LLM-summarisation compaction. On ten small tasks that is invisible — which is precisely the boundary condition the vault wants marked. prune-and-summarize-beats-full-history and compaction-is-a-serving-event-not-a-context-tactic describe machinery whose value is a function of horizon, and here is the horizon below which it buys nothing. The mean task took 7.1 tool calls; nothing was ever going to fall out of a four-turn window.

Five tools was enough, and the failures say where it stops. restrict-the-tool-surface-to-cut-cost-not-capability argued a narrow surface is cheaper without being weaker; Ark is the extreme point of that claim and mostly supports it. But look at refactor-03: the agent renamed a public function in its definition and in the visible tests, missed one module's import, passed the public suite and failed the hidden one at test collection. That is exactly the failure a keyword-only retrieval layer with no regex and no structural index would produce — review-the-diff-and-search-all-usages names the discipline it lacked, and structural-index-beats-grep-at-lower-cost names the component that would have fixed it. The other failure is the same family in miniature: handled the visible cases, wrong exception type on the unseen one. Both failures are propagation, not reasoning — which is the argument for spending the next 500 lines of harness on retrieval rather than on the loop.

The cost line is the one to keep. Under US$0.50 for ten maintenance tasks, ~3,300 tokens each. This vault holds agent-cost-spans-orders-of-magnitude; Ark marks the bottom of that range with a working agent attached, and it is a useful sanity check against any harness whose per-task bill is three orders of magnitude higher without a matching capability claim.

Bounds, and they are the reason this is graded T3 and single-study. Ten hand-written tasks on a synthetic 8-file application, one run per task, one model, no ablation — nothing here isolates what the five-tool surface or the four-turn window contributed, because nothing else was tried, and the author notes directly that non-determinism means the numbers may not reproduce. The tasks each touch 2–4 files in a codebase small enough to read entirely, so this measures the floor of small maintenance work and says nothing about the regime where the-80-percent-problem-is-a-context-gap bites. Treat 8/10 as an existence proof for the architecture, not as a score.
