A ranking is a family of claims, and this one is unanswerable at its own resolution

This map holds a long line of results about whether a benchmark number means what it appears to mean — contamination, judge bias, reward hacking, disclosure. All of them ask whether an individual score is trustworthy. This asks a question the map has not asked: how many claims does a leaderboard make?

The answer follows from what a total order asserts. A reader who accepts that rank 3 beats rank 4 and rank 4 beats rank 5 has already accepted that rank 3 beats rank 5 — that is what an order is for. So 134 submissions license 8,911 pairwise claims, the 36 tied pairs included, because the table still prints one name above the other and "no more than chance" is itself the finding for such a pair. Nobody submitted 8,911 claims. Nobody corrected for them. The multiplicity is created by the presentation.

And at the declared unit it cannot be paid for. Instances nest in 12 repositories. Twelve clusters admit 2¹² sign assignments, so the sharpest two-sided p-value any comparison on this data can produce is 0.000488 — which sits above Bonferroni's threshold for a family of 8,911. Holm and Bonferroni both support 0 of 8,911. Benjamini–Yekutieli, the honest offline comparator for arbitrary dependence, keeps 2,038. Naive p < 0.05 keeps 6,987. Move to the instance level and Bonferroni jumps to 6,467. The leaderboard's own ordering is legible to a reader as settled fact and is, at the resolution its keys declare, almost entirely unadjudicated.

The narrower reading does not rescue it. Restrict to the 133 adjacent-rank claims — the minimal family a defender might argue the table actually asserts — and naive p < 0.05 supports 2, while 0.05/133 is already below the sharpest p-value the data can produce, so no adjacent claim rejects under Bonferroni either.

This is the same shape as agent-benchmarks-underdisclose-their-harness and the disclosure line generally: the artifact that the field treats as the summary of a result systematically omits the thing needed to interpret it. There the omission is the harness; here it is the unit of analysis and the family size. It also sits directly against the practice this map keeps finding — a-null-measured-once-is-a-point-estimate and the rest — because a leaderboard is the most-read evaluation artifact in agentic software engineering and it has never been treated as a multiple-comparison problem at all.

The second corpus is the calibration, and the author says why it matters. BIG-Bench Hard keeps 19 of 26 claims. "A corpus on which every case study returned zero would measure the instrument's own severity and nothing about the analyses." A paper with real, large, plentiful effects survives; a ranking of 134 near-neighbours does not. That asymmetry is the finding — the procedure is not uniformly destructive, it is destructive exactly where the claims are dense and the separations are small, which is what a leaderboard is.

A smaller result worth keeping for judge and metric work. On causal_judgement and ruin_names, two-sided McNemar clears 0.05 (p = 0.0474, p = 0.0498) while direct prompting outscores chain-of-thought on the raw counts — a two-sided test rejects on large discordance in either direction and reports nothing about which. The system never checks that a rejected claim's observed sign matches its label, and the author surfaces the gap rather than burying it. Any pipeline that reads "significant" as "in the hypothesized direction" inherits this.

Bounds. Retrospective reconstruction with the outcomes in hand; the family is chosen after the fact rather than declared in advance. The conditional-super-uniformity assumption the sequential guarantee needs is assumed and unverified for both corpora, because nobody knows their true dependence structure — it can only be declared. Synthetic validation is reported and is unflattering to everyone: at the instance level every procedure tested, including the one this paper proves sound, exceeds its own α under a complete null. And the guarantee itself is weak — mFDR_η is a ratio of expectations that implies neither the false discovery rate nor the family-wise error rate; in a 4,000-trial simulation of equal-skill models where every discovery is false by construction, the raw false share reached 1.00 while mFDR_η read 0.0031 and passed.
