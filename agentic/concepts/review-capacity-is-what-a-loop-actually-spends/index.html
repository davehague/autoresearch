The gate is already automated on both sides, and it closes in about seventy seconds

This map's second strand has been arguing that review throughput, not review accuracy, is where agentic review actually binds. Here is the population-scale picture of what that looks like now that both sides of the pull request are machines.

The scale is no longer negligible in absolute terms even while it is a minority in relative ones. 248,641 agent-authored PRs received at least one AI-attributed review — 8.8% of the 2.83M identified — across 10,345 repositories, with cross-product review (a different vendor's agent doing the reviewing) at 45,269, or 1.6%. Both grew by more than two orders of magnitude over three quarters of 2025. The methodological consequence the authors draw is the one that matters for everything else in this vault: empirical software engineering can no longer sample GitHub as though the population were human-authored. Any study of review latency, comment density, or PR quality that does not stratify on agent attribution is now mixing two populations.

The structural finding is who reviews whom, and it is nearly bimodal. Copilot-authored PRs are reviewed by Copilot 95.7% of the time; Cursor-, Google Jules- and Claude Code-authored PRs are reviewed by a different product essentially 100% of the time (0.0%, 0.0%, 0.5% same-product). Codex alone is balanced at 54.1%. Whether your generated code gets checked by the same vendor's model or a different one is not a choice most teams are making — it falls out of which product they picked.

The latency number is the one to carry. Median PR-open to first AI review is 1.2 minutes cross-product and 4.7 same-product. In a human review pipeline the gap between authoring and reviewing is where the design pressure lives; here it is seventy-two seconds. And the authors refuse the obvious reading of their own direction: cross-product being faster looks like evidence against "a product rubber-stamps its own," but the reviewer-level rows show the aggregate gap is compositional — fast reviewers are over-represented in the cross-product set, and Copilot (median 17.7 min, p75 74 min) dominates the same-product one. They also flag that timestamp completeness differs sharply between the groups (79.2% vs 31.9%), so the comparison is complete-case and reported descriptively. That restraint is why the note is graded strong: the numbers are large and the interpretation is small.

The finding with the longest reach is that the reviewer's output depends on who wrote the code. Hold CodeRabbit fixed and vary the authoring agent: its refactor-comment share moves from 10.5% on Copilot-authored PRs to 35.0% on Claude Code-authored PRs. The paper is explicit that this may reflect the PRs each agent produces rather than reviewer behavior, and that the categories are CodeRabbit's own self-declared labels rather than validated defect types — so this is not a claim that one agent writes worse code. What survives regardless: downstream tooling that aggregates or summarizes AI review output will report systematically different pictures depending on which agent authored the change, under an identical reviewer. Any dashboard counting "refactor findings per PR" as a quality signal is partly measuring authorship.

Read together with faster-ai-review-is-not-better-review, which found across 1.02M PRs that AI participation buys speed and no measured quality, this is that result's mechanism arriving at the extreme: the loop now closes faster than a human could open the diff, and nothing in this dataset says a defect is more likely to be caught.

Bounds, and they are heavy. Attribution is signature-based and asymmetric — far more reliable on the reviewer side (vendor bot logins) than on the author side (commit trailers agents may not emit and squash merges strip), so absolute counts are lower bounds and rates describe the attributable population rather than all AI-authored PRs. "Closed-loop" requires an AI on each side but does not require the absence of human review; the mined stream is AI-attributed events only, so some of these PRs certainly also got human review and the study cannot see it. "Cross-product" is product-level, not model-level — two products may share a foundation model. Public GitHub only. Everything is observational, confounded by PR size, language, repository and product integration behavior, and the authors decline every causal reading available to them.
