Summaries to find it, the original text to decide, and the instruction that caused it

Given the attribution ceiling in naming-the-responsible-role-is-a-different-target-from-localizing-the-step, the useful question is what construction moves it. Three design choices here do, and each contradicts something a harness builder would reasonably try first.

Search broadly on summaries; decide on the original text. The trajectory is partitioned into consecutive segments by rule — no language model, fixed character and step limits, five steps of overlap context, original step IDs preserved — and one LLM call per segment produces a summary plus candidate error step IDs. A second call stitches adjacent summaries into a subgoal-organised outline. Then the original logged text of every retained candidate is pulled back for the final decision. The compression buys the search; the raw text carries the judgement. This is the same asymmetry search-the-harness-with-raw-traces-not-summaries found in harness optimization, arriving in diagnosis: a summary is adequate to point at a region and inadequate to convict a step.

Then trace the candidate back to the instruction that produced it. For a candidate emitted by an executor or verifier, retrieve the nearest preceding handoff instruction addressed to that role, and compare the two. If the instruction already contains the decisive error and the later step carries it out unrepaired, the instruction is the root; if the recipient departed from the instruction or introduced a new decisive error, the candidate stays. This is the mechanism that beats the baselines' attraction to the late executor, and it is only available because the labelling rule made handoffs first-class. It rhymes with naming-a-coordinator-creates-no-structural-leadership from the diagnostic side — the decisive event in a multi-role run is often a handoff, and a method that only inspects the step where the symptom appeared cannot reach it.

And a programmatic validator sits after the model, not around it. Before scoring, the predicted role must occur in the trajectory, the root and supporting IDs must refer to recorded steps, and quoted handoff text must actually occur in the referenced instruction; an invalid response gets one retry with validation feedback, after which unsupported fields are removed and the output flagged or marked an abstention. The authors are careful about what this buys: output validity and textual provenance, not semantic correctness. That distinction is worth stealing wholesale — it is the cheap half of audit-agent-claims-against-trajectory-evidence, mechanically enforceable, and it turns a confident hallucinated step index into an abstention rather than a wrong answer.

The result that should change a build decision is the negative one. Dependency-guided search over decisions, tool outputs and messages reaches 2.8% exact and 12.5% within-five — below plain all-at-once prompting on both. Binary search over the trajectory is nearly as bad at 3.4%. The structured-traversal instinct, which is the one an engineer reaches for when a trajectory is too long to read, is the one that loses here. The authors bound this to the evaluated implementation rather than to dependency-based diagnosis in general, and that caveat is real — but the direction is consistent with one-hop-dependency-inspection-misses-transitive-config-impact on the configuration side: dependency edges recovered after the fact are a weaker representation of what actually coupled than they look.

Carried as suggestive rather than strong for a specific reason: RCTA is not ablated. Candidate recall, handoff tracing, the backward instruction check and the validator are four mechanisms bundled into one number, and nothing here says which does the work. At 24.1% exact accuracy the method is also nowhere near deployable as an unattended root-cause reporter — it is a better instrument for building corpora and triaging, not an answer.
