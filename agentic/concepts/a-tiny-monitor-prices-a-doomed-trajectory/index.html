The stopping decision is worth 0.6B parameters and no more

This map's thesis is that agent spend is a sequential learned-budget-allocation problem — over models, over iterations, over actions — and that the allocator has to be a trained artifact fitted to the traffic it serves (a-routing-policy-must-be-learned-from-rollouts, route-with-a-classifier-trained-on-live-traffic). The allocation this measures is the crudest and most valuable one: stop paying for this run.

The result that matters commercially is that the allocator is nearly free and does not want to be bigger. A 0.6B monitor — under 2 GB of VRAM, 15–52× cheaper per token than the policies it watches, adding 0.1–0.6% compute overhead — reclaims 20.4% of total execution tokens at a 5% false-positive budget, against 12.5% for a log-probability-based predictor and 11.4% for a step-count control. Scaling the backbone to 4B produces no improvement and often less recall, which the authors read as prefix-level failure signals saturating early. That is the opposite of the usual finding in this map, where the monitoring or routing component keeps wanting more capability, and it makes the economics unambiguous: the check is a rounding error against the thing it is checking.

Two properties make it deployable rather than a benchmark artifact.

It reads text, not internals. Input is the issue statement plus a window of the last eight thought/action/observation steps and a pin of the latest patch-producing step. No policy logits, no hidden states — so it monitors closed APIs, where the log-probability baseline is structurally inapplicable, and still reclaims 16.0% at the tight budget on Gemini 3 Flash. It is trained on one policy's trajectories and transferred, unchanged, to three others across two families.

The dial is a false-positive budget, not a threshold. The operator sets the fraction of would-succeed runs they are willing to abort, and the controller maximizes detection subject to it. That is the right shape for this map's ledger, because it prices the intervention in the currency the buyer actually holds: 5% collateral damage buys 15–20% of the token bill; 25% buys 49% of tokens and 68.3% of failure detection but is only tolerable when paired with a recovery step (carry-the-edits-not-the-narrative-across-a-restart).

The input ablation carries a lesson beyond this system. Removing the recent window costs 13 points of recall; adding a global trajectory summary costs three. Coarse summarization of a trajectory is worse than not having it, in a monitor whose whole job is to read trajectories — the same shape as the overlay design's rejection of text summaries, and a caution for anyone compressing agent history for a downstream consumer.

Bounds. One benchmark, one scaffold, 100 test instances; the monitor is trained on the same benchmark it is evaluated on, so transfer is demonstrated across policies and not across repositories or task distributions — the number to expect on your own traffic is unmeasured, and this map's own position is that such an allocator must be fitted to the deployment it serves. The token savings assume the aborted runs would have failed, which is exactly the FPR the budget is bounding. Energy and carbon figures are modelled from parameter-count FLOPs rather than measured. And the 20.4% is a saving only at the 5% operating point, which by construction recovers nothing — the accuracy gains live at budgets where the net token spend goes up by 20.5–43.8%.
