The reviewer agent knows where, the static LLM knows what — and neither is good enough

This map has treated reviewer quality as one axis, tuned as a single dial. Bugbot's 40 experiments moved flag volume and flag quality together (review-agent-precision-is-a-tunable-not-a-given); a production gate priced both sides at catch 0.20 / fix 0.75 / false-alarm ≈0. Useful numbers, but they aggregate a distinction this study pulls apart: the reviewer has to decide what is wrong and where it is, and repository access improves exactly one of those.

Agents that explore the codebase land comments on the right lines at 20–22% against 7–9% for every diff-only reviewer — a factor of about three, and the single largest differentiator in the study. On whether the comment identifies a real issue, the diff-only reviewers are ahead: the best static LLM scores 21.44% against the best agent's 15.10%. Read carefully, that is not "agents are worse reviewers." The static LLMs are producing three to ten times as many comments and scoring relevance per comment, and their volume is itself a maintenance cost this map already prices — 9,060 comments across 362 tasks is not review, it is noise with a good hit rate. But it does mean the marginal value of exploring the repository is positional, and nothing in these numbers says grounding improves judgment.

The instruction that follows is a reallocation of effort, and the paper states it plainly: improving repository navigation likely yields greater gains than scaling model capability. That is a concrete answer to a question the map has been asking since the capacity-ceiling findings — given that the reviewer agent is now the gate in most deployments and its false-negative rate is unmeasured almost everywhere, what actually moves it? Not the model. The retrieval layer. Which puts this claim in direct contact with context-engineering's retrieval results, notably structural-index-beats-grep-at-lower-cost — a graph-of-definitions index bought +39.6 pp of localization inside a coding agent at lower cost per solve. Nobody has yet put a structural index behind a reviewer agent and measured the localization column. That is a cheap, obvious, unrun experiment.

The absolute numbers deserve their own sentence. Requiring both criteria, the best production reviewer in the study scores 5.81% against human review comments on real repositories. The paper is careful that this understates real utility — agents surface valid issues the benchmark's humans never wrote, and those count as failures here — but the direction of that bias is the same direction as every optimistic story about reviewer agents, and the vault's standing position is that the false-negative side is unmeasured. This is the closest thing to a measurement, and it is not flattering.

Caveats: human alignment is scored by an LLM-as-a-judge and is the noisier of the two columns — which matters, because it is the column on which agentic reviewers lose. Ground truth is a human review comment, so an unwritten-but-real issue scores as a miss. 362 tasks, one harness, one point in time, and the agentic systems are production products whose versions move weekly.
