Nominal leadership is not structural leadership, and the failures sit on seams nobody owns

The cheapest way to assign leadership to a team of agents is a sentence in a prompt, and it is the way most orchestration frameworks do it. If the label worked, the communication graph would show it: traffic concentrating on the coordinator, and success rising on the tasks that need arbitration most.

Neither happens. Filtering each run's message graph to the channels carrying a disproportionate share of an agent's traffic leaves 0 of 1,170 channels at eight agents on the distributed task and 2 of 1,077 on the chained task — under the coordinator condition as much as the flat one. The dense graph is dense everywhere and the sparse graph is sparse everywhere; neither concentrates on a hub. Whatever the prompt says, no hub emerges.

On success the story is the one this vault keeps finding: the apparent effect was a sampling artifact, and the artifact ran in two directions. At four agents the coordinator leads 8/10 against 5/10 — until the flat condition's second byte-identical collection is pooled, at which point it is 8/10 against 12/20 (p=0.42). At eight agents the direction reverses, and the one contrast that survives multiple-comparison correction across six cells is the flat team beating the coordinator, 20/20 against 6/10. That reversal also fails to reproduce: the 244-run sealed replication finds flat and coordinator level under every file policy. So both the effect and its apparent reversal were noise, and only the deliberate same-configuration duplication (a-multi-agent-configuration-is-a-distribution-not-a-run) made that visible. This is one-run-scores-an-implementation-not-an-idea applied to a topology choice, and it is why coordination-deltas-need-a-measured-noise-floor is a precondition for this map rather than a footnote in it.

The constructive half is the eight-step chain, and it is the clearest failure mechanism this map has. The task succeeds 9/10 at two and four agents and fails all ten at eight. Every failure sits on the same seam: compute_tax passes its tax on unrounded, format_invoices rounds every figure once at the end, and each agent holds one half of that convention. At two and four agents one agent owns both steps and reconciles the halves internally. At eight the seam falls between two different owners and no agent holds responsibility for it. The teams were not silent — rounding was discussed in all ten runs, and those teams messaged more than the four-agent ones. Talking more did not close an interface nobody owned. The mismatch is invisible to each agent, whose own step is correct, and invisible in the finished code, which runs.

That reframes what decomposition costs. This map has priced decomposition in coordination overhead and in the saturation threshold. The real price is structural: splitting work creates interfaces, each interface needs an owner, and a decomposition that leaves one unowned produces a failure that more communication cannot repair and outcome-level evaluation cannot see. It is the fleet-scale version of subagent-delegation-adds-a-silent-handoff-failure-class, and it says the design question is not "how many agents" but "does every seam land inside somebody's ownership."

Two bounds. The coordinator here is a prompt clause with no enforced routing, so this measures nominal leadership — an enforced star topology is a different intervention and is named as follow-on work; the distinction is the finding, not a gap in it. And the exploratory run-level regressions find no single network property that buys success: more messaging and writing go with failure, more reading with success, but the associations explain about a tenth of the variance and the causality is open, since a run that goes well needs less repair traffic. The graph records how a run went more than it predicts how it will go.
