Amend the rule, regenerate the batch

The stated principle is this map's own name: "You fix the process (loop) that produced the code" — not the individual file. Two completed migrations at a scale nothing else in the vault touches, and four mechanisms carrying it: mechanical work queues built from disk state so the queue is resumable by construction (build-the-work-queue-from-disk-state); objective verification where compiler errors, test failures and behaviour diffs against the original are the ground truth and no agent's report is; adversarial review with multiple reviewers in separate contexts and a disagreement escalating to a third agent; and rulebook-driven regeneration — when the same mistake recurs, amend the rule and regenerate the affected batch.

Why this lands on the map's live tension. Hypothesis (A) holds that the coordination tax is not shared-versus-private state but raw, unvalidated state moving through a serializing controller — unit size × validation × routing (verified-shared-state-beats-orchestrator-routing, event-passing-beats-shared-context-in-agent-fleets). This fleet is the limiting case of the validated arm. Its shared substrate is the filesystem; its admission check is the compiler and the suite; there is no orchestrator rewriting or routing peer discoveries, because a worker reads the queue off disk and writes a file back. Where DeLM paid an LLM to verify ~100-token gists at admission and lost 4.9 points without it, a compile error is admission-time verification that costs no model call at all — and hundreds of agents coordinate over 1M LOC on it. The one place the design does serialize is a build daemon batching patches and rebuilds to prevent races, which is precisely the map's distinction: serialize the mechanical operation, not the knowledge.

And it answers the within-agent limb's open question with a third option. VRR-Stop found the repair loop net-destructive past round 2 under a noisy verifier (repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum); CodeRescue found "reflect on the execution feedback" the worst of three recovery policies at 27.5% (recovery-actions-are-not-a-capability-ladder). This practice neither iterates on the artifact nor escalates the model: on a recurring error it moves the repair up a level, to the generator, and regenerates. That is what a strong mechanical oracle buys — with a compiler as the verifier, the marginal gain stays positive long enough for the loop to be worth running, which is the boundary condition the map says decides everything.

The adversarial-review layer is the same independence property on the judgment side, and it is the fourth arrival in a fortnight: an-independent-checker-beats-the-same-model-checking-itself (79% vs 71%), structure-outweighs-the-verification-step (rescues 6 → 2 when observe/compare returns to the generating model), run-several-scoped-reviewers-not-one-mega-agent (separate scopes, separate windows), and here separate contexts with a third-agent tiebreak — implemented as a fleet topology rather than a model selection.

Note the routing inversion that makes it affordable: small models implement in parallel, the largest models review and write the rules. The expensive model is spent on the artifact that governs a batch, not on each item in it.

Caveats: T2 practice narrative, two case studies, no control arm, and an unusually favourable task class. A language port hands you three oracles for free — a total type/syntax checker, a working reference implementation, and a human-written suite — none of which ordinary feature work has. The 19 post-merge regressions carry no severity breakdown or denominator, and "100% of the existing suite" is a statement about the old suite (a-passing-suite-does-not-mean-a-tested-change). The $165k excludes the human effort the post explicitly says to front-load into the rulebook and its stress test, which is the un-priced input the whole result rests on. The process transfers; the magnitudes do not.
