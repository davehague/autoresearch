The spec stopped being documentation and started being the input that survives

This map's oldest claim is that the specification is a contract that must be held outside the agent, and its most recent one is uncomfortable: the-first-configuration-a-team-commits-is-usually-its-last found that across 441 repositories, 73.8% of committed AI configuration artifacts are written once and never modified, 83.1% of adopting repositories stop at one maturity level, and reversals are zero. The field is accumulating durable, unamended, agent-obeyed contracts written at the moment of least experience with the tool.

This is the practitioner-side account of why that matters, and it names the asymmetry cleanly. P1's observation is that upstream specification work is not new — experienced teams have always invested in it — but its consequentiality is. Specification quality now propagates directly into generated code, and weak input produces weak output that LLMs do not push back against the way an experienced engineer would. That is the missing half of a-stale-convention-file-costs-more-than-no-file, which measured that when a written standard and working code disagree the agent follows the standard every time, even when the standard is worse. One study measures the obedience; this one supplies the reason it is dangerous — the loop that used to catch a bad spec was a human declining to build it, and that loop is gone.

False correctness is the name for what arrives instead of an objection. The generated artifact is syntactically correct, locally coherent, and passes. What it cannot do is explain itself. P2 reports being unable to change parts of a system because he could no longer trace "why they are now where they are." P5, in his own written survey response before the interview: "functionally correct, but nobody understands why." The consequence for this map is specific: false correctness shifts validation away from behavioral testing and toward reasoning about architectural intent, contextual consistency and long-term maintainability. Tests cannot adjudicate any of those, which means the spec is now load-bearing at exactly the point where the executable safety net has no coverage.

The reported effort redistribution is the practice, such as it is. One participant estimates two thirds of task time now goes into specification refinement; another reports much longer architectural planning at the start with correspondingly shorter coding. The authors offer this as a pattern rather than a recommendation: effort that used to sit in implementation has migrated upstream, and organizations should budget for the redistribution rather than treating planning as overhead to compress. That is consistent with the vault's rulebook finding — a spec validated before execution is paid for, not free — and it is the same shape as declare-what-each-step-reads-and-writes at the human rather than the machine layer.

And the adoption numbers keep it from being a success story. 13 of 50 respondents use a steering file at all; 6 of 50 use a structured Requirements → Design → Tasks workflow. Two independent instruments now agree that externalization is the practice everyone describes and few have adopted, and that among adopters almost nobody amends.

Bounds. Five interviews, convenience-sampled, unpiloted survey instrument, single-coder analysis without inter-rater reliability, one participant a co-author, Swiss/Central European over-representation. The two-thirds figure is one practitioner's estimate of his own time allocation, not a measurement. Nothing here shows that more specification effort produced better outcomes — the study reports what people do and why they say they do it, which for this map is corroboration of a mechanism already measured elsewhere rather than new evidence of effect.
