A stance marker, and it matters mostly because of who signed it

This is a position paper with no study behind its central claim, and it is worth carrying for two reasons. The first is provenance: it is signed by substantially the group that built and maintains SWE-bench and mini-swe-agent, saying that their own instrument no longer tracks the binding constraint. This map already holds the outside critique — benchmarks-conflate-model-harness-and-penalize-alternatives made the same family of argument from the benchmark-design side and was graded T3 for the same reason. Getting it from the maintainers is a different kind of evidence: not a stronger measurement, but a much weaker case for dismissing it as an outsider's complaint.

The second is that the four dimensions map cleanly onto findings this vault already has, which is what turns a manifesto into a checklist.

  • Task alignment — the agent infers the latent specification and surfaces what it could not resolve. The named failure is the clarification spiral, where each correction compounds the misunderstanding. The vault's version is stronger than the paper's: clarification-is-a-harness-affordance found asking is a property of the scaffold rather than the model, and underspecified-targets-drive-unsafe-actions measured what happens when nobody asks. The paper adds the measurement complaint — benchmarks collapse the whole exchange into pass/fail, and there is no public corpus of what the exchange looked like.
  • Steerability — meaningful decision points exposed during the run, so a human can redirect a subgoal without taking the keyboard. Current systems are argued to be bimodal: full autonomy or constant supervision. keep-the-agent-interruptible-and-reversible is the design principle; intervene-on-a-trigger-not-on-a-schedule and the-correction-window-is-the-first-third-of-the-trajectory are the two results that say where the decision points belong, which is more than the paper offers.
  • Verifiability — output structured so a human can check it. The sharpest line in the paper is that unit tests are the dominant verification paradigm in benchmarks and stop reducing cognitive load the moment the same agent writes the code and the tests. That is cogenerated-tests-and-fixes-fail-together stated as a design complaint, and it sits beside half-of-an-agents-own-passing-checks-cannot-see-the-bug and the-agents-final-report-is-its-least-reliable-artifact.
  • Adaptability — experience carried across sessions without losing what was learned. Assessed bluntly: today's agent memory and skills "are often little more than indexed markdown files," and nothing in the evaluation regime rewards cross-session learning because agents are scored on isolated tasks. The vault agrees and has receipts on both halves — memory-delivery-not-storage, and skills-regress-as-much-as-they-gain for the losing-what-was-learned part.

What is genuinely new is the last of the four research directions and the framing of the second. Oversight should be proactive and task-aware — surface a visual preview or an interactive summary conditioned on what the change is, rather than defaulting to a test run — which is a design brief this vault has no evidence for or against, and which is the natural continuation of rank-the-diff-instead-of-commenting-everywhere. And interaction metrics ported from HCI — turns to resolve, intervention frequency, verification effort — applied to trajectory logs at scale, which is a concrete proposal that the observability tooling in observability could already execute against existing traces.

Bounds, and they are heavy. No systematic review: the paper does not report how many papers or benchmarks it examined, or what fraction of benchmarks involve a human at all, so "humans are missing" is asserted at the level of a literature impression rather than measured. Its supporting statistics are quoted from a third-party developer survey. The four dimensions are explicitly not claimed to be exhaustive. And it does not price its own proposal — user simulators are offered as the scaling route while the authors concede that measuring simulator fidelity is an open problem, which is the same regress this vault keeps hitting whenever a proxy is proposed to replace a proxy. Graded T3 and single-study accordingly; the paper's one quantitative contribution is carried separately and with its own caveats at passing-patches-diverge-behaviorally-from-the-reference.
