The limit on how much you hand off is what it costs you to check the result

The useful thing a systematic review does to a discourse is separate what everyone agrees on from what everyone is asserting. Both halves land here.

The agreement, and its single load-bearing principle. The sources converge on the same components — a trigger, a machine-checkable stop condition, durable state outside the context window, encoded project knowledge, isolation for parallel work, an independent verifier separated from the implementer, budgets with a documented kill switch, binding constraints on what an unattended run may change, and defined escalation points. The most emphasized is the maker/checker separation: the verifier's existence is named as the reason one "can walk away"; one implementation makes rejection the default, so output must earn approval; and the sharpest warning in the corpus is that a loop "satisfies the gate you wrote," not the goal behind it.

From that, the principle worth keeping: raise autonomy only as far as you can check the result at acceptable cost. Verification cost, not model capability, is what bounds delegation. That reframes the whole hand-off ladder — turn-based → goal-based (hand off the stop condition) → time-based (hand off the trigger) → proactive (hand off the prompt) — as a sequence of decisions each priced by what checking gets harder. It also separates two things a single "autonomy level" conflates: agency (how far one agent proceeds without intervention) and orchestration (how many run at once and who coordinates). They are independent axes, and treating autonomy as a per-task choice rather than a level a developer reaches and keeps is the more defensible framing.

The disagreement is about novelty and it is not settled. "A renamed cron job." "Automation was a thing before LLMs." The strongest version of the objection concedes the practice and relocates it: what is new is the non-deterministic worker, which puts the engineering into the guardrails rather than the trigger. A second strand predicts the practice dissolves into the tools, since /loop and /goal shipped as built-in commands and already absorb what the shell-loop technique worked around. This map does not need the dispute resolved — the measured object is scheduled, verified, state-carrying agent runs, whatever they are called.

The evidence grading is the point of the note. The two numbers that circulate — ~1,300 agent-written pull requests per week at Stripe, 423 Firefox security fixes in a month — have no published study or dataset behind them, and the discourse's own synthesis note says the circulating figures "are mostly secondhand summaries and should be treated as rough reference." Meanwhile the sources are not independent: at least three of the twelve build explicitly on one origin author's posts, so the apparent convergence on building blocks partly reflects a single lineage rather than replication. A review that discloses its own correlation structure is doing the thing this vault asks of a T3 aggregation, and the result is that the building-block consensus should be read as one well-articulated design with adherents, not as convergent evidence.

The failure catalogue is better sourced than the benefit catalogue, which is itself informative: runaway cost (~8M tokens in 48 hours from an over-eager CI-fixing loop), infinite fix loops, verifier theater, notification fatigue, drift between a loop's committed instructions and its state, and two named second-order risks — comprehension debt (the gap between what is in the repository and what anyone understands grows with loop velocity) and cognitive surrender.

And the practical bottleneck named repeatedly is not the agent at all: review capacity. Nightly runs open more pull requests than teams can review, so volume defeats the human guardrail the loop depends on — review fatigue turns the gate into a rubber stamp "while the pipeline still reports green," and "the loops that stick are the ones where somebody was already paid to read the output." That is review-capacity-is-what-a-loop-actually-spends in this map's own vocabulary, and it is the same quantity as the verification-cost bound, seen from the human side.

Bounds. Exploratory review, not systematic in the PRISMA sense; twelve sources over seven weeks, selected by one author who had been following the discourse. All claims are practitioner self-report — this note is graded on the review's rigor about its sources, not on the sources' rigor about their numbers. The controlled experiment that would settle the value question (interactive vs goal-driven vs scheduled, same tasks) is proposed and unrun.
