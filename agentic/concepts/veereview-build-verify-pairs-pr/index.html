The map's Verdict-Diagnosis gap, found inside the measuring instrument rather than inside the review it measures

agentic-code-review's strand (1), verifier efficacy & independence, has spent months accumulating instances where a review verdict turns out to be noisy evidence about what actually happened — (verdict-diagnosis-gap-security-review) and its several extensions all show a checker whose pass/fail output tracks something other than the property it claims to check. VeeReview is the first source on this map to find the same gap one level up, inside an instrument that was specifically preregistered to avoid that failure mode, and to report the failure as its own headline result rather than have it extracted by a later audit.

The study's design makes the failure legible rather than merely suspected. The authors preregistered the hypothesis (agents would show higher build-only rates, especially on the rule-decided pair), the analysis plan, a falsification rule, and — critically — a calibration bar: at least 80% agreement with an independently human-adjudicated reference set before any cohort comparison could be trusted. The instrument cleared none of its four pairs against that bar (4-60% agreement), and the human reference labels themselves carried weak inter-rater agreement (κ=0.154). That second number matters as much as the first: it means the "ground truth" VeeReview was calibrated against was itself a contested measurement, which is this map's pooling-conceals-variance and Verdict-Diagnosis themes compounding rather than isolated to one layer.

The one informative structural detail is which pair survived standardization and which didn't. Three pairs rely on an LLM judge constrained by deterministic signals; the fourth (Module Design↔Unit Test) is decided by a pure mechanical rule over the diff and repository tree, no LLM involved. That rule-decided pair is the one whose standardized effect (controlling for linked issues, shipped tests, and PR size) shrank to an interval including zero — weakly suggesting the presentation confound the authors name (linked issues, tests, size) lives in the LLM-judged components rather than the deterministic one, though the study was not designed to isolate that cleanly.

Because the instrument failed calibration, this map should not carry "agent PRs have lower build-only rates than human PRs" as a finding — the authors themselves decline to make that claim, reporting the odds ratios as "observations made with this instrument" rather than validated results. What the map should carry is the methodological lesson: a preregistered calibration check, reported honestly on failure, is what makes an uncalibrated instrument legible as uncalibrated instead of quietly shipping as a result. Most agent-vs-human PR comparisons in this literature do not run or report this check at all.

Bounds: small calibration reference set (25 PRs) with weak inter-rater agreement limits how precisely the calibration failure itself is measured, even though the direction (well below the 80% bar on every pair) is unambiguous. Single study, not yet independently replicated; under review at Empirical Software Engineering. The cohort odds ratios should not be cited as an established agent-vs-human difference — only as what an uncalibrated instrument reported.
