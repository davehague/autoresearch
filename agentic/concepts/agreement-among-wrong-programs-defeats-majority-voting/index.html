Four wrong programs that share a bug are a majority

The worked example in the paper is small enough to hold in your head. A coupon rule is valid when subtotal >= 25 and count >= 3. Three programs implement it correctly; two use subtotal > 25; two use count > 3. On an input that triggers only one bug class, voting is 5:2 and correct. On the input subtotal=25, count=3 — the one that exposes both — the four wrong programs all say "invalid" and the vote is 3:4 against the truth. Naive agreement fails precisely on the discriminating input, which is the input you generated the whole matrix to find.

The fix is not more inputs. It is dropping the assumption that all voters are equally reliable: fit a latent error rate per candidate by maximum likelihood over the whole consistency matrix, so a program whose outputs fit the inferred latent states poorly is discounted rather than counted. Measured, that is worth F1 76.62 against 70.83 for voting on the same matrix — the entire gain in recall (68.75 vs 59.65) at comparable precision, which is the right shape: voting is not wrong when it fires, it simply falls back too often.

The part that matters for this vault is the coupling test. Dawid–Skene assumes candidate outputs are conditionally independent given the latent state, and every candidate here is sampled from the same model — the exact condition cogenerated-tests-and-fixes-fail-together measured at a 1.87× coupling factor between a reproduction test and a fix drawn from one trajectory, and that a-verifier-sharing-the-interpretation-shares-the-error identified as a common-mode failure rather than a narration one. So the assumption should be expected to break. The authors test it the only way it can be tested without labels: group the wrong candidates by identical output patterns and read incorrect adoption as a function of how few groups there are, since fewer groups means more wrong programs behaving identically. If dependence undermined the estimator, adoption should climb as groups fall. It does not — 10.2% at one group, 7.3%, 3.1%, 14.3%, 10.0% — staying inside a band with no monotone trend.

That is a bound, not an exoneration, and the paper says so: the estimator can still adopt an incorrect reference when many wrong candidates share a bug that the generated inputs never expose. What the test rules out is the dominant version of the worry — that same-model sampling makes the whole construction degenerate. Read against cogenerated-tests-and-fixes-fail-together, the reconciliation is about what is shared: a test and a fix emitted in one trajectory share a single reading of the bug, while ten independent brute-force samples share only a model, and the second coupling is evidently much weaker than the first. The practical corollary for anyone building this: diversity of candidate reading, not diversity of sample, is what the estimator is spending, so sample the references at high temperature from a fresh context rather than continuing one.

Bounds. The stratification is observational and pooled across four models on 233 instances, with the 4-group cell (14.3%) sitting above the 1-group cell, so the band is noisy and the argument is the absence of a trend rather than a fitted null. Competitive-programming problems with schema-expressible inputs. The estimator's robustness to input source is checked on only the 65 function-call problems. And the whole construction presupposes that a brute-force reference is cheap to run, which is why the authors flag stateful repository-level tasks — where executing ten candidates a thousand times is not free — as the open extension.
