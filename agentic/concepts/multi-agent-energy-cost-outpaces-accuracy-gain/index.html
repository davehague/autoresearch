Strand (2)'s fixed-substrate-cost argument gets its first hardware-measured energy unit instead of a token count, and the number the map has been accumulating in tokens and dollars turns out to look the same in kilowatt-hours

This map's second strand holds that scaffolding and cache-lifecycle costs are a fixed substrate charge that exists before any allocation policy decides anything, and every source in the strand so far has denominated that charge in tokens, dollars, or wall-clock seconds. This source denominates it in measured energy — CodeCarbon reading Intel RAPL (CPU) and NVML (GPU) directly, not a token-count estimate — and finds the same shape the strand already believes: multi-agent decomposition is an expensive fixed cost that a Pareto-aware practitioner mostly shouldn't pay. 6.36x the energy and 6.07x the wall-clock time of a non-agentic baseline on average, worse than the map's existing harness-level and orchestration-level efficiency findings by roughly an order of magnitude in scale, because this source is comparing against the barest possible baseline (a single query) rather than against an unoptimized agentic configuration. The Pareto-frontier result is the sharper number for a routing decision: across five SE tasks, 59 of 66 Pareto-optimal configurations are non-agentic or single-agent — multi-agent buys a place on the efficient frontier in only 7 of 66 cases, and the one task where it earns an average accuracy improvement (vulnerability detection) is the exception, not a template for "more agents, better results" generally.

This sits beside, rather than replacing, the map's orchestration-efficiency finding (orchestration-efficiency-is-model-invariant) that holding models fixed and varying only the orchestration layer cuts cost 41% at higher quality — the two are not in tension, because that source compares two engineered orchestration layers against each other, while this one compares engineered multi-agent decomposition against no decomposition at all. Read together they sharpen strand (2)'s practical advice: the substrate cost is not inherent to having more than one agent, it is inherent to decomposition that doesn't pay for itself on the task at hand — an unoptimized multi-agent workflow is expensive, a well-engineered one is not automatically so. The within-family energy result (two 7B models differing 6.4x in energy per run, with the cheaper one also outperforming both tested 4B models) is a small but useful addition to the map's "parameter count is a poor proxy" theme, now measured in a unit (kWh) none of the map's existing sources use.

Scope: six open-weight models only — no frontier closed-weight model (Claude-, GPT-, or Gemini-class) is energy-measured here, so this does not directly speak to whether the same 6.36x multiplier holds for the agentic configurations this vault's other cost findings are mostly about; absolute energy figures are specific to the three tested hardware platforms.
