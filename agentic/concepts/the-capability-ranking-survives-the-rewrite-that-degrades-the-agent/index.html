The instrument that reports the score is blind to the property most people think the score implies

The interesting thing about this result is not that agents get worse when the codebase is rewritten — a few points, sometimes significant, always a lower bound because the sampler is deliberately not adversarial. It is that the number everyone reads does not move in a way that reveals it. In three of four scaffold-benchmark panels the ordering of models by resolve rate is identical before and after perturbation; in the fourth the only change is a swap between two models 0.2 points apart at baseline. So a leaderboard built by running every model on the unperturbed repositories and one built by running them on rewritten ones would agree on who wins — while disagreeing completely on who is brittle.

That splits "how good is this agent" into two instruments computed from the same runs, and only one of them is currently published. Capability ranking is stable under perturbation; robustness ranking is not stable across anything (robustness-ranks-differently-under-every-scaffold). A benchmark number can therefore be uncontaminated, correctly labelled, honestly judged and fully reproducible, and still say nothing about whether the agent survives the ordinary refactoring a repository undergoes between the day the benchmark was built and the day the agent runs on your code.

The per-instance layer is where the honesty is. With 20 runs per condition a Newcombe interval for a difference of two proportions is ±16 points even for an instance resolved in every single run of both conditions — so only 14 of 432 instance-configuration cells exclude zero, and the authors say plainly that at the instance level the comparison is underpowered rather than dressing it up. What carries the aggregate is direction plus concentration: point estimates run 65 positive to 35 negative on one benchmark and 79 to 44 on the other, and three instances alone account for roughly two-thirds of the largest configuration's 6.7-point mean. A single instance swings the other way by 55 points in one cell, and the paper refuses to read it as a repository-level effect because there is one such instance in the sample. That is the correct treatment of a heavy tail and it is the same shape as pair-the-runs-or-the-mean-hides-the-churn: the mean is a net, and the net here is carried by a handful of cells.

The design choice worth stealing. Perturbed runs use one run per variant, twenty variants, and the split is derived rather than asserted: averaging per-variant rates is unbiased for the population mean at any number of repeats, but the variance (Kσ² + ν)/R is strictly increasing in the repeats-per-variant whenever variants differ in difficulty. A run spent on a fresh variant samples both sources of randomness; re-running a variant resamples only the agent. Anyone measuring an agent against a distribution of environments rather than a fixed one should spend the budget the same way.

One methodological caution the paper had to design around. Agents frequently detected the perturbation and reverted the repository outright — git reset — when a variant carried too many decoys, which "destroys the measurement rather than making it harder". Two sampler caps exist solely to keep variants inside the range an agent treats as ordinary code. Any perturbation-based benchmark has to price that: the agent can notice it is being tested, and noticing is a confound, not a capability.
