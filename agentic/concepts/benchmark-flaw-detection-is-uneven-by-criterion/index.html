The audit can be automated for the flaws that leave their evidence in the transcript

This map has spent a month establishing that benchmark numbers misgrade — through retrieval (benchmark-scores-inflate-when-agents-can-retrieve-the-fix), through construction defects (benchmark-misalignment-concentrates-in-the-unresolved-tail), through apparatus asymmetry (harness-asymmetry-manufactures-model-differences), through the wrong unit of evidence (one-run-scores-an-implementation-not-an-idea). Every one of those was found by somebody reading transcripts by hand. A single evaluation run can produce 100 million to a billion tokens, so the operational question the map had not asked is whether the reading can be automated. The answer is a partial yes with a sharp internal boundary, and the boundary is more useful than the average.

What separates a detectable flaw from an undetectable one is whether judging it requires knowing what the benchmark meant. Guessing vulnerability — is the answer space small enough that a heuristic gets there — is fully legible in the transcript, and the best scanner reaches sensitivity 0.93 at F1 0.74. Tool failure is not: deciding whether a broken command is a benchmark defect or an intended obstacle needs design context the scanner does not have, and the same infrastructure gets F1 0.27 at best, with one scanner model showing no discrimination at all. Ground-truth access and answer-format ambiguity land between, at F1 0.30–0.50. A single "we scanned it and it looks clean" is therefore not one claim but four, of wildly different strengths.

Two engineering consequences follow, both cheap.

The scanner model is a first-class variable, not an implementation detail. The two frontier models tested differ by a factor of four in F1 on the same criterion and disagree most where performance is worst — and exploiting that disagreement beats either alone in both directions: a max rule (either flags) buys sensitivity, an agreement rule buys specificity to 0.96–0.98. That is the same configuration lever this vault carries for reviewer agents (run-several-scoped-reviewers-not-one-mega-agent, an-independent-checker-beats-the-same-model-checking-itself, review-agent-precision-is-a-tunable-not-a-given), arriving at the eval-audit layer.

Know what an audit buys before running one. Ruling out a violation rate above 5% takes roughly 75 human-graded or 120 scanner-graded transcripts. Estimating prevalence within ±5% takes about 200. And estimating a scanner's own sensitivity takes about 100 verified positives — on the order of 1,000 manually screened transcripts at 10% prevalence, which this study could not reach even with stratified over-sampling, which is why several of its sensitivity intervals span most of the unit range. So the honest posture is the one this vault has adopted for every gate it has measured (a-deterministic-prefilter-makes-a-model-gate-affordable at recall 0.769, verifier-catch-and-false-alarm-rates-are-measurable at catch 0.20): an automated benchmark audit lowers a base rate; it does not clear a class. A benchmark that survives one is less likely to be broken, by an amount you can only state if you know the scanner's sensitivity for that criterion.

The scanners' actual findings on SWE-Bench-Verified are worth carrying separately as replication: source issues that describe the gold patch verbatim including the files to change, and models reading upstream git history containing the human patch and implementing it without deriving a fix. That is an independent team, a different benchmark in the family, and a different method finding the same two leakage channels a vendor audit found on SWE-bench Pro — the first non-vendor confirmation of that mechanism in this map.

Caveats. Explicitly a proof of concept: the scanners were developed to a working standard and frozen, not elicited to their ceiling, so these are lower bounds on what the approach can do. Human labels are the referent and are themselves shaky in places — the authors report real subjectivity, cases needing deep knowledge of benchmark construction, and consensus resolution rather than a measured inter-rater agreement. Only four of eleven transcript-suitable criteria were built. Confidence intervals on sensitivity are wide enough that the per-criterion ordering is softer than the point estimates suggest. Two scanner models; benchmarks selected for having an existing common-framework implementation, which controls scaffolding variation and makes the sample unrepresentative. And the authors name field-wide standardization gaps in transcript and metadata conventions as an active degrader — so part of what these numbers measure is the ecosystem rather than the method.
