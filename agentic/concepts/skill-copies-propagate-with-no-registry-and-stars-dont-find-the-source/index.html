The population census this map already carries (3.8M files, half copies, no registry) gets its missing dimension — who copied from whom, when — and the answer to "where should a scanner look" turns out to be the opposite of the obvious heuristic

the-skill-population-is-half-copies-with-no-registry established the scale of the problem from a single snapshot: a natural-language artifact selected probabilistically at run time, propagating by folder copy with no package manager, no version constraint, and no revocation path. That source was explicit about what it could not answer: which repositories are sources, which are copies, and whether anyone could target an audit at the former rather than scanning everything. This source answers exactly that, by mining the git history underneath the same underlying corpus rather than a single snapshot, and the answer is a specific, actionable negative result: the heuristic every registry-less ecosystem defaults to — rank by popularity — identifies the wrong repositories. Dated copy out-degree and GitHub stars are nearly uncorrelated (tau=-0.10), fake-star campaigns make stars actively manipulable, and most of the highest-static-out-degree repositories turn out, once dated, to be late adopters rather than true sources. A ranking model trained on nothing but dated copy-propagation behavior — not content, not popularity — and evaluated on a strict future holdout finds 30x more of the audit value a limited review budget could capture (14.9% vs 0.5% of future high-risk adoptions prevented, auditing the same number of repositories).

The update-propagation number sharpens this map's no-revocation-path concern into a measured rate. The parent source could only argue structurally that nothing tells a copy its origin was fixed or withdrawn; this one measures how often a copy follows its source's later edit at all, security-relevant or not: 20.9%, against 47-84% for copied code files in the dependency-reuse literature the paper cites. Skills are not just unregistered — they are measurably stickier and slower to patch than the ordinary code-cloning problem this map already treats as a known risk, and the asymmetry this source finds in what drifts (modified copies gain command-execution capability more often than they lose it, and the gain comes from adopting a different version wholesale rather than from the copying owner's own edits) means the drift that does happen skews toward more capability, not less.

A hard ceiling worth carrying forward rather than treating as a shortcoming of the method: even a ranking chosen in hindsight, with perfect knowledge of the held-out period, prevents only about a quarter of future high-risk adoptions, because roughly half of what eventually proliferates arrives as a brand-new skill with no copy history yet to rank on. No audit-targeting strategy built on propagation history alone can close that half — it argues for a complementary point-of-publication check (static content scanning at creation, the sibling-map finding skill-time-preapproval-is-the-one-harness-supply-chain-defect-a-marketplace-scan-can-catch already carries) rather than better ranking.

Interaction edge: agent-harness-architecture — that map's harness-supply-chain-defects entry measures the content risk baked into skills at publish time (3.8% of setups carry a skill pre-approving an unrestricted shell); this source measures the distribution risk on top of that content — once a risky skill exists, this is how far and how fast it spreads, and how rarely a fix catches up to it.

Bounds: single-author paper; covers only the first ten months of the SKILL.md format's existence on public GitHub — private/enterprise ecosystems, plausibly the highest-stakes population, are entirely outside this measurement. Risk flags mark capability (bundles executables, pre-approves tools) rather than confirmed malice; stated precision 98.3%, recall 80.9%, so risk counts are a lower bound. Repository-type and risk labeling is LLM-mediated (Opus 5.5 + Sonnet 5.5, kappa 0.77-1.00 against a human-resolved reference for a sample; disagreements resolved manually for 18 repos / 28 skills).
