A better plan is worth more than a better model, and the best planner is not the biggest

This map's within-agent limb has spent a month establishing what the loop should not do — that an unvalidated repair loop is negative value, that continuation has negative expected value past a computable threshold, that the informational payload of execution feedback is statistically zero against a content-free placebo. It has been much quieter on what the loop should do before it starts. DCAS supplies a controlled number for the pre-execution half, and the number is large.

The design is the cleanest kind: the executor model, the scaffold and version, the benchmark and the 100-turn execution budget are identical in every condition. Only the plan handed to the executor changes. 42.8% with no plan, 57.8% with a Claude Sonnet 4.5 plan. The ladder in between is monotone in planner capability — self-plan 48.2, an open-weight 480B planner 49.2, frontier planners 54.2–57.8 — which is the expected shape and confirms plan quality is the operative variable rather than the extra turn.

Then the shape breaks in a way worth keeping. Sonnet 4.5 beats Opus 4.5 as a planner (57.8 vs 54.2–56.0) on the same executor, despite Opus being the more capable general model. The authors offer two non-exclusive readings: a stronger planner's plans implicitly assume reasoning a 30B executor cannot reliably perform, or Sonnet's output style interacts more naturally with a scaffold primarily deployed with Sonnet-class models. Either way the practical claim is the same and it is a genuine addition to this vault's routing thinking: the premium model is not automatically the right one for the governing role.

That cuts against — and refines — route-the-premium-model-to-the-governing-role. The governing role is still worth spending on: the gap between no-plan and the best plan is 15 points, far larger than any executor swap available at that price. What DCAS adds is that "spend more" and "spend on the strongest model" come apart, and the missing measurement is a two-dimensional one nobody has run: planner tier × executor tier, to find whether the optimum tracks the executor or is fixed.

The result also sits precisely on this map's independence thread, which has been progressively narrowed. an-independent-checker-beats-the-same-model-checking-itself found a cheap cross-model call recovering 79% of injected errors against self-checking's 71%. reviewer-value-tracks-capability-headroom found the most independent condition — a weaker model reviewing a stronger one's draft — the only significantly harmful cell. DCAS is the same variable on the planning side rather than the checking side, and it lands with the reviewer result: an external planner helps in proportion to its capability and its calibration to who has to execute the plan. Independence buys nothing here; the plan is not a check.

The internalization half matters for what it says about where the leverage lives. Fine-tuning on 576 planning-aware trajectories recovers most of the injected-plan gain without an external planner at inference (55.8% self-plan against 57.8% injected), and the PlanOnly/Plan+Exec contrast cleanly separates two things that had been one word. Training on planning turns only produces its largest gain in the no-plan condition (53.8%) — so what transferred was the scaffold's turn-by-turn structural conventions, not the ability to use a planning step. Only training that includes execution against an explicit plan installs the productive use of one. For anyone building a loop rather than training a model, that is the argument for making the plan a first-class artifact the loop must execute against, rather than a prompt asking the model to think first.

Bounds. One executor model, one benchmark, one scaffold for the controlled arm, Pass@1 at a single attempt with no seeds or intervals reported — this vault's own one-run-scores-an-implementation-not-an-idea applies, and the 15-point headline has not been floored. The planning phase runs up to 500 additional turns of codebase exploration before execution begins; the plan makes no code changes, but plan quality and extra exploration are therefore not fully separated. The Sonnet-over-Opus inversion is a single contrast on one executor and is offered by the authors as a hypothesis with two candidate mechanisms, so it should be carried as a caution against assuming planner rank rather than as an established ordering.
