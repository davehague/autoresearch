The observational instrument this map's precision/recall arguments have been missing: not what a reviewer catches, but what a developer chooses when several AI-generated options of different security quality sit in front of them

This map's largest body of evidence is about review agents — their precision, their volume problem, their capacity to catch what a human reviewer would miss. This source measures the layer underneath that: an individual developer, offered several AI-generated implementations of varying security quality, deciding which to keep and then editing it, with no review agent involved at all. The title states the finding as the map's own governing tension restated for humans instead of checkers — developers neither fully "trust" (defer entirely to AI output) nor fully "verify" (systematically inspect for the vulnerability classes the study engineered into the suggestion set). Selection and post-hoc editing are measured as two separable behaviors: which suggestion a developer picks when security quality varies, and what they do to it afterward — and the paper's framing is that vulnerability identification tracked surface cues (code style, apparent completeness, plausible-looking structure) more reliably than it tracked the actual presence of a memory-safety defect.

Read against this map's reviewer-side findings, this is the human-in-the-loop complement to a-falling-false-alarm-rate-reads-as-a-better-reviewer and the broader theme that a system optimized to look trustworthy and a system that is trustworthy are not the same object — here demonstrated on the developer's own judgment rather than on a reviewer agent's calibration. It also sharpens why "a human is in the loop" is an incomplete answer to a review-agent's known false negatives: if the human's own vulnerability-identification behavior is cue-driven rather than analysis-driven, the human backstop this map's contested strand-two capacity findings lean on inherits a failure mode of its own, one this vault has previously inferred rather than measured directly.

Bounds: the task domain (C linked-list manipulation) is chosen for experimental control and is a classic memory-safety setting specifically — the cue-driven-selection finding is strongest evidence for memory-safety-class vulnerabilities and untested for other classes (injection, auth, deserialization) that present differently on the page. The five-suggestion sets were researcher-engineered to vary security quality rather than sampled from a live assistant's actual output distribution, so the specific selection rates measured here are not a claim about how often a real coding assistant offers a vulnerable option in practice — only about what happens when one does.
