The adoption problem is aim, and aim is cheap

Two distributions over the same six themes. What human reviewers actually comment on, from 18,000 reviews that produced a fix: correctness 44.4%, code quality 19.2%, security 19.1%, and everything else under a fifth between them. What a deployed AI reviewer emits, tagged against the same taxonomy: security 2.0%, correctness 25.5%, and best practices at 30.8% — four times its human share, with code design at 350% of its.

That much is a familiar complaint, and on its own it does not distinguish two very different diagnoses: the reviewer is aimed wrong, or humans discount machine-authored comments on the things that matter and only tolerate them on nits. The paper separates these with a control set — 712 AI-authored reviews that engineers actually acted on — and their distribution is correctness 46.1%, security 20.1%, code quality 19.0%, which is the human baseline to within a couple of points. The taxonomy captures reviewer priorities, not reviewer identity. When an AI review addresses correctness or security, engineers act on it at human-comparable rates.

So the gap is in emission, and emission is the cheapest thing on this map to change.

That control does real work against this map's existing ledger. The 2026-07-27 finding was that resolution rate measures usefulness rather than precision, because the plurality of non-resolution is the reviewer being right and uninformed (grade-the-reviewer-on-what-the-author-fixed). This is the matching decomposition on the other side of the pipe: a large share of the low-adoption problem is not that the comments are wrong, and not that they are machine-authored, but that they are about the wrong things. Both halves point at the same conclusion — a reviewer's headline metric is dominated by targeting and context, not by the model's judgment.

The second half is a different prediction problem, and it is the one that is tractable. This map established on 2026-07-20 that triage cannot relieve a capacity shortage: acceptance is only weakly predictable from the change itself (AUC 0.676 from all submission-time signals) and review effort barely at all (R² 0.12), leaving reviewer supply, reviewer diversity and gate enforcement as the levers (review-effort-is-organizational-not-artifact-determined). Spotlight does not predict whether a diff needs review. It predicts where inside it to look, which is a within-diff ranking rather than a cross-diff classification, and that turns out to be answerable: 2.4× the incumbent on triage-level quality estimation, 3.3× on landing a top-5 finding at the true defect location, at 5× fewer tokens and 6× lower latency.

The trade is deliberate and worth naming, because it is the same trade the map already recorded from the opposite direction. Repository grounding triples localization and loses on judged relevance — grounding improves aim, not judgment (repository-grounding-buys-localization-not-judgment). Here taxonomy grounding makes the same trade on purpose: aim at the three themes humans act on, deprioritize the two the incumbent over-produces, and accept that the system is now a direction-of-attention device rather than a source of insight. That is coherent with the map's cheapest-lever finding — the strongest predictor of resolution is an inline one-click suggestion (OR 1.62) while clarity and relevance are negligible. Actionability beats insight, and placement beats both.

The efficiency numbers matter more than they look. Time-to-first-review falls from 335.9 seconds to 55.4 — from a turnaround too slow to sit inside a review workflow to one that does. A reviewer that costs a fifth as many tokens is also a reviewer you can afford to run several of, which is the map's diversity lever (run-several-scoped-reviewers-not-one-mega-agent) becoming affordable rather than merely advisable.

What this does not do: absolute precision and recall stay low on both systems (0.108/0.097 against 0.036/0.033 on the problematic slice). The map's 5.81% best-overall-score-against-human-comments result stands. Nothing here measures the false-negative side, which remains this map's oldest unmeasured quantity — CRBench scores whether the system flags a diff that is known to contain an issue, never what it misses on a diff nobody flagged.

Bounds: one engineering environment, one codebase, one review culture, and the gap is measured against that org's incumbent reviewer, not a named commercial product — so the −89.5% security figure characterizes a baseline rather than the field. The semantic grader deciding precision and recall is an LLM, and CRBench filters ground truth to issues determinable from the diff alone, which the authors note may inflate precision for both systems equally.
