The routing policy is a trained artifact, confirmed at production scale

This map's headline has had three independent T1 arrivals from three different objects — models across a session, iterations within a task, recovery actions after a failure — and no production instance. This is the production instance, and it agrees with the sharpest of the three claims rather than the softest.

a-routing-policy-must-be-learned-from-rollouts was established on a 360-example split: four frontier models used zero-shot as routers landed at or below the dumbest useful fixed policy while costing up to 4×, and a 4B fine-tune on the deployment's own execution rollouts beat all of them. Cursor's router is the same shape at three orders of magnitude more data — a classifier over query, context, task complexity and domain, trained on 600k live requests from the deployment it routes for, then A/B'd on millions. Two organizations, two scales, one conclusion: the mapping from request to correct model is not semantic, so it does not live in a prompt.

Two design details are worth copying. The router is cache-aware in training and in production evaluation, which is the operational form of prompt-caching-inverts-cloud-versus-local-economics — a router scoring candidates on published per-token rates is optimizing a number that does not describe its own workload. And the cost-quality dial ships as three named modes rather than a continuous λ (expose-a-cost-quality-dial-not-a-fixed-policy), which is a product decision but also an honest one: three operating points can each be measured, where a continuous dial usually is not.

The baseline it beats is a behaviour, not an algorithm: ~60% of developers pin one model as their default. Every fixed-policy result in this map predicts that is wrong in both directions at once, and this is the first field measurement of how common it is.

Caveats: T2 vendor post for a paid product. No confidence intervals, no task distribution, no public definition of "frontier-quality performance" beyond two proprietary metrics, and the headline 60% (A/B) sits beside a customer-reported 30–50% without reconciliation. Quality parity is asserted at the same time as the price comparison (resolve-rate-gains-do-not-carry-quality-gains), so treat the per-commit table as a price ranking under an unverified parity assumption. Suggestive for the structure, single-study for every magnitude.
