Three rival explanations, and only one predicts a falling hazard

Why do agentic prompts only grow? Three mechanisms are on the table and they make opposite predictions about the same measurable quantity — the probability that an instruction of age a is deleted at the next commit.

  • Instruction staleness — requirements stop applying, so old instructions become deletable. Predicts hazard rising with age.
  • Content fragility — brittle instructions die young, leaving robust survivors. Predicts a hazard bent down by composition.
  • Imperfect recall — the reasoning that would license the deletion decays faster than the instruction does. Predicts hazard falling with age, and something no rival predicts.

The hazard falls: log-hazard −0.032 per commit, 95% CI [−0.047, −0.019], on 28,426 deletions. Content fragility is tested directly rather than argued away — a gamma-frailty refit in its strongest form absorbs 30.8% of the effect and leaves the slope essentially intact at −0.0355. And the discriminating prediction is the one that settles it: if latent reasoning is encoded in a maintainer's head at write time, then each additional maintainer who touches the file should also depress the hazard, independent of age, because a handoff destroys reasoning the same way time does. Controlling for file activity, β_multi-author×age = −0.021 (z = −11.7). Staleness has no story for that; fragility has no story for that.

Why this is an engineering claim and not a sociology one. Deciding an instruction is safe to delete means estimating its marginal contribution across every constraint the prompt is meant to satisfy. Redundancy defeats one-at-a-time probing — two instructions each covering one constraint look individually free, and deleting both breaks it — so an honest audit needs subset probes and costs O(2^|D|) in a prompt of |D| instructions. That is why the rational maintainer of a file whose rationale has evaporated is append-only: adding is O(1) and diffuse, deleting is exponential and acute. The growth is not laziness; it is the correct response to a missing input.

This is the same shape as a-standing-policy-decays-into-one-more-retrieved-source and a-loaded-skill-is-not-a-retained-requirement, one level up. Those measure a rule losing force inside a run. This measures the reason for the rule losing recoverability across a repository's history, and it is the harder problem, because the run can be re-run and the reason cannot be re-derived. It also names precisely what agent memory systems get for free and authored instructions do not: MemGPT evicts on overflow, Mem0 deletes on detected contradiction, Zep timestamps superseded entries — every one keys on an observable staleness proxy. An authored instruction has none. It does not become outdated by being old or rarely triggered, which is exactly why prevent-context-growth-upstream-not-downstream is the only lever that works here.

Bounds. The hazard is fit on the 22.7% of instruction deaths that are not wholesale rewrites or migrations, and rewrite deaths outnumber the fitted deletions 3.4 to 1 — a rewrite threshold that mistook ordinary pruning for a bulldozing would pull real deletions out of the risk set. The multi-author covariate counts who has edited the file, not whether an instruction's own author has left. Matcher error inflates the old-age tail and therefore biases the slope toward zero, making the decline conservative. Observational throughout: the mechanism is identified by which predictions survive, not by assignment.
