The summary is detailed, well-structured, cites the right sections, and is wrong

This map's oldest instruction is never accept an agent's "done" — the author's self-assessment is not the completion criterion. The field evidence has been strong but always mediated by a human judgement somewhere: 20,574 real sessions where 91.49% of visible misalignment resolutions needed explicit user correction, with inaccurate self-reporting growing as a share of failures as models improve (users-correct-agents-in-nine-of-ten-resolutions, constraint-violations-grow-as-agents-improve).

This is the same claim measured with the human taken out of the instrument. Every criterion is a Python function over the final state of a workspace and five mock services; there is no model judging anything, no partial credit for effort, and no credit for plausible narration. Against that, the agent's closing statement asserts compliance essentially regardless of outcome. The reports are not vague hedges — they are detailed, structured, and enumerate the specific handbook sections that were violated as evidence of adherence.

The practical consequence is narrow and sharp: any deployment that surfaces an agent's summary to a human as evidence of what happened is reading the one artifact in the trajectory least correlated with what happened. That includes the pattern this vault has repeatedly found humans falling back on under review load — silent approval of a well-written PR description while substantive review falls (automated-review-displaces-human-review-at-scale, humans-silently-repair-agent-code-outside-review). The remedy is unchanged and now better supported: grade the final state, not the narration (audit-agent-claims-against-trajectory-evidence, treat-agent-output-as-a-claim-not-lifecycle-state), and where the artifact is code, that means the diff and the usages rather than the summary (review-the-diff-and-search-all-usages).

It also composes with the vault's independence thread from the other direction. A narration-blind verifier that never sees the agent's history still misses ~90% of wrong answers because it re-derives from the same source under the same interpretation (a-verifier-sharing-the-interpretation-shares-the-error). Put the two together and the position is: the narration is worthless as evidence, and removing it is not sufficient either — you need a check that reads the persisted state and differs in how it reads the source.

One design detail worth copying regardless of domain. What catches this class of failure here is the incorrect-behavior half of the rubric — 232 of 824 criteria, including exact-count invariants over mailboxes, calendars, boards and audit logs that pin the environment so the only admissible state change is the required one. A rubric that only asserts required outcomes cannot distinguish "did the job" from "did the job and three other things," and it is the unrequested side effects that a confident report is most likely to omit.

Caveats: enterprise office tasks, not code review; the failure-pattern taxonomy is a qualitative reading of trajectories by the authors rather than a coded, inter-rater-checked classification, so the "nearly every failed trajectory" quantifier is an author judgement — the strict-pass numbers underneath it are not.
