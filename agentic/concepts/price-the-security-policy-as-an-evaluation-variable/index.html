Hardening is never free, and the currency differs by model

This map's containment thesis is that high-risk constraints belong below the prompt layer, enforced by the environment rather than requested of the agent (contain-at-the-environment-layer-first, enforce-high-risk-constraints-below-the-prompt-layer, framework-guardrails-add-little-over-model-refusal). The evidence for that has been about whether enforcement works. This is the first study to make enforcement severity the independent variable and measure what it costs when it does work.

The setup is the reason to trust it. Three cumulative policy levels — control (root, open egress, writable filesystem), non-root, and a NIST-derived high level restricting egress, filesystem and privilege together — are compiled into the environment by native Linux mechanisms the agent cannot reconfigure, inherited by every child process so a subprocess cannot evade them, with pre-flight probes failing closed. The agent meets each limit only as a native runtime failure. There is no policy text to comply with, so nothing here is a compliance-scoring result; it is what happens when the environment simply says no.

Every bundle loses on both axes, and no two lose the same way. Under the strictest policy all twelve model–harness bundles move into the same quadrant — lower success, higher cost — but the ordering inverts between axes. Grok 4.5 gives up the least success (−7.1 pp) and absorbs the largest cost inflation (+167.3%). Claude Sonnet 5 loses the most success (−18.3 pp) at a modest +21.4%. GPT-5.6 Luna is Grok's mirror: +16.0% cost for −18.0 pp. The authors decline to rank on three trials and no intervals, and report it as a spread — the right call, and the spread is the finding.

Two consequences follow, and they are for different readers.

For anyone choosing an agent to deploy inside real controls: a leaderboard position obtained in a permissive sandbox does not survive the environment you will actually run in, and the shape of the degradation — success loss versus spend inflation — is a per-bundle property. Model choice is policy-dependent. That is attribute-performance-to-the-stack-not-the-model with the environment's security posture added as a term, and it lands next to install-time-security-is-a-harness-model-pair-property as a second case where a security outcome belongs to the pair rather than the model.

For anyone reading a cost comparison: hardening's second currency is money, and it is large. Deep sampling at 100 runs per condition finds median per-run cost roughly tripling on both probed tasks — including one whose success rate barely moved — so the cost of policy decouples from its effect on success. Failing runs cost about as much as passing ones (0.96× and 1.11×), meaning policy-induced failures consume nearly the full inflated budget. Reading all 200 hardened trajectories attributes 97% of the movement to a single behaviour: workaround construction, the agent abandoning the blocked path and rebuilding a toolchain from source or substituting data. That is not the agent giving up; it is the agent doing more work to route around the wall, which is exactly why the bill grows (failed-recoveries-burn-compute-not-terminate, retry-cost-is-an-architectural-property).

The model–harness ablation is worth separating out because it disentangles two things this vault usually sees fused. Grok 4.5 remains the higher-cost model under either harness at every policy — so the cost extreme follows the model. But moving either endpoint model to the other harness lowers success at every policy — so success follows the bundle. Cost and capability are not attributable to the same layer.

Bounds. One benchmark (89 Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks), three trials per cell, no confidence intervals on the headline shifts. The step from non-root to NIST-derived high moves several surfaces at once and is treated as a whole-configuration effect, so no single axis is credited. Costs are list prices over one OpenRouter route at measurement time. Provider-side safety classifiers touched five bundles and 98 trials and are retained as measuring the bundle as deployed (worth at most 2.2 pp to any bundle, and not rising with severity); one bundle's declared fallback served 29.9% of its requests, so those numbers describe a gated route. Boundary-Bench is released as an open-source Terminal-Bench plugin.
