The intervention is definitely doing something, and "something" is the wrong question

A team shipping a context intervention — a skill, a retrieved document, a set of tests in the prompt — wants a signal cheaper than a full eval to tell them it is working. Representation shift is an obvious candidate: measure how far the model's internal state moves, and treat movement as evidence of uptake.

The probes say movement is real and easy to detect. Prompt condition is decodable from prompt-end hidden states at 0.953 AUC on MBPP+, and a four-way condition probe on the larger model reaches 0.964 macro-AUC. The model unambiguously registers what you put in front of it.

The probes also say movement is nearly orthogonal to the thing you care about. The success probe on the same states tops out at 0.723 AUC and is near majority-class on the saturated benchmark; the rescue probe (does this context turn a failure into a success) has 17 positives and an accuracy equal to the majority baseline. Whatever the hidden state encodes about the intervention, it does not encode whether the intervention will pay.

Then the part that makes this worth a note rather than a footnote. Grouping tasks by their paired behavioural outcome and residualising the shift on added prompt length, rescued tasks carry 0.21 SD more shift than stable failures [0.01, 0.42] — and harmed tasks carry 0.51 SD more shift than stable successes [0.32, 0.70]. Shift tracks disturbance in both directions, and it tracks the damaging direction more than twice as strongly. A proxy that is merely uninformative wastes your time; this one would point you toward the interventions doing the most harm. Reinforcing it from the condition side: assertion-only and irrelevant-test prompts routinely shift representations more than the useful tests while scoring worse.

For evaluation-integrity this is a new entry in the map's standing catalogue of cheap proxies that misgrade an agent — and unusually it is a proxy from the interpretability side rather than the outcome side. The map's existing members mostly flatter: the merge label, the outcome pass rate, the LLM judge, the agent's self-report, resolution rate (resolution-rate-is-not-precision), coverage on buggy code (coverage-stops-predicting-when-the-code-is-buggy). This one inverts, which is a worse failure mode than optimism because it survives the obvious sanity check: you will see your intervention "landing" hardest exactly where it is breaking things.

The general form is worth stating beyond this paper: an intervention's measured influence on the model is not a directional measure of its value. That is the same sentence the skills-regression work reached at outcome level — a library's description acts on the model whether or not the body is ever opened, producing 14 of 81 and 70 of 243 coded regressions (a-skill-description-acts-without-being-invoked, skills-regress-as-much-as-they-gain) — arrived at here through the hidden state instead of the task outcome. Influence and benefit are separate quantities, and the field measures the first.

Carried at suggestive, deliberately. The authors label the task-level shift analysis post hoc and exploratory, and the probing work correlational — it shows how prompts change representations, not a causal mechanism inside the model. The rescue probe's 17 positives make that arm uninformative on its own. Two Qwen checkpoints, one completion per condition. What survives all that is the qualitative claim, which is robust across the condition-level and task-level views and is the part practitioners would act on: do not read representation movement as uptake.
