The reason a leaderboard can be precise and still be measuring the wrong thing: scaffold is counted as signal or as noise depending on the claim, and most published rankings never say which

This map's core demand has always been "report the null beside the effect, for the specific contrast being claimed." This source sharpens what the contrast is a contrast of before the null question can even be asked: an agent leaderboard score is not one number with one reliability, it is at minimum two objects (the model, and the model-scaffold system) with wildly different reliabilities on the identical underlying data (0.935-0.994 vs. 0.148-0.841 on the same nine benchmarks). A published ranking that doesn't say which object it's ranking is not wrong, exactly -- it's underspecified in a way that lets a reader assume the tighter number applies to the claim they actually care about (usually: "is model A better than model B," which is the model object, the less reliable one).

The mechanism behind the gap is concrete rather than merely statistical: scaffolds don't just add noise uniformly, they change which tasks get solved (posterior probability 84% that task-by-scaffold variance exceeds task-by-model variance), while the aggregate benchmark score can stay roughly flat across scaffolds (the model-minus-scaffold contrast favors neither direction, ~50% posterior probability). That is a scaffold effect hiding inside a null-looking top-line number -- exactly the shape this map's standing demand exists to catch, applied one level up from a single reported delta to an entire leaderboard's ranking claim.

The task-scaling result is this map's cleanest demonstration yet that the null you need depends on where the uncertainty actually lives. More tasks is the default response to "I don't trust this ranking," and it is uninformative or worse when the dominant uncertainty is scaffold coverage rather than task sampling -- the paper's own data shows a hard ceiling (+0.10 at most) that no amount of task-only scaling crosses. This is the budget-allocation mirror of this map's variance-decomposition theme (statistical-rigor-of-deltas's own standing demand to decompose variance by layer before spending more compute on any one layer) applied to evaluation design rather than to a reported effect.

The pooling result is the positive instruction this map has been missing: when scaffold coverage is the binding constraint, the fix is not "run the same benchmark more" but "run more different benchmarks at the same total task budget" -- which is a reliability gain and a cost reduction (83% projected) from the same lever, because breadth averages out scaffold-specific advantages that depth cannot touch. The external validation (pooled estimates tracking four held-out benchmarks far better than a naive average, 0.789 vs. 0.463 Kendall's τ) is the paper's own honesty check on its own fix, not a free assertion.

Interaction edge: agent-harness-architecture and harness-self-evolution -- this map's standing "harness and model are a non-separable term" finding (e.g. self-built-harnesses-lag-on-code-and-research-not-writing) is the mechanism this paper measures directly as a reliability property rather than a capability property: if scaffold and model interact on which tasks get solved, then any harness-search or harness-evolution result that reports a model ranking without also reporting which scaffold(s) it used is reporting a number whose reliability this paper's framework says is the less trustworthy of the two available readings.

Scope: two independent corpora (HAL, Harbor Index) agree qualitatively, but posterior rank intervals stay wide even after pooling -- many neighboring models have ordering probabilities near 1/2 -- and removing the single highest-signal benchmark (CORE-Bench Hard) increases pooled uncertainty more than any other removal, so the pooling gain is partly anchored on one informative benchmark rather than distributed evenly across the battery.
