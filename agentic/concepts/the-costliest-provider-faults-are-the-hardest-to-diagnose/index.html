The infrastructure layer has its own attribution ceiling, and it sits in the same band as the agent's

This map has two standing numbers about how well anything can say what went wrong: first-wrong-step attribution over agent trajectories is right 28.8–38.0% of the time (first-wrong-step-attribution-is-mostly-wrong), and agentic root-cause analysis over a live observability stack tops out at 25.3% (agents-cannot-enumerate-concurrent-root-causes). Both are about the agent's reasoning or the system it operates. This adds a third subject — the provider response path — and it lands in the same band: 52.45% / 55.5% for rules, 47.25% / 53.52% for a frontier model, neither clearing 56%.

That the ceiling recurs across three unrelated subjects is the interesting part. It suggests the constraint belongs to the class of task — reconstructing a cause from a trace that was not built to record it — rather than to any one instrument.

The inverse relationship is the operational finding. The faults that cost the most success are the ones diagnosed worst. Truncation is worth up to 52.63 points on a pipeline system, and the rule-based scanner names it 4.3% of the time; empty responses are worth 22.5–56.73 points, and while the scanner names the type 96.74% of the time it locates the step in 38.04%. Meanwhile timeouts — near-harmless in the intermittent regime — are diagnosed at 91.04% / 89.55%, because a timeout announces itself. This is fail-loud-instrumentation-over-plausible-substitutes arriving one layer below where this map has been looking: the pipeline is not writing a plausible substitute, the provider is, and a truncated reply is a perfectly well-formed one that just stops.

The two methods are complementary, and the disagreement statistic proves it rather than the accuracy table. Cohen's κ is 0.813–0.907 on crash faults — the two methods are effectively one instrument there — and collapses to 0.098–0.372 on omission and value faults, with the LLM catching truncation at 34.41% where the rules catch 4.30% and the rules catching schema at 52.46% where the LLM manages 27.05%. That is the same layering argument a-deterministic-recheck-beats-a-learned-monitor-on-precision makes for detection, restated for diagnosis: run the deterministic signature scanner and escalate to a model, because they fail on disjoint classes. Neither is a cheaper approximation of the other.

And the failure is mostly silent, which is where it meets this map's founding finding. Between 21.82% and 65.71% of induced failures surface nothing to the user — the conversation pattern is the worst offender at 65.71%, the single-agent loop at 61.09%. silent-errors-dominate-agent-bug-reports put the modal symptom at 42.4% of organically filed agent bugs; this is the same shape under a known, injected cause, which means a monitor keyed on exceptions or exit codes will not see the majority of what a degraded provider does to an unattended fleet.

Caveats: one agent system (Mini-SE) and one benchmark for the diagnosis experiment, chosen because its traces are longest; the rule signatures were derived from public API documentation rather than from the injector, which is the right discipline but leaves their coverage of a real provider's failure vocabulary untested; and diagnosis accuracy is computed only over failed cases, so this says nothing about false alarms on healthy runs.
