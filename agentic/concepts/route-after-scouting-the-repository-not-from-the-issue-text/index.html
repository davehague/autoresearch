The router's problem is not which model is strongest; it is whether the cheap one will do here

This map's thesis is that agent spend is a sequential learned-budget-allocation problem and that the allocator has to be fitted to the traffic it serves. Every allocator it has carried so far reads something about the run — the issue text (route-the-session-not-the-query), live traffic (route-with-a-classifier-trained-on-live-traffic), a trajectory prefix (a-tiny-monitor-prices-a-doomed-trajectory). This one reads the repository, before dispatch, with a 7B model that costs under half a cent of GPU time per task, and hands what it found to whichever fixer is chosen.

The economics are the result and they are large. On the full 266-task Python census of SWE-bench Pro under the benchmark's own capped tier, the system solves 159 where the best single frontier model solves 158, at $0.230 per solve against $1.274 — about a fifth. The one-task edge at n = 266 is a match and the authors say so; the cost ratio is the finding. The accounting is deliberately conservative against the system: system rows are all-in (routed fixer API plus the searcher's entire GPU bill plus verification sandbox infrastructure) while solo rows count fixer API spend only.

The baseline that makes it interpretable is one this vault should demand everywhere. Any point on the segment between a cheap model's (cost, accuracy) and a strong model's is reachable by randomly splitting traffic — blind mixing — so a router is only interesting above that segment. The reported configuration sits +3.60 points above it. That is the routing analogue of charge-every-search-method-the-same-budget: a cost-quality point means nothing until you know what chance would have bought at the same price.

Two mechanism findings travel further than the headline. First, decoding, not training, is what made the searcher useful: greedy decoding finds the right files at 0.110 and a single sampled draw at temperature 0.9 reaches 0.306 — a 2.65× gain that decomposes into the searcher's willingness to commit to a handoff rising 3.4× against an 18% cost in per-handoff recall. An RL rig was built, ran 50 clean steps, learned nothing, and was shelved after an autopsy found the reward aimed at a behaviour already at ceiling; the model could localize when it chose to, and sampling recovered the choosing. Second, the search behaviour transfers off its training languages: on the nine-language multilingual suite, spontaneous-handoff file F1 is 0.630 on six never-trained languages against 0.455 on three trained ones, surviving a difficulty-matched control (0.791 vs 0.524 on single-gold-file tasks) — the authors' hypothesis, held as a hypothesis, being that reading directory trees and tracing imports is a structural strategy rather than a vocabulary. The contrast carries a real confound they name: the weakest trained cells are also the smallest training slices and may be structurally harder to localize in.

Bounds. One benchmark's Python slice, three repositories, so the effective diversity is closer to three codebases than to 266 independent draws (per-repository recall 0.472–0.671). All absolute rates are specific to the capped, silent-budget, auto-submit tier — the auto-submit-at-cap mechanism alone rescued 24 of one frontier model's 158 solves and 33 of another's 139, so the tier is worth 9–13 points. Costs are provider list prices at measurement time with one fixer served as a quantized build. Contamination control is one-sided: the searcher's training corpus excludes a 23-repository blocklist covering every evaluation repository, verified by a programmatic gate, and no such control is possible on the four closed fixers.
