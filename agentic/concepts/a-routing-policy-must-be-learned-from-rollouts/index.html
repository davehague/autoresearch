You cannot prompt your way to a router

The vault has been accumulating a claim about the cheap tier: it is something you build, not something you pick from a price list. Harness adaptation recovers up to 89.7% of frontier performance at 4% of cost (move-task-difficulty-from-the-model-to-the-harness); cutting the tool surface held pass rate while costing less in 3 of 4 regime×agent cells (restrict-the-tool-surface-to-cut-cost-not-capability); cache design made a frontier API cheaper per token than locally-served quantized weights (prompt-caching-inverts-cloud-versus-local-economics); 0.5–4B specialists staff production loop stages at 0.02–0.1× frontier cost. This adds the routing policy itself to that list, and it is the most counter-intuitive entry, because routing is a judgment call and judgment is what frontier models are for.

Four frontier models were handed the problem statement, the execution verdict, the stderr trace and the three action descriptions, and asked to choose. The best of them matched always-replan — a policy with no intelligence in it whatsoever — while spending four times as much, and two of them scored below always-replan. A 4B model fine-tuned on the deployment's own rollouts nearly doubled the best zero-shot score. The diagnosis is that the routing signal is not semantic. Nothing in "repair using the execution feedback" versus "escalate to a stronger model" tells you that this stderr shape on this benchmark at this difficulty is the 28% that cheap recovery solves. That mapping exists only in the rollouts, which means it exists only after you have paid to collect them — and it is presumably specific to your model pair, your action set and your task distribution.

The ablation reinforces the point in a way that is easy to act on: a coarse metadata prefix — source, difficulty tag, algorithm tag — bought more than upgrading the backbone did. Routers are not starved of reasoning; they are starved of distributional cues that execution feedback alone does not carry, especially on wrong-output failures where the trace says little.

The practical shape of the advice: treat the router as a component you train and re-train, budget for rollout collection as a first-class cost, and expect the policy to go stale. That last point is this map's standing open question, sharpened rather than answered — nobody has measured how fast a routing policy decays across a model or harness upgrade, and per harness-is-tuned-per-model-and-version the upgrades never stop. A learned router is a recurring bill, not a one-time build; the offsetting news is that the retraining artifact is 4B, and the budget knob on top of it re-calibrates from a stored table without touching the weights at all.

Caveats: one 360-example test split; competitive-programming and function-level benchmarks rather than repository-scale SWE work; a single primary model pair (GPT-5.4-nano/GPT-5.4) with one cross-check on a Gemini pair; the prompt-only routers were run zero-shot with no attempt at prompt engineering or few-shot examples, so this bounds the naive approach rather than the best possible prompted one.
