Manually read raw generations before trusting bias statistics

Derived directly from a case where it would have worked, the paper prescribes a specific, cheap check for analysts who cannot avoid working in the oracle-less regime (oracle-bearing-corpora-catch-degeneration-for-free): before any aggregate bias number is computed, for every distinct generation condition (each producer × language × prompt template), read 15–20 raw generated items in full. The sample-size floor comes from n ≥ ln(1−C)/ln(1−p) — at a 99% confidence target and a 30% reference fault rate (borrowed, conservatively, from the paper's own positive-control injection rate), n=13 items suffice to see at least one faulty item; fifteen is offered as a floor and twenty as a margin against lower or more diffuse fault rates. Alongside the read, report the mean word count of the generated field per condition — in the paper's own case the corrupted and corrected pipelines produced barely-overlapping length distributions (roughly 2 words vs 20–47 words), and a length that sits far from what the generation prompt asked for is the first mechanical flag. Finally, report two degeneration rates: the fraction of items whose generated field is identical to its source factual field (verbatim repeat), and the fraction that begin with a conversational opener or consist of a fragment shorter than three words. The paper's own fault scored 12.2% and 81.0% respectively. The prescribed decision rule is blunt on purpose: a condition whose degeneration rate rises above single digits should be resolved — the pipeline fixed, or the condition excluded — before it enters the statistics, not flagged with a footnote afterward.

The protocol is offered as a worked example, not a universal standard, and the paper is unusually careful about the distinction: what generalizes is the shape of the diagnostic (a generation fault of this class leaves a bimodal signature — the affected condition's length and degeneration rate separate sharply from the unaffected ones), not the specific thresholds, which the paper derives from its own single case. A task whose well-formed answers are themselves short, or whose faults manifest as subtle paraphrase rather than truncation, needs its own length and degeneration baselines established first, not the numbers above imported wholesale. The paper also applies the standard against itself consistently: it names, rather than hides, that it never ran this same protocol on its own English reference corpus (HaluEval), trusting it on reputational grounds instead — an honest demonstration that the check is easy to skip even when you know to run it.

For evaluation-integrity, this is the map's first concrete, bounded, pre-registration-style gate specifically for synthetic corpus construction, sitting upstream of every other misgrading pattern the map tracks: it targets aggregate-robustness-checks-miss-stimulus-level-faults's failure mode directly, and it is cheap enough (fifteen items, no API cost beyond what was already spent generating them) that "we didn't have budget to check" is not an available excuse.

Caveats: the fifteen-to-twenty-item threshold and the single-digit degeneration cutoff are the values this one case happened to exhibit, offered explicitly as a worked example rather than a calibrated standard; the protocol catches item-level degeneration, not judge bias itself — a corpus of perfectly well-formed items can still measure a biased judge, and passing this check is not a substitute for an oracle. Single author, arXiv v1, no venue. Single-study strength.
