The map's within-agent cost evidence has mostly measured tokens-per-solve and cache economics without separating turns from observation size as competing cost drivers; this source runs that separation directly and finds the field's dominant optimization target is the smaller of the two terms

This map's cost thread — the two-model-by-three-harness cost/accuracy split, the serving-cache reproducibility findings, the per-call preamble cost-spread result — has established that cost and capability move independently and that cache/serving-layer details are a real term in the bill. What none of it isolated is which structural property of a trajectory actually drives cost: the size of what the tools return (the thing most compression research targets) or how many iterations the loop takes (turn count, which this map's loop-termination and repair-round findings touch but have not costed directly). Run as a direct 600-trajectory correlational audit, the answer is lopsided: turn count explains 84% of log-cost variance, observation size 4%. The most expensive trajectories in the corpus are the ones with the most turns and the smallest observations — the inverse of what an observation-compression story predicts, and a genuine pushback against treating tool-output size as the primary cost lever this map's prior sources have implicitly assumed when they report tokens-per-solve without decomposing it.

The caching number sharpens this map's existing cost-channel findings rather than replacing them. 98.6% of input tokens across the corpus are served from a prefix cache, which means flat per-token pricing — the unit most of this map's cost comparisons implicitly use — overstates real cost by roughly 7x on average under near-ideal caching. That is a measurement-validity note for every cost figure on this map computed without accounting for cache discounting, adjacent to model-routing-token-economics's substrate-cost strand. Interaction edge: model-routing-token-economics — the derived break-even condition for delegating a turn to a cheaper model, and the finding that current delegation practice sits close to that boundary (increasing cost in about half of evaluated settings), is a direct instance of that map's allocation- policy strand measured against a term (turn count) this map supplies.

Why this extends rather than confirms a specific prior claim. It does not contest that cost and capability vary independently or that the apparatus matters — no single existing note on this map asserts observation size as the dominant cost driver outright — it adds the decomposition (turns vs. observations) nobody on this map had run, and finds the field's actual compression research effort is aimed at the smaller of the two terms.

Bounds: 600 trajectories, two scaffolds, two models — a correlational audit, not a controlled intervention removing turns and measuring task success; delegation's break-even result is reported as near-boundary on existing practice, not validated as a winning policy; workshop publication (POVC '26 @ ASE 2026), not yet independently replicated.
