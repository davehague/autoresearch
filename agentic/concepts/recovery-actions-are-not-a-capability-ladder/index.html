The next increment of effort has a shape, and the shapes are not ranked by price

This map's headline — allocate spend sequentially, under a global budget, from a policy learned on cheap observable state — now has a third independent T1 behind it, and this one moves the claim rather than merely replicating it. route-the-session-not-the-query allocates models across a session; learn-when-to-stop-not-just-which-model allocates iterations, including the decision to stop. This allocates the kind of the next increment: repair the failing solution using the execution feedback, discard and re-plan cheaply, or escalate. The general form the map states — the unit of routing is the next increment of effort — survives all three. What it gains is a second dimension: that increment has a shape as well as a size, and the shapes are not a ladder.

The 28 / 27 / 45 split is the finding. Nearly a third of recoverable failures are solvable only by a cheap action, which is capacity a cascade leaves on the table by construction, and the split moves by domain — BigCodeBench is dominated by cheap-only recoveries, where oracle routing cuts mean cost by more than 10×, while the harder TACO tiers are escalation-heavy. This is why the cascade looks defensible in aggregate and is expensive in practice: it is right about the 45% and wrong about the 28%, and it pays full escalation price for both. The learned router beating always-escalate on solve rate and cost (81.7% at 5.51 m$ vs 68.6% at 7.22 m$) is not a cost-quality trade at all — the fixed policy is simply dominated.

The number practitioners should sit with is always-reflect at 27.5%, the worst of the three fixed policies, below even naive discard-and-retry. Feeding the error back and asking for a repair is the default recovery every coding harness ships. It is the weakest single thing you can do with a failure. That composes with the map's sibling limb in loop-engineering, where repair rounds past a small optimum destroy validity (repair-loops-have-an-interior-optimum): iterate-on-feedback is neither the best first move nor safe to repeat, and both results were measured in the same fortnight.

The budget mechanism deserves a line because it is the part that ships. A Conformal Risk Control layer calibrates a deployment-time cost penalty over a stored table of breakpoints, so changing the budget is a re-scan rather than a retraining run — which is the practical objection to budget-aware routing methods that bake the budget into the training objective. The guarantee is deliberately on expected cost, not solve rate, precisely because the actions are non-monotone: cost-quality curves here need not be concave, operating points must be chosen empirically, and at intermediate budgets the policy can over-route to cheap actions on instances only escalation solves. That honesty is the right shape for this map, whose standing open question is exactly the tail behaviour that average-case routing results hide.

One number bounds everything above: ~30% of attempted problems fail initially and are recovered by no action in the set. Routing improves the reachable population; it does not enlarge it.

Caveats: 360-example test split, so frontier points rest on a few hundred instances; the tasks are competitive-programming and function-level benchmarks rather than repository-scale SWE work, where execution feedback is noisier and "recovery" is many steps rather than one call; three actions are explicitly a minimal first-order set; the cheapest-successful training label is a convenience, not a calibrated success probability; the CRC guarantee is marginal over calibration draws, and any solve-rate gain from moving the penalty is an empirical property of the learned router, not a conformal one.
