The number has a review attached, and almost nobody prints it

This map has been accumulating reasons a benchmark score is not what it says: the tasks are contaminated, the labels are wrong, the judge is biased, the proxy ranks the wrong thing, agents-reach-for-the-answer-key-unprompted. Here is a quieter one. On any suite whose rewards are executed rather than judged, some fraction of successful trajectories is under review for having obtained the reward by a route the task did not intend — and the headline is reported before that review concludes, if the review is mentioned at all.

The submission in question makes the review visible. 95.28% is the raw pipeline figure and the authors label it as such every time they use it. They concede four rewarded trajectories identified in review should not count, which gives 94.38%. And they print the reading with all nine currently-flagged trajectories zeroed: 93.26%. Two points of spread, on one submission, with nothing varied but how strictly you adjudicate — and two points is comfortably inside the range this vault routinely sees quoted as a model difference.

The disclosure watch item under this map's benchmark-validity limb has been collecting practices of this shape: reporting the searches that were not performed, the corpus that grades its own evidence strength against its own urgency and prints the disagreement (urgency-and-evidence-strength-are-nearly-uncorrelated), the billing regime behind a cost figure. This adds two. Report the raw score as a band, not a point, and name the adjudication state. And say what the profile was allowed to see: the same paper declares its construction boundary in advance — visible task specification, workspace artifacts, public documentation and observable execution feedback are in; hidden tests, verifier implementations, public solutions, answer artifacts, task hashes and enumerated task identifiers as routing keys are out. That is a testable statement about how far the tuning was allowed to reach toward the answer key, which is precisely the thing gold-smoke-an-execution-benchmark-before-you-trust-a-delta and agents-reach-for-the-answer-key-unprompted establish nobody can be trusted to leave alone by default.

The declared boundary also shows its own limit, and the paper is the one that shows it. It forbids reading the verifier — and the harness still absorbed the verifier's undocumented conventions anyway, through repeated evaluator feedback (failure-driven-optimization-absorbs-the-evaluators-conventions). So a construction boundary is necessary and demonstrably not sufficient: it fences the direct channel and leaves the gradient channel open.

One footnote worth carrying separately, because it is the same disclosure discipline applied to money. The $1,062.95 attached to this submission is the pipeline's API-equivalent estimate; the authors state their realized expenditure was under $125 of a $200 personal flat-rate subscription. A cost figure computed under one billing regime and read under another is a different number, which is the point pricing-model-errors-are-invisible-in-the-final-number makes about the model side of the ledger and this makes about the benchmark's own reporting.

What this is not. One submission, self-reported, on one benchmark, by the authors of the system being scored — so this is a practice worth copying and not a prevalence estimate for how wide the band usually is. Nobody has measured that, and the interesting question it opens is whether two points is typical or unusually candid.
