A free-to-compute uncertainty signal — reusing activations the weak agent already produces — drives escalation routing without the training-from-rollouts this map's other learned routers require, and extends the same signal past the escalation decision into post-handoff oversight of the strong agent

This map's standing claim on the allocation-policy strand is that a routing policy is a trained artifact, not a prompt — zero-shot escalation by a frontier model underperforms a fixed policy, and the useful router has to be fine-tuned on the deployment's own rollouts (a-routing-policy-must-be-learned-from-rollouts). LEASH offers a third path between "zero-shot prompt" and "trained-on-rollouts classifier": a training-free signal computed by comparing the weak agent's own hidden state at each step to its own stored history of past successful and failed trajectories. Because the signal reuses activations the weak agent already produces during normal action generation, it needs no extra forward pass and no dedicated monitor training — cheaper to deploy than either alternative this map has priced, at the cost of requiring white-box access to the weak model's hidden states (ruling out the signal where the weak model is itself served as a black-box API).

On the economics specifically: matching an always-on frontier model's task success at 19.2% lower cost is a direct instance of this map's "treat cost as a first-class signal, allocate sequentially" principle, applied to a domain (long-horizon computer-use GUI tasks) the map's existing coding-agent-centric routing evidence has not covered. The 9.1-point gain over random escalation at matched cost is the map's clearest demonstration yet that which step triggers escalation matters as much as how much budget is allocated to escalation overall.

The oversight layer is the genuinely new object. After handoff, LEASH repurposes the weak model as a "shadow observer," using the same latent-uncertainty comparison to keep watching the strong, otherwise-opaque agent's trajectory — a governance function riding on a cost-routing signal rather than a separate control. This is the sharpest point of contact with runtime-action-governance's standing concern that a black-box strong agent, once unleashed, has no internals a cheaper layer can monitor: LEASH's shadow observer is exactly such a layer, built from a signal the routing decision already computed for free. Filed primarily here because the headline numbers are a cost-routing result; the shadow-observer mechanism is the interaction edge worth reading from the governance side.

Where it sits against the map's other escalation caution: escalating-mid-run-can-cost-more-than-starting-over found mid-run model switching recovering less than half its expected value and, for one tested pair, strictly dominated by discard-and-restart. LEASH's result does not contradict this — it is a different decision (when to escalate at all, triggered by a calibrated uncertainty signal) rather than the mechanics of the handoff itself, and the two findings compose: a good escalation trigger does not make a bad escalation mechanism good, and vice versa.

Bounds: evaluated only on OSWorld computer-use tasks across four backbones; untested on coding or tool-use-dialogue domains this map usually draws from. The uncertainty score estimates the weak agent's own failure risk, not escalation's expected benefit — the authors' own Proposition 1 needs escalation gain non-decreasing in that score, which can fail when both agents struggle on the same task. Post-handoff shadow-observer oversight is not causally isolated from the routing and escalation layers in this evaluation. Single industry-lab paper (Simular), not yet independently replicated.
