The bottleneck moved, and so did the supervision — off the gate rather than onto more reviewers

review-capacity-is-what-a-loop-actually-spends measured the throughput end of this from public GitHub: 2.83 million agent-authored pull requests, 8.8% receiving an AI review, median first-review latency of 1.2 minutes cross-product. That is the field's answer to who closes the gate. This is a much smaller instrument answering a question the population data cannot reach: what did teams change when the gate stopped fitting?

The answer is not more reviewers. It is three layers, and the paper's distinction between the first two is the part worth stealing, because it is drawn by mechanism rather than sequence. A preventive guardrail — a specification, a steering file, an architectural plan — shapes what gets generated, and its effect depends on whether the generation process actually consults it. An executable guardrail — a lint rule, a test, a CI check — is evaluated against the output regardless of how the output was produced. They are not stages. They run concurrently, deliberately, so that the executable check still fires when the steering artifact is stale, ignored, or simply absent from that particular trajectory. That is a design rule this vault can use directly, and it is the practical answer to a-stale-convention-file-costs-more-than-no-file: you do not fix a stale convention file by trusting it harder, you shadow it with a check that does not care whether it was read.

Two pressures, and only one of them is volume. The obvious one: P2 reports code writing has become "very cheap" with "the bottleneck clearly moving to review"; P1 puts manual review of an AI-assisted one-shot implementation at three to four times the generation time. The less obvious one is what the authors name false correctness — generated artifacts that are syntactically correct and locally coherent yet cannot be validated in context. P2 could no longer change parts of a system because he could not trace "why they are now where they are." P5, in his written survey response before any interview: "functionally correct, but nobody understands why." This is the mechanism behind the finding this map already holds from the measurement side — that the loop now closes faster than a human could open the diff and nothing in the data says a defect is more likely to be caught. False correctness explains why: the validation burden moved off behavior, which is the only thing a fast reviewer checks well.

Human oversight changed shape twice, and both changes are legible. In altitude — P4 describes replacing full comprehension with operational explainability, the requirement that generated systems stay diagnosable and reconstructable on demand via abstraction layers, visualization, logs and interpretive tooling. And in time — P1 describes observing generation "step-by-step … interrupt early" rather than reviewing after completion, which relocates the human from a gate to a monitor. The threshold is explicitly not a single bar: a short-lived UX prototype may need no line-level understanding, while a database migration demands precise comprehension of every statement. That is the same context-dependence type-the-transition-check-by-what-can-actually-verify-it argues for at the machine layer, arriving at the human one.

The denominators are the honest part and they cut against the enthusiasm. Only 13 of 50 use steering files at all and 6 of 50 use structured Requirements → Design → Tasks workflows. Read beside the-first-configuration-a-team-commits-is-usually-its-last — 73.8% of committed AI configuration written once and never revised, across 441 repositories — the picture is consistent from two independent instruments: externalization is the practice everyone describes and few have adopted, and among those who have, almost nobody maintains it.

Bounds, and they are large. Five interviews, convenience-sampled from one alumni network with an unpiloted instrument, over-representing Swiss and Central European contexts. Reported practice and perception, not observed behavior — no repository data, no longitudinal measurement. Single-coder thematic analysis with no inter-rater reliability assessment, mitigated by member-checking and written follow-ups. One of the five participants is a co-author, disclosed with two named bias risks and two stated mitigations. The survey is explicitly not offered as inferential evidence. The 3–4× review ratio is one participant's estimate, not a measurement. Treat every number here as a well-documented anecdote with a denominator attached, and the framing — three layers, mechanism-not-sequence, false correctness — as the durable contribution.
