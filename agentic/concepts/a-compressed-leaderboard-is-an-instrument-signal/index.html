A benchmark that cannot tell a flagship from its predecessor is making a claim about itself

The useful transferable move in the SciCode audit is not the defect taxonomy but the trigger. Nobody suspected the benchmark because a test failed; they suspected it because the scores had a shape a working instrument does not produce — the four strongest models of the year within four points of one another, and a successor model scoring exactly what its predecessor scored. From there the reasoning is a two-branch disjunction anyone can run: either the capability is genuinely absent, or the instrument is compressing. The second branch is checkable and the first is not.

The mechanism that produces the compression is worth stating plainly, because it explains why plateaus are systematically more likely to be instrument artifacts than they look. Every model meets the same defective step. A defect that fails all of them subtracts from all of them, and subtracting a constant from the top of a distribution that is already near its own ceiling compresses the differences that remain. Where the benchmark also scores all-or-nothing over a cumulative chain — as this one does — the subtraction is multiplied: one bad step forfeits the problem, and a model that would have differentiated itself downstream never gets there.

This sits beside the other members of the map's the-agents-were-right-and-were-marked-wrong ledger — an-evaluator-sharing-its-reference-solver-cannot-be-self-checked (the evaluator contradicted its own inputs and nothing internal could catch it) and a-unanimous-agent-failure-flags-a-broken-task (read failures across agents, not per agent) — and it generalizes both to the aggregate. Where the unanimous-failure rule reads one task's transcripts, this reads the leaderboard's shape; where the shared-solver finding says a benchmark cannot audit itself from inside, this says the distribution of scores is the one signal available from outside without redoing the science.

The rule has a stated expiry, exactly like its siblings. It discriminates only while the ceiling is not real: once models genuinely saturate a corrected benchmark — and this one is already at 98.3% subproblem accuracy for the leader with the expert background supplied — compression means saturation and the diagnostic stops working. It is a construction-and-maintenance-time instrument, not a permanent one, and the honest version of it is: a plateau licenses an audit, not a conclusion in either direction.

Bounds. This is one benchmark and one audit, and the plateau-implies-defect inference is supported by a single successful instance rather than by a survey of plateaued benchmarks — a benchmark can plateau for perfectly ordinary reasons. The audit that resolved it was expensive and expertise-gated (five research fields, per- problem re-derivation, an adversarial second pass, all internal to the author team with no external blind validation), so "audit the plateau" is a cheap thing to suspect and an expensive thing to settle. Carried as suggestive: the reading rule transfers, the inference does not license a conclusion on its own.
