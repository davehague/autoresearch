If the strong model solves nearly everything the cheap one does, there is nothing to combine

Routing literature is largely written as if models were complementary specialists — different strengths, and a router that picks the right one beats any of them alone. On repository-level issue resolution that premise appears not to hold. Replayed over three benchmarks' published per-task outcomes at zero model cost, the weaker model's solve set is contained in the strongest's at 0.941 / 0.912 / 0.773. The obvious objection — that this is an artifact of comparing cheap models against frontier ones — is tested and fails: pools built from frontier models alone, one per lab, leave containment at 0.90–0.93. Four families of learned router were tried and none exceeded always calling the strongest model.

The consequence is a reframing rather than a defeat, and it is the useful part. Between any two models, the accuracy prize is the thin sliver either solves alone; the large shared region is where a cheaper model would have sufficed all along. Accuracy routing has to find the sliver and cannot. Cost routing only has to predict membership in the shared region, which requires no complementary skills at all — and the shared region is most of the benchmark. That is the same move this map made when it stopped treating the allocator as a quality device and started treating it as a budget device (expose-a-cost-quality-dial-not-a-fixed-policy, learn-when-to-stop-not-just-which-model), now with a measured reason why the quality direction is closed on this task class.

A second-order observation from the same study's solo baselines sharpens the point in the other direction: price does not order capability. Kimi K2.5 solo resolves 149 of 266 at $0.106 per task while GPT-5.2 solo resolves 139 at $0.570 — dominating on both axes simultaneously, +3.76 points at roughly a fifth the cost. Where a cheaper model is also more accurate, no routing sophistication is required to capture it and none of the deployed routers this map tracks would have found it from task text.

Bounds. This is an offline replay of other groups' published outcome tables, not a controlled experiment: the routers are the authors' reimplementations, the containment figures depend on which models were in each published pool, and "every observed gap fell within noise" is stated without a floor of the kind coordination-deltas-need-a-measured-noise-floor would demand. The claim is confined to repository-level issue resolution on SWE-bench-family benchmarks — themselves under this vault's standing contamination advisory (benchmark-scores-inflate-when-agents-can-retrieve-the-fix), which if anything would inflate containment by making the same retrievable tasks solvable for everyone. Nothing here says solve sets are nested on other task classes, and the authors do not claim it. Carried as suggestive: the design implication is what transfers.
