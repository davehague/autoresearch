The map says a human must review the spec because the agent cannot — this is the first measurement of what happens when the human brings the agent along

agents-are-weak-at-spec-critique is one of this map's founding claims and the reason its prescription has a human in it: the best agent finds 44.4% of design-proposal defects, so the spec-review phase is a load-bearing gate rather than optional polish. Every reader of that note has to decide what "a human reviews the spec" means in a shop where the human has a chat window open. This source is the first controlled answer, and it is not the comfortable one: the assisted human found fewer defects than the unassisted one, and gained no time for it.

The result splits along a seam worth keeping. Model support hurt detection — the scan for indicators — and did not touch classification, the judgement of whether a found smell is nocuous or innocuous. Once a participant had correctly identified a smell, they reasoned about its severity just as well either way. The authors read these as cognitively distinct activities and note the same split has been reported before, where inspectors gave accurate justifications once a violation was correctly identified regardless of what representation supported them. That is a usable boundary: the model appears to displace the sweep, not the judgement.

The proposed mechanism is delegation, and it lines up with what this vault already holds about human-agent handoffs. The plausible reading is that participants offloaded part of the inspection to the model and stopped engaging with the requirements themselves — automation bias, in a population without the domain expertise to catch a miss. It is the human-side counterpart of humans-silently-repair-agent-code-outside-review and of comprehension-debt-outpaces-review-capacity: the failure is not that the assistant is wrong, it is that its presence changes how hard the person looks.

What this does and does not do to the map. It does not contradict the standing claim — nothing here says the agent could do the review instead, and the finding makes the gate more important rather than less. What it pushes back on is the unexamined assumption that the gate's yield is a constant once you have staffed it with a human. The practice implication the authors give is a placement rule, not a prohibition: keep model assistance out of the initial detection pass and put it in post-inspection review or justification refinement — which is the same shape as elicit-the-spec-in-its-own-phase-before-synthesis, phase-separating a task rather than banning a tool from it.

Bounds — and this one needs its caveat quoted, not summarized. On the headline detection contrast the 95% credibility intervals overlap; the effect the authors characterize as clear is the period × sequence interaction, not this −8% main effect. Treating −8% as a settled negative overstates what the model supports, which is why this note is graded suggestive rather than strong. Participants are bachelor students with no practical inspection experience, and the over-reliance mechanism is argued specifically for novices — an experienced inspector with an established strategy may be a different case entirely, and the authors say replication with professionals is needed. The documents are simpler than industrial requirements and constrained to at most one smell per requirement. Ground truth was reconciled by author discussion with no inter-rater agreement metric computed. Nothing here speaks to a model used to generate, repair, or statically lint a specification — only to a human consulting one while inspecting.
