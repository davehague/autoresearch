Correctness and contribution are different quantities, and the field has been grading the first

Everything this map builds on trajectory-level attribution rests on an assumption nobody had tested: that the credit a per-step signal assigns tracks what the step actually contributed. This audit tests it against ground truth that does not consult the signals at all, and the answer is no.

The instrument is the contribution. Executed replay: at every decision point, re-sample K=4 alternative actions the policy itself supports, roll each forward to terminal, and take the shift in outcome distribution against at least three replays of the factual action. The realized continuation is never used as the factual estimate — the contrast is between two distributions. This measures contribution, which is different from correctness, and the paper's framing is the load-bearing part: a correct step can contribute nothing because the trajectory was already determined, and an incorrect step can be pivotal because it opened the state recovery came from. Only contribution is what a training loop pays for, and existing step-level benchmarks grade against annotated correctness.

Against it, everything is placebo-level. The verdict order was frozen before collection with the controls gate first: a family whose interval overlaps its own marginal-matched shuffle is placebo-level regardless of its point estimate. Implicit credit, judge scores, and the policy's own confidence all fail it, in both model families, under both generations of the instrument — and the paper's re-run after a chat-template defect is the closest an audit of this kind gets to internal replication: the repair moved the measurability map materially and did not move a single verdict class.

The result is not that credit anti-tracks contribution. It is that nothing distinguishes the trained scorer's ranking from the same scores with their step-assignment shuffled. Three measurements sharing no estimator, no test statistic and no failure mode agree.

What it does and does not overturn here. This does not touch outcome-level grading, and it does not say trajectory evidence is worthless — audit-agent-claims-against-trajectory-evidence is about claims against gathered evidence, a different object. What it bounds is the per-step ranking layer: localize-first-wrong-step-not-final-answer and close-the-loop-from-attribution-to-rerun both assume a step-level signal that concentrates on the steps that mattered, and this is the first measurement of that assumption against executed causal ground truth. It sits directly alongside first-wrong-step-attribution-is-mostly-wrong, which reached a compatible verdict from the accuracy side (28.8–38.0% strict agent-and-step) against annotated labels; this reaches it against causal ones, and is the stronger statement because it removes the possibility that the annotations were the problem.

Two structural facts about the ground truth travel with the null and matter more than it, because they scope every replay-based attribution method:

  • Contribution is sparse. 30.5% of measurable turns are pivotal; the per-turn noise floor is exactly zero at 86.0% of them. Most of a trajectory is causally inert. An attribution instrument's job is to find the minority that is not, and its baseline for "doing nothing" is very high.
  • Measurability is model-dependent in both directions. The fraction of turns with no policy-supported counterfactual is 13.1% for one 7B policy and 26.8% for a similar-scale 8B one — a factor of 2.05 with non-overlapping intervals, driven by probability-mass concentration rather than capability — while the family that is harder to measure is the one with more causal signal where measurement is possible (38.3% pivotal vs 30.5%). Replay auditability is a property of the (policy, environment) pair, not an environment constant, and the exclusions are right-truncated on the fluency axis (included-minus-excluded mean log-probability −0.70).

The mechanism behind the null is separately identified: implicit-credit-is-a-fluency-echo.
