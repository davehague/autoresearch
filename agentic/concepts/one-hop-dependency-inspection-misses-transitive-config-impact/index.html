Change the model, and the thing that breaks is two hops away

The practical question a configuration model has to earn its keep on is: I changed one prompt / swapped one model / edited one policy — what do I now have to re-check? The intuitive answer is "whatever depends on it directly", and this is the only measurement in the vault that tests it.

On a graph deliberately built to contain a two-level dependency, one-hop traversal recovered 6 of 9 affected items; transitive fixed-point propagation recovered 9 of 9. The three misses were reached through an intermediate revision — the shape where a model change touches an agent, which is composed into a subsystem, which a workflow calls. A third of the blast radius, invisible to the check most people would write.

Two properties make the propagation usable rather than merely correct. It is scope-independent: iteration count was 3 for local, intermediate and global changes alike, matching the O(|E_C|) bound, so the check does not get slower as the change gets bigger. And it is deterministic — five repetitions of every case produced identical impact sets, governance states and metrics, on a substrate whose ordinary output is stochastic. That is the point of governing configuration rather than behaviour: the answer to "what did I just touch" should not vary between runs of the tool that answers it.

The inspection-scope numbers — 84.6% reduction for a local change, 69.2% intermediate, 61.5% global — are the marketing figure and should be read with the paper's own qualifier, which is unusually honest: they count configuration elements no longer requiring verification, not inspection time, operational cost or human productivity. On an eleven-item graph, "84.6% reduction" means nine items instead of eleven.

Bounds, which are severe enough to keep this a design signal rather than an operational one. Eleven and thirteen items — three orders of magnitude below any real deployment, and the transitive-vs-one-hop comparison ran on a graph constructed to exhibit the dependency it then demonstrates, which makes it an existence proof rather than a prevalence estimate. Nobody has measured how often real agent configurations contain two-level dependencies, which is the number that would decide whether this matters. The identical results across three frameworks are an artifact of projection making the dependency structures equivalent, so Campaign B measures the algorithm, not cross-framework variation. Single author, no users, no production system, and no evidence that recovering the full impact set improves any outcome — see govern-the-agents-configuration-as-a-versioned-object for the rest of the model's bounds.
