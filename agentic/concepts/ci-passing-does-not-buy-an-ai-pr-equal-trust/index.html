CI passing was never the trust bar this map assumed it was — reviewers price AI-authored PRs on accountability signals that CI cannot check, and the penalty hits routine changes almost as hard as risky ones

This map's strand (2) has been assembling the human-judgment side of the verdict-diagnosis gap from adjacent angles: a 12-interview case study found perceived risk opens a persistent management/developer expectation gap, and a security-suggestion study found trust in the generation process lowers scrutiny without eliminating it. This survey supplies strand (2) its largest, most directly quantified reviewer- behavior dataset yet, and the headline result reframes something this map had been treating as settled — that CI-passing is the baseline trust threshold a PR clears before human judgment even engages. It isn't, for AI-authored PRs specifically: willingness to accept without further explanation drops significantly for every one of eight change types tested, including the three lowest-risk ones (cleanup, documentation, test additions), despite CI having already passed in every scenario presented. The authorship penalty is not an artifact of less-experienced reviewers being more cautious — it shows no significant association with reviewer experience at all, which rules out the easiest explanation for why the gap exists.

The paper's own concept for the mechanism — reviewability debt — gives this map vocabulary it has been missing. CI is necessary but insufficient because it can show checks passed without showing whether a refactor preserved behavior, a performance claim is meaningful, or a security-sensitive change was understood by an accountable human; the unresolved gap between "looks complete" and "is actually accountable" is debt the reviewer pays at the review interface, not later. That names precisely the mechanism behind this map's verdict-diagnosis entries: a verdict can be technically correct while concealing exactly the kind of missing grounding this survey's respondents describe triaging for.

Interaction edge: evaluation-integrity — this survey's reviewer-side finding (an AIPR's validation claim is trusted less even post-CI) is the human-judgment complement to that map's claim-authoring-side finding that roughly half of "validated" PRs (agentic and human alike) name no quantified metric at all (half-of-validated-agentic-prs-report-no-quantified-metric): one measures how often a validation claim is empty, the other measures how much reviewers discount a PR even when it isn't — together they bracket the review-trust gap from both sides of the claim.

Bounds: self-reported stated judgments from a questionnaire, not observed review behavior; "AIPR" is operationalized as perceived/self-reported authorship, not verified line-level provenance (the authors are explicit that generation, prompting, editing and validation are often intertwined); because the AIPR item always followed the HPR item for the same respondent, the measured gaps are stated as upper bounds on the pure authorship effect, not a clean causal estimate; constructs (reviewability, fairness, accountability) use study-specific items rather than validated psychometric scales.
