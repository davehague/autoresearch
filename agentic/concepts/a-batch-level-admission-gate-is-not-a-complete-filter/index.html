Where post-hoc curation is bounded, and where it is not

Last night's arrival made an unusually strong claim: post-hoc curation is bounded below pre-commit gating no matter how good the detector gets (skill-contamination-survives-removing-the-skill). The argument is structural rather than empirical — skills are distilled conditioned on the pool live at the time, so a defect admitted at round 3 is reference material for everything written afterwards, and removing it leaves its descendants. Source-only removal recovered 1.7 of 12.3 lost points; an oracle removing the full lineage recovered 6.7; 5.6 survived perfect provenance.

This source pushes back on both halves, and the useful outcome is a scope condition rather than a reversal.

Against the sufficiency of the gate. SkillProx's forward stage is an admission gate of exactly the shape this map has been endorsing: re-execute the candidate on the batch that produced it, accept only on non-decreasing accuracy, roll back regressions. The authors then measure what got through and report that one seed's gated skill still contained two estimated-negative units, with the reason stated plainly — "batch-level gates can miss content harmless locally but harmful under broader validation." They also decline the monotonicity claim the gate seems to license: it "constrains only the current training batch B_k … it does not imply monotonic performance across iterations, on the validation split, or on the test set." A gate whose acceptance test is drawn from the same distribution as the proposal is a filter, not a proof, and this is the same failure the pre-commit study found at the combinatorial level, where 21 individually-clean skills cost 8 points in company (an-unguarded-skill-pool-peaks-then-degrades).

Against the bound on removal. Here post-hoc removal is worth more than the admission loop, not less: −2.5 points when ablated against the closed-loop gate's −1.5, and a single section's removal moving validation hard accuracy 46% → 54% for 3.12% of the artifact (deletion-needs-its-own-instrument-not-a-generic-edit).

The reconciliation is the conditioning, and it is a clean boundary. The irreversibility result is about a pool whose members condition each other's authorship across rounds — the recovery gap is entirely the descendant lineage. SkillProx removes sections of one artifact, audited against a held-out validation split, where the removed unit is inert reference text that wrote nothing. So: post-hoc removal is bounded below pre-commit gating exactly where the removed material has conditioned later writes, and unbounded where it has not. The operational rule that follows is more useful than either paper alone — gate at admission, audit for removal, and record provenance, because provenance is what decides which of the two regimes you are in, and no shipped skill library records it.

Carried as suggestive and pushing back on a scope rather than on a result. The two studies share no benchmark, no backbone family and no unit of analysis — Terminal-Bench 2 skill pools across five evolution rounds against SpreadsheetBench sections inside one skill — so this is convergent reasoning about mechanism, not a replication attempt or a head-to-head. Neither has run the other's arm; the decisive experiment (a lineage-recording skill bank, gated at admission and audited for removal, against each intervention alone) is cheap and unrun.

Rebuts: skill-contamination-survives-removing-the-skill — on the claim that post-hoc curation is bounded below pre-commit gating unconditionally.
