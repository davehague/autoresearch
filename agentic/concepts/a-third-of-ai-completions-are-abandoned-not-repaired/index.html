The completion accepted at 10:00 and deleted at 10:23 is in nobody's dataset

This map has been assembling an argument that the maintenance bill for AI-generated code is undercounted by construction. Review comments appear on 2.18% of agentic PRs while humans backfill 72.5% of the gap in silent follow-up commits (humans-silently-repair-agent-code-outside-review); post-merge corrective maintenance runs 49% above the human baseline and scales with skipped review (agentic-code-carries-higher-corrective-maintenance). Both measure repair at commit granularity or later, and both note that whatever happens earlier is invisible.

This is what happens earlier. 31% of accepted completions are removed rather than repaired, and the modal removal lands 23.6 minutes after acceptance — inside the editor, before any commit exists. The timing distribution says the whole decision is made fast: half of all editing activity within 50 minutes, a visible cliff at 15. None of it produces a diff against a parent commit, a PR, a review comment, or a revert.

The sequencing analysis gives the behaviour a shape rather than a rate. Edit types occur at significantly different times (H = 394.5, p < 0.001) in a strict order: remove (23.6 min) → fix quality (28.3) → customize (49.3) → change functionality (59.2). Developers first settle whether to keep the code at all, and only then invest in it. And the path into abandonment is the counter-intuitive part: removal is likelier after a first attempt to customize (23%) than after fixing quality (14%) or changing behaviour (12%). The completion that gets thrown away is not the obviously broken one — it is the one close enough to invite adaptation and subtly enough misaligned to defeat it. The same asymmetry this map found on the reviewer side, where the harmful reviewer rewrites and the helpful one edits (a-weak-reviewer-rewrites-where-a-strong-one-edits).

The survival curve is bimodal, not centred: median 63% of the completion remains, but the mass sits at "kept nearly intact" and "removed nearly entirely," and developers add little of their own (20% of final code, with 36% of final files carrying under 5% human-added material). Adaptation is not the common outcome. That reframes the useful quality question from how much will this need fixing to is this right, and it is the strongest available argument that partial-credit measures of AI code quality are describing a mode that barely exists.

Two consequences worth carrying past this note. First, the acceptance rate — the field's most-quoted adoption number, and the denominator behind most "share of code written by AI" claims — is sampled at the moment of maximum optimism, and a third of it evaporates within the hour. Second, the interval is instrumentable: repair intent is distinguishable at minute scale and by type-ordering, so an editor that logged edit trajectories would have a per-completion quality signal needing no judge, no review comment and no merge outcome.

Scope bound, and it is a real one. The unit here is an accepted inline completion (median 97 characters, 9 lines), not an agent patch or a PR — so this transfers to the vault's agentic maps by layer and mechanism rather than as a measurement of coding agents. The population is opt-in through one VS Code extension over one window with unknown skew; only accepted completions are captured, so the 31% is conditioned on acceptance; edit-type labels are LLM-judge assignments by majority vote; and trajectory reconstruction is heuristic enough (header matching, git-diff extraction, a CodeBERTScore threshold of 0.68) to cut 78K pairs from 4K developers down to 56K from 1K, leaving the cleanly-reconstructable subset.
