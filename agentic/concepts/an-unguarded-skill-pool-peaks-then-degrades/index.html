More skills is not more capability, and the curve has a peak you can measure

This vault has carried the damage for weeks without the shape. An evolved skill set regresses about as much as it gains (skills-regress-as-much-as-they-gain); generic memory scaffolds degrade all ten models tested (memory-scaffolds-can-degrade-long-horizon-agents); LLM-written context files lose points at higher cost where developer-written ones gain (generated-context-files-cost-more-than-they-help). Every one of those is a level claim — this artifact costs you something. None of them says what happens as the artifact keeps growing.

It has a peak. Pass@1 climbs to 62% at 105 skills and falls to 50% at 179, ending two points above where it started after a fivefold increase in the pool. The authors call this the capability–contamination tipping point and formalize it as k* = argmax_k P(k), with three levels underneath: individual contamination (a skill whose singleton gain is negative), combinatorial (two skills each individually harmless whose joint gain is negative), and systemic (the non-monotone curve the first two produce).

The combinatorial level is the one that matters for engineering, because it is the one per-skill review cannot reach. Ablating the marginal-gain subset-selection gate — admitting every candidate that passed individual screening — grows the live pool 37 → 58 and drops pass@1 8 points. Those 21 extra skills each cleared a schema check, an A–B replay showing no individual degradation, and a semantic review. They were harmful only in company.

The mechanism is the same admission-gate shape this vault trusts everywhere else, moved one step earlier. Last week's arrival priced the regression tax as the cost of a missing rejection gate and generalized warrant-aware acceptance from cite the failure you are fixing to replay the episodes you might break (gate-a-skill-update-on-a-replay-of-what-it-might-break, harness-optimizers-fabricate-failures-to-fix, pre-write-admission-buys-reliability-by-serializing). This is a second group, one week apart, arriving at replay-before-admission independently — and its ablation says the same component is load-bearing: removing the held-out A–B replay is the single most expensive deletion at −10 points, because it is the only check that tests behaviour rather than form or plausibility.

Two details worth stealing whatever you think of the headline.

A conjunction of cheap heterogeneous critics beats one good one. Deterministic schema validation costs no inference, the A–B replay costs no inference, and only the semantic review spends one LLM call — and the first two reject most candidates before that call happens. The ablations show the three fail on different skills (malformed entries, silently harmful ones, plausible-but-fabricated advice), so none substitutes for another. That is a gate you can afford to run on every skill your fleet writes.

The gate is cheaper than the contaminant. Gating cost is paid once at admission; a bad skill is paid on every later trial and, through its descendants, in every later round. Measured: the gated agent's per-trial tokens stay at 0.77–0.94M while the ungated pool's climb to 1.30M for worse accuracy. A smaller curated bank is a token intervention as well as a quality one — the same direction gate-a-skill-update-on-a-replay-of-what-it-might-break found in its cost table, from a different starting point (model-routing-token-economics).

Where the ordering breaks is informative rather than exceptional. On InterCode NL2Bash — single-step natural-language-to-bash mapping — ungated accumulation stays net positive (+8.0 over the static seed), because each trial invokes few skills and contamination chains stay shallow. Contamination bites on long, multi-step work, which is exactly where the Terminal-Bench Hard tier collapses (53% → 35% ungated, against 59% gated). If your agent's tasks are short and independent, an unscreened bank may cost you nothing yet.

Bounds worth holding onto. The authors decline the per-round significance claim themselves: 95% Wilson bands are ~30 points wide at n=50 and overlap at intermediate rounds, so the argument rests on trajectory shape — single-peaked against monotone non-decreasing across five rounds — not on any one contrast. One benchmark family, one primary backbone driving distillation and gating, 50 evolution tasks, 25 test tasks behind the transfer band. And there is no compute-matched control: nothing here compares the gating budget against spending the same compute on test-time sampling, which is the objection that holds this map contested (harness-evolution-underperforms-matched-budget-test-time-scaling, charge-every-search-method-the-same-budget). Scale comparisons against ACE and AHE are disclaimed by the authors as not head-to-head.

Rebutted by: a-batch-level-admission-gate-is-not-a-complete-filter — on the gate's sufficiency, not on the curve. A second group's admission gate of the same shape admitted two estimated-negative units by their own measurement, with the reason stated plainly: a gate whose acceptance test is drawn from the same batch that produced the candidate "can miss content harmless locally but harmful under broader validation." That is this note's own combinatorial level — 21 individually-clean skills costing 8 points in company — reappearing one layer up, against a gate rather than against per-skill review. Gating still converts the inverted U into monotone improvement here; it does not make a removal instrument unnecessary.
