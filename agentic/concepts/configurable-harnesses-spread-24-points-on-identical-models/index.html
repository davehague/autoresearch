A 23.8-point harness spread on the same models, and a named taxonomy of where the reasoning-to-evidence link snaps

Harness-Bench is a purpose-built diagnostic: 106 tasks built from realistic agent-use patterns, reviewed for solvability and oracle-checkability so scoring doesn't lean on rubric judgment alone, run across multiple configurable harnesses on the same underlying models. The aggregate result restates this map's core thesis with a fresh, large-sample instance — 23.8 points separate the best (NanoBot, 76.2%) and worst (OpenClaw, 52.4%) configurable harnesses, and a model-bound agent (Codex, 80.4%) outperforms nearly every configurable harness outright, which is a pointed data point against "just wrap any strong model in a configurable harness and you'll do at least as well."

The paper's second contribution is naming where the harness term comes from: execution-alignment failures, cases where the agent's stated reasoning disconnects from tool feedback, workspace state, evidence, or the output contract it was supposed to satisfy. Over a third of all failures (36.4%) are contract/format violations — the agent produces a plausible-sounding answer that doesn't match what the harness's output contract actually requires — with tool/recovery issues (24.6%) a distant second. This gives harness-as-score-confound a fifth vocabulary for the mechanism, alongside harness-induced-variance-exceeds-model-induced-variance-7x's controller-theoretic framing and this map's own accumulated apparatus-term findings — and a genuinely new datum: stronger model backends show lower cross-harness variance, meaning the harness choice matters more, not less, for weaker models, which cuts against any assumption that a strong-enough model can absorb a mediocre harness.

Bounds

Controlled, sandboxed, offline workflows — the paper is explicit its results are "diagnostic measurements under a fixed benchmark protocol, not guarantees of real-world deployment performance," not live services. It evaluates complete harness configurations as a unit rather than isolating which specific mechanism inside a harness (retrieval, memory, permission structure, tool-calling interface) drives the gap — a question agent-harness-architecture's design-lever findings are better positioned to answer. Process-quality scoring relies partly on rubric-based or LLM-assisted assessment, inheriting judge-side bias risk.
