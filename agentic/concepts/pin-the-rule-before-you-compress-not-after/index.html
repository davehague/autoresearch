Label it while you still have the whole sentence

The remedy to compaction-drops-the-safety-rule-at-the-rate-it-drops-the-log is not a better compaction prompt. It is a type decision made before the text is compressed, with each type routed through its own retention policy: constraints and procedures pinned unchanged, beliefs and preferences compressed, episodic material collapsed. Constraint recall goes to 1.00 / 0.95 / 0.80 at 50 / 25 / 10% and stabilizes at 0.96 from round two, at zero model tokens and effectively zero latency, against 0.53 → 0.10 for the production path.

Three findings here are portable to any context pipeline, independent of the specific framework.

1. The label must be assigned before compression, and "clean up afterwards" recovers about half. Add a regex stage that detects constraints and re-inserts them after compression, and recall goes from 0.55/0.18/0.02 to 0.83/0.65/0.60. That is real, and it stops there — because declarative-form rules carry no imperative marker for a regex to find, and declarative is the majority form of real safety text. The remaining gap closes only when the classification happens while the full sentence still exists. Any pipeline that reconstitutes rules downstream of a summarizer inherits this ceiling.

2. The verifier is not a nicety, and its absence produces a specific lie. On the 1.4% of configurations too dense to compress safely, the system with a post-compaction verifier escalated to Unsafe on 28% of them. The same configurations without the verifier reported apparent 1.00 recall while silently dropping a mean 57% of the constraints that should have been kept. Not "degraded" — reported success. This is a-compactor-that-audits-itself-reports-recall-it-did-not-achieve's case in this map's own machinery, and it is why removing any single component of the three (labels, verifier, escalation) drops a deployed agent below 0.50: they fail in different ways and only compose.

3. Pinning the relevant rules beat handing over the whole policy. On τ-bench retail, the pinned configuration reaches 37.7% paired pass against 28.6% for the full uncompacted policy and 29.2% for truncation (p = 0.003 / 0.005 over 329 completed model-task pairs). Compression that keeps the right things outperformed no compression at all. The gain concentrates on the smallest model (gpt-5.4-nano, 22.5% → 36.0%), which is the mechanism: a weaker model benefits most from not having to locate the governing rule inside a wall of policy. Note the comparison is equal-policy but not equal-tokens, and on the harder airline domain the pinned configuration ties the full policy rather than beating it — the effect is bounded by domain difficulty, and the paper says so.

The honest ceiling, stated by the authors first. Per-type retention preserves no more constraints than the classifier detects. On the medical benchmark the residual 4.5-point gap to the full-policy ceiling is exactly the nine declarative items the classifier missed at its 0.93 operating recall. So this converts a type-blind compression problem into a classification problem — better, because classification recall is measurable and improvable and compaction loss is neither, but not free.

The practice, in three lines. Write rules in a form a classifier and a human both recognize as rules — imperative beats declarative, and roughly half of real safety text is written the wrong way. Decide the type at indexing time, not after compaction. And put a deterministic check after the compaction step that verifies each pinned item is still present verbatim, because the alternative is a run that reports full fidelity and has lost most of its constraints.

Bounds. Retail is imperative-heavy and easy for the classifier; airline is harder and the method ties rather than wins there. The τ-bench comparisons are not equal-token. Cheap classifier variants span 0.27–0.77 constraint recall, so the cost/recall frontier is a real deployment decision rather than a solved one. Everything downstream is measured on three benchmark domains, two of them customer service.
