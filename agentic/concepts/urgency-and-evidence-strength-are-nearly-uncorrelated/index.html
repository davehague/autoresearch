The catalogue ranked its own practices twice and the two rankings do not agree

An engineering monograph on agent reliability ranked 52 of its practices by consequence for a real decision, and separately recorded whether each carried at least one strong evidence item. The correlation between the two is −0.004. The author reports this in order to make an editorial point — the chapter selection is authorial judgment, not an evidence-derived consensus — but the number is the more interesting artifact, and it is a problem for every graded vault including this one.

What it means if it holds. An evidence hierarchy is a filter on claims, and consequence is a property of decisions. They come apart because the practices that matter most are frequently structural: separate the recovery identity from the production identity, validate write authority at the boundary rather than at the scheduler, refuse to treat a self-report as completion. Each is testable against its own mechanism and none has a study estimating how often the omission causes loss — so each has an asymmetric cost of waiting for trial evidence, and each would be filtered out or demoted by a strength-ranked reading. Meanwhile the well-evidenced material clusters where experiments are cheap to run, which is not where the money is.

The honest form of the finding is a warning about how to read a graded corpus, not a licence to ignore grading. This vault's evidence model already insists that status moves both ways and that one top-tier null outweighs several weak positives; what it has never claimed is that its strongest-evidenced claims are its most consequential ones. On this evidence they are unrelated, and a primer that ordered its imperatives by tier would be ordering them at random with respect to what happens if you skip them.

The disclosure practice around it is worth copying wholesale, because it is what makes the number believable. Three publisher indexes were unreachable — one returned HTTP 403 to automated clients, one had a dead credential, one had no key — and all three are reported as not performed, "a disclosed source limitation, not an inference of zero relevant results," with the query plans preserved for a later edition. A credential-free substitute census then surfaced 50 publications absent from every other lane, which were queued rather than quietly absorbed past the cutoff. A targeted self-audit of the ten practices whose only support was a synthesis graded strong reduced six of them, because the source demonstrated a hazard or an adjacent result rather than the stated remedy. And the grading instrument is released uncalibrated on purpose: no external graders, no inter-rater agreement value reported, a blinded 20-practice packet and a κ script shipped so someone else can run the pass — with the author naming the asymmetry, since his own book asks operators to calibrate graders against independent labels before trusting them.

That is the standard this vault should be held to as well: report-judge-agreement-per-condition-not-per-panel and estimate-reference-reliability-before-it-drives-repair apply to the grader in the loop, and "the search we could not run" is a reportable line, not an absence.

Bounds and grade. T3, single author, single adjudicator. The −0.004 is computed over 52 practices from one catalogue with one person's urgency ranking and one person's evidence grades — the correlation could be an artifact of either ranking, and the author says the phrase "nearly uncorrelated" refers to this calculation and not to all 206 entries or to any latent measure of importance. Carried as an open question rather than a finding: nobody has tested whether the same divergence appears in an independently ranked corpus.
