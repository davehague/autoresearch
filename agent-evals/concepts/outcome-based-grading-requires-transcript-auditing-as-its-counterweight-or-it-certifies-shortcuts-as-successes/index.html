Outcome-based grading requires transcript auditing as its counterweight, or it certifies shortcuts as successes

Grounded in patterns-for-building-cybersecurity-evals; belongs to evals-that-localize-the-fix.

Most of the surveyed benchmarks grade what happened rather than how, because exploitation is open-ended and enumerating valid methods is impossible. That choice is what makes these evals cheap to build and tolerant of creative solutions. It also means an agent that reached the goal by an unintended route scores identically to one that did the intended work.

The counterweight the benchmarks pair with it is automated transcript auditing — reviewing the run to confirm the agent exploited the intended vulnerability rather than finding an unrelated shortcut. The two are a matched set. Adopting outcome grading without the audit is adopting half a pattern, and the half you kept is the half that inflates your numbers.

Why this extends. It adds a second failure mode to the vault's existing worry. automated-eval-platforms-recall-74-87-of-expert-labeled-failures-but-systematically-miss-the-ones-requiring-domain-taste-so-adopting-one-buys-breadth-rather-than-judgment establishes that automated analysis misses a category of real failure. This is the mirror image — a grading design that manufactures a category of fake success. Both push the same way: the score is not the finding, and something has to read the trace.

Practical read. The audit is a per-run check on how, which means it produces exactly the localization that outcome grading destroys. It belongs in this thread rather than filed as a security-specific hygiene step.

Why suggestive. Recurs across independently built benchmarks, and the reward-hacking failure it guards against is well attested in the wild. What is missing is any figure for how often the audit actually catches something — no benchmark in the survey reports an audit rejection rate, so the size of the problem it solves is unknown. A single reported rate would move this either direction.
