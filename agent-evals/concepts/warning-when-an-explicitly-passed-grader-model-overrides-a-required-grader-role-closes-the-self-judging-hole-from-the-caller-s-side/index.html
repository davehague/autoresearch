Warning when an explicitly passed grader model overrides a required grader role closes the self-judging hole from the caller's side

Grounded in inspect-ai-changelog-unreleased-after-0-3-259; belongs to design-for-verifiability.

From the unreleased window: "Model-graded scorers warn when explicit model bypasses required model role."

This is the second half of a mechanism whose first half shipped three days earlier. a-runner-can-now-require-the-grader-role-so-the-judge-cannot-silently-resolve-to-the-model-under-test let a scorer declare that it needs a distinct grader role, closing the case where the judge quietly defaulted to whatever model was under test. That left the obvious hole: a caller who passes a grader model explicitly overrides the requirement, silently, and the eval runs with the declaration satisfied on paper and violated in fact.

The shape is worth naming because it recurs. A constraint declared at one layer and overridable at another is not a constraint — it is a default with good intentions. The vault has the same structure elsewhere: a stated token budget that "barely reaches agent behaviour," a sandbox policy that an example config bypasses, an admissibility condition that ships as a flag. In every case the enforcement is only as strong as the least careful call site, and the fix pattern is the same one used here: make the override visible rather than forbidding it, because forbidding it breaks legitimate uses.

A warning rather than an error is the right call and worth noting as a design precedent. There are real reasons to pass a grader explicitly — pinning a judge version, running a judge-comparison ablation, reproducing an old result. Hard-failing those would push people to drop the role requirement entirely, which is worse. A warning keeps the declaration meaningful while leaving the escape hatch instrumented.

The unrun experiment this vault keeps asking for is still unrun. design-for-verifiability has been carrying the open question of whether the grader-role flag changes any published score. Two enforcement mechanisms have now shipped in under a week and nobody has reported running an eval with and without them to see what silent self-judging was worth. It is one flag and two runs.

Bounds. One changelog line on an unreleased branch. A warning is not enforcement, and nothing states where the warning surfaces, whether it appears in the eval log, or whether it is visible to someone reading results months later — which is the only place it would matter for an old number. One runner. No measurement of how often explicit overrides actually occur.
