A reported rate is a composite of exposure, execution, observation and adjudication, and two of the four have nothing to do with the agent

Grounded in redagentbench-executable-red-teaming-faithful-measurement; belongs to what-the-headline-number-averages-away.

The framing claim of a 1,661-case agent-safety benchmark: a single attack success rate "collapses exposure, execution, observation, and adjudication into one ASR." The four stages, as the paper defines them — exposure, whether the intervention reached the agent at all; execution, what the agent actually did in the environment; observation, which trajectory or state evidence was available to look at; adjudication, the mapping from that evidence to a label.

Why this extends rather than repeats. Every prior case in this theme is aggregation over results: mean versus minimum across items, pass@k versus pass^k across trials, completion versus safety, route accuracy versus answer accuracy. All of them combine things the agent produced. This decomposes the measurement pipeline instead, and the consequence is sharper than any of them — only one of the four stages is a property of the agent. Exposure is a property of the attack construction; observation is a choice about which artifact you look at; adjudication is a choice about how you label what you saw. A rate that moves can move for three reasons that have nothing to do with the system under test, and the reported number is silent about which.

It generalizes past safety, which is why it is worth the shelf space. Nothing in the decomposition is specific to attacks. A task success rate has the same four stages: did the task reach the agent in a runnable state, what did the agent do, what did the grader get to see, and how did the grader label it. Read that way, the four-stage split is a superset of most of this vault's standing complaints — the seven unreported environment parameters sit in exposure, the transcript-versus-state question sits in observation, judge severity and rubric choice sit in adjudication, and the model-or-harness argument is entirely inside execution. It is the first framing here that gives those a shared address.

The same paper measures two of the stages and finds them expensive. Observation is worth 7.73–11.72 points on its own (showing-the-judge-the-final-state-instead-of-the-transcript-moves-the-reported-rate-about-ten-points-and-flips-the-label-on-up-to-a-fifth-of-cases). Exposure — specifically whether the agent was told it was being evaluated — is worth another 5–7 (telling-the-agent-it-is-being-evaluated-moves-the-measured-rate-five-to-seven-points-and-not-always-downward). Two stages, roughly 15 points between them, neither reported by anyone.

Single-study, one week old, and a framework rather than a finding. The decomposition is an analytical claim, not a measured one; its value is that it names things that were previously unnamed and that the same paper then puts numbers against two of them. The honest test is whether it survives being applied to a benchmark whose authors did not invent it.
