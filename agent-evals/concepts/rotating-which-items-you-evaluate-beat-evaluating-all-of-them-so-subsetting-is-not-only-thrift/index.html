Rotating which items you evaluate beat evaluating all of them, so subsetting is not only thrift

Grounded in task-coevolve-adaptive-validation-task-selection; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run.

On online text classification with a 20% evaluation budget, adaptive resampling "outperforms Meta-Harness by about 1% even when using only 20% of the validation set." The authors' reading: "One possible reason is that Meta-Harness overfits to the validation set during search, leading to lower performance on the test set. This result suggests that changing the evaluation samples across iterations can reduce the risk of overfitting."

Every subsetting lever in this vault is defended as accuracy-preserving — you give up a little quality to pay a lot less. Item-response subsetting cuts safety evaluation cost 97–99% with about ten items and approximately preserves the ranking. Coreset selection has a stated accuracy cost. The framing is always a trade. Here the cheaper protocol won.

The mechanism is ordinary once stated, and its absence from eval practice is the interesting part. A fixed validation set used to select among 60 candidate harnesses is a selection surface, and 60 selections against the same items is enough to fit it. Rotating which items are looked at each iteration breaks that. This is the machine-learning practice that every practitioner applies to model training and almost no eval harness applies to itself — and it lands on the vault's existing observation that harness tuning is a per-model artifact with a recurring bill: if the tuned harness is partly fitted to the validation items, part of what you paid for does not transfer.

The consequence for how this theme argues. Cost-cutting has been framed here as a concession — cheaper measurement, slightly worse measurement. On this evidence the honest framing for iterated selection is different: evaluating everything every round is not the gold standard it is treated as, because it is the configuration most exposed to overfitting the validation set. That reframes the question a team should ask from "how much quality will subsetting cost me" to "how many times am I selecting against these items."

Bounds. One setting (online text classification over three datasets), three seeds, an effect of about one point — nowhere near enough to call the ordering settled, and the paper hedges it as "one possible reason." On Terminal-Bench the same method came in ~1% below full search rather than above, though that protocol makes the validation set the reporting set and so structurally favours full search. Read as a direction with a plausible mechanism, not as a result: the claim that rotation reduces validation overfitting is asserted from an outcome difference, not demonstrated by a diagnostic.
