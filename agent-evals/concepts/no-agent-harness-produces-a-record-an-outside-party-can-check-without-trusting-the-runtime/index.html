No agent harness produces a record an outside party can check without trusting the runtime that produced it, so every eval result in this vault rests on self-report at the infrastructure layer

Grounded in harness-architectural-convergence-three-coding-agents; belongs to design-for-verifiability.

Nine dimensions were compared across three harnesses. On eight, the three differ or converge. On one they are not divergent but absent: external verifiability, defined as a tamper-evident record an outside party can check without trusting the runtime that produced it.

The paper draws a six-rung ladder and every harness stops below the line:

  • deepagents — checkpoints exist for resumption, not evidence; the resume path rewrites message history to patch dangling calls.
  • pi — append-only on the newer path, but the sessions are files the process owns: mutable, and unverifiable by a third party.
  • dsh — climbs highest and is the instructive exception: durable approval records, a visible-implies-logged invariant, recovery that closes rather than truncates an interrupted turn. It still stops below the line, aiming at operational reconstructability (can the runtime rebuild what happened) rather than external verifiability. Its own documentation is candid at the boundary — ctx.tools.restrict() is "a visibility composition, not a permission boundary."

The two empty rungs are empty in different ways. Nobody produces a tamper-evident record an outsider can check (rung 5), and nobody treats "what was this data as of," "may this result leave the building," or "prove the cost ceiling held" as first-class provable questions (rung 6).

The consequence for this vault is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: every eval result here is trust-based at the layer below the eval. A benchmark score, a harness delta, a judge's kappa, a paired resolved-item comparison — all are computed from a log the runtime wrote about itself, and the runtime can rewrite it. This vault has spent weeks establishing that the harness can silently mis-score the model (a-failing-sandbox-was-handing-its-error-to-the-agent-as-command-output-so-infrastructure-failures-entered-the-log-as-capability-failures, a-multibyte-read-boundary-silently-ended-tool-output-for-the-rest-of-a-session, the-harness-performed-the-commit-the-experiment-was-attributing-to-the-model). Each of those was found because someone noticed something odd and went looking. There is no mechanism, in any of the three harnesses, that would let a reader who was not present detect that the record had been edited.

The author's explanation is the part that predicts. The absence is read as a selection-pressure asymmetry rather than an oversight: the three converged on everything a developer at a terminal needs, and verifiability is precisely what that audience does not need. Auditability is prescribed top-down in the research literature — the Autogenesis protocol makes versioned lineage and auditable rollback its centrepiece — while being absent bottom-up in every harness read. So the gap is not a failure of imagination but of demand, which means it closes when someone with provenance requirements starts buying, and not before.

What a team could do about it today is small and nobody is doing it. Content-hash the reused prefix (the replay audit in symtrace-repair-or-resample-replay-controlled-debugging hashes all 6,159 reused nodes and reports 6,159/6,159, which is the first thing in this vault resembling an integrity check on an eval record), and refuse to grade a run whose log the recovery path rewrote.

Suggestive rather than strong: it is an absence claim over N = 3, made by an author who discloses in the threats section that this dimension aligns with their own research interest. An absence over three cases is a hypothesis about the field, not a measurement of it. But the three were chosen for maximal spread and the strongest of them documents its own boundary, which is the best kind of evidence an absence claim can have.
