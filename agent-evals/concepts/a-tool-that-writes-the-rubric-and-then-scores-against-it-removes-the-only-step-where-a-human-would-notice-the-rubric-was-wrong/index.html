A tool that writes the rubric and then scores against it removes the only step where a human would notice the rubric was wrong

Grounded in hamel-selecting-the-right-ai-evals-tool; belongs to build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling.

Named as the specific thing to refuse when choosing an eval platform: "Be deeply skeptical of features that promise full automation without human validation, as these can create a powerful and dangerous illusion of confidence." The concrete instance is a feature that generates evaluation criteria and immediately scores against them, which "often hides flaws behind a high score." One vendor's AI scorer is cited by name as the example.

Why this confirms. automated-eval-platforms-recall-74-87-of-expert-labeled-failures-but-systematically-miss-the-ones-requiring-domain-taste-so-adopting-one-buys-breadth-rather-than-judgment measured the outcome — automated platforms find most mechanical failures and systematically miss the ones needing domain taste. This supplies the mechanism, as a product feature rather than a recall statistic. A system that authors the criterion and then applies it has closed the loop on itself: the criterion is never seen by anyone who knows the domain, and the score it produces is high precisely because the criterion was drawn from the same distribution as the output. The failure is not that the model grades badly; it is that the step where a human would have said "that is not what we care about" was deleted as a convenience.

The score direction is the trap. A broken grader that scores low invites investigation. A rubric drawn from the outputs it will grade scores high, and nobody investigates a high score. That is the same asymmetry as fixing-grading-bugs-moved-one-model-from-42-to-95-percent-so-the-measuring-instrument-can-own-more-of-a-score-than-anything-it-measures with the sign reversed, and the reversed sign is worse, because the low-score version at least gets looked at eventually.

It sharpens rather than contradicts the automated-error-analysis lead. parallel-error-analysis-agents-over-stored-traces-is-the-first-report-of-the-transcript-audit-obligation-being-paid-by-machine recorded machine-run error analysis as the first instance of the transcript-audit bill being paid at all. Nothing here argues against that. The distinction being drawn is between automating the labour — read a thousand traces, group them — and automating the judgment about what counts as a failure. The first is the bill this vault has said nobody pays. The second is the step that makes the whole exercise circular.

The checkable version, for anyone evaluating a platform. Ask where the criteria came from and who read them before the first score was produced. If the answer is "the tool generated them from your traces," the number is a description of your traces.
