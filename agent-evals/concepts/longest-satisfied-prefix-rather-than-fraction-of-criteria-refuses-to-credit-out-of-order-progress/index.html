Longest-satisfied-prefix rather than fraction-of-criteria refuses to credit out-of-order progress

Grounded in agentrewind-mettlebench-recoverable-execution; belongs to evaluating-over-a-horizon.

82 long-horizon engineering tasks, each with a hidden ordered checklist of interdependent acceptance criteria (640 total, mean 7.80 per task). Partial credit is not the fraction passed. Let ℓ be "the length of the longest prefix (g₁, …, g_ℓ) such that gᵢ(s_T) = 1 for all i ≤ ℓ"; checklist prefix progress is ρ(s_T) = ℓ / n. An agent that satisfies criteria 1, 3 and 5 of five scores 0.2, not 0.6.

This vault has collected partial credit as an unambiguous good and this is the first source to give it an opinion. Subtask partial credit recovers signal binary grading throws away; process reward diverges from strict pass in every configuration; per-check scoring beats a task-level pass. All of those count what was achieved. Prefix progress asks a different question — how far did the run get before it stopped being buildable-on — and it is the right question here because the corpus was screened to make it so: later deliverables must quote run-time values earlier stages produced, "digests, counts, or identifiers, in at least two places," so "an agent that skips or fakes an earlier stage therefore cannot fill in a later one." Out-of-order credit would be credit for work that cannot support what follows.

The paper then shows the metric's eligibility condition by failing it. Transferring to Terminal-Bench 2.0, where acceptance criteria "are not semantically ordered," it reverts to plain fraction-of-criteria. So prefix progress is not a better partial-credit rule in general — it is the rule you become entitled to once your checklist is genuinely ordered, and knowing which kind you have is the work. That parallels the eligibility framing this vault reached for cheap subsetting rules: the question is not which method is best but which one your data qualifies you for.

It measures something the success rate provably does not. GPT-5.4 and GLM-5.1 reach similar task success, but their unsuccessful runs average 50.8% and 38.1% prefix progress. Two models that look equivalent on the headline are 12.7 points apart on how far they get before collapsing — which is the distinction a team choosing a model for long-running work actually needs, and which binary success destroys.

The feedback channel is the other half of the design and it is deliberately narrow. On each submission the backend "returns natural-language feedback identifying only the first unsatisfied checklist item," with the full checklist hidden. That keeps the ordered metric honest — an agent cannot optimize against the tail — and it makes the eval a sequential process rather than a single scored artifact. Because all criteria share one environment state, "subsequent actions may invalidate previously satisfied criteria," so a prefix can shorten: this theme's "previously-passed checks must stay live" requirement, enforced by construction rather than by policy.

Bounds. Single study, 82 tasks rewritten from five existing benchmarks. The criteria "were written by an LLM agent, under a protocol we wrote," so the ordering that prefix progress depends on is machine-authored with human review, and a mis-ordered checklist would silently under-credit. Prefix progress is harsh in a specific way the paper does not quantify: an agent failing criterion 2 and completing 3 through 8 scores 0.125, and no analysis reports how often that happens. Whether ρ correlates better than fraction-of-criteria with anything a team cares about is not measured — it is argued from the task design. The 50.8%/38.1% comparison is two models on one benchmark.
