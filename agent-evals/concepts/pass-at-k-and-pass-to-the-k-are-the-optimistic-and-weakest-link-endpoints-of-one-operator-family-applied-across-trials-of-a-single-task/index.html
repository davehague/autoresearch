pass@k and pass^k are the optimistic and weakest-link endpoints of one operator family applied across trials of a single task

Grounded in anthropic-demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents; belongs to what-the-headline-number-averages-away.

Two metrics, reported together rather than one instead of the other. pass@k is the probability of succeeding in at least one of k attempts. pass^k is the probability all k succeed. The worked example: at a 75% per-trial success rate, three trials all pass with probability 0.75³ ≈ 42%. The guidance is that pass^k is the number that matters for a customer-facing agent, where users expect the thing to work every time.

Why this extends. mean-and-weakest-link-aggregation-produce-completely-disjoint-top-fives-on-a-54-model-public-leaderboard established that mean and minimum are endpoints of one operator family on a pessimism parameter, and that the choice reorders a leaderboard. That was across items. This is the same pair across trials of one item, which is an axis this theme had not touched at all — an axis every eval already has, since agentmembench-memory-strategy-comparison is the only source here that even removes sampling variance by decoding greedily.

The arithmetic on the trial axis is harsher than on the item axis, and that is the reason this is worth its own atom. Averaging over items degrades gracefully: a mean and a minimum over ten items differ by however unequal the items are. Compounding over trials is exponential and it does not care how good you are — 75% becomes 42% at k=3, 90% becomes 73%, 99% becomes 97%. The gap between the optimistic and the pessimistic reading grows with every repeat the user makes, and no measurement is needed to compute it.

The decision it changes, and it is available today. Any team quoting a per-trial success rate to a stakeholder who will run the agent repeatedly is quoting the wrong number, and the conversion is one exponentiation on data already collected. This is the cheapest thing in this theme by a distance: no new runs, no new grader, no labelling. Report both, name k, and let the pessimism be a stated choice rather than an artifact of which formula the framework's default happened to be.

The connection worth carrying. what-an-eval-costs-to-run treats k as pure expense — every repeat multiplies the bill, which is why suites default to one trial. This says k is also what makes reliability visible at all: at k=1 there is no distinction between a 75% agent and a 99% agent that a single run can show you. So the trial count is a joint cost-and-validity dial, not a thrift dial, and it belongs in both themes.

Why suggestive. No study, no data. It is a vendor guide's definitional framing plus arithmetic, and the arithmetic is trivially correct given independence between trials — which is the assumption doing all the work and which nobody has tested for agents. Trials of an agent on the same task are plausibly correlated, through caching, through a deterministic failure in the environment, through a prompt the model reliably misreads; if they are, pass^k is not p^k and the compounding is milder than stated. Nobody in this vault reports trial-to-trial correlation.

What would move this. Any team publishing pass@k and pass^k side by side on the same suite with the ranking under each, which is the trial-axis version of the HELM demonstration. And separately, an empirical pass^k against the independence prediction — the gap between them is the correlation nobody has measured.
