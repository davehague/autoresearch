Predicting an unrun item's outcome works inside a scenario and collapses to chance across scenarios

Grounded in contextual-mirt-evaluation-on-unseen-questions; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run.

Fit a model that gives each LLM a multidimensional latent capability vector and each question a learned embedding-derived representation, then predict outcomes on questions nobody ran. Within a scenario (80/20 split, five folds, 11 HELM scenarios of 436–29,407 questions), 90th-percentile question-wise AUC on held-out items runs 0.865 to 1.000 — imdb 1.000, air_bench 0.985, boolq 0.978, wikifact 0.953, math 0.950, dyck_language 0.865.

Across scenarios it stops working, and sometimes inverts. Fitting on air_bench_2024 gives off-diagonal AUCs of 0.519–0.621. imdb → gsm gives 0.409, below chance. entity_data_imputation → gsm gives 0.420. Some pairs do transfer — babi_qa → entity_data_imputation 0.943, gsm → math 0.881 — with no stated rule for which. The authors' own conclusion: within-scenario success "does not necessarily translate into reliable prediction under cross-scenario transfer," which they name as "a central open challenge."

The boundary lands on the wrong side of the use case, and that is the whole finding. This theme holds several ways to cut eval cost by dropping items: adaptive-item-selection-reproduces-full-benchmark-rankings-at-80-to-99-percent-lower-cost-so-eval-set-size-is-a-dial, a-fixed-informative-item-subset-extracted-once-captures-most-of-the-savings-without-adaptive-machinery-in-the-loop, a-second-independent-team-cuts-safety-evaluation-cost-97-to-99-percent-with-about-ten-items-so-the-item-selection-result-is-no-longer-single-source. This is the complementary move — never run the item at all — and it inherits the same limit in sharper form. You can save on the eval you already have; you cannot save on the eval you do not have yet. A team adding a new capability area, a new customer domain, or a new task type is asking the cross-scenario question, and the answer there is a coin flip.

It also has the cold-start problem this theme already recorded. item-response-theory-subsetting-has-a-cold-start-cost-in-historical-response-logs-that-a-single-team-cannot-pay and a-415470-outcome-response-matrix-across-17-benchmarks-exists-so-the-irt-cold-start-is-an-availability-problem-rather-than-a-cost-one are both about needing a dense model×item response matrix before any of this can be fitted. Nothing here changes that: the saving is real only after the labelling that makes it possible has been paid, and that sunk cost is not counted in any of the numbers above.

A quoting caution. Both metrics are AUCs — question-wise ranks models on a fixed item, LLM-wise ranks items for a fixed model — and both are summarised at the 90th percentile of their distribution rather than the median or mean, which flatters them. Neither says anything about calibration, so "predict our score on the new set" is not a claim this method makes.

Bounds. HELM-derived question answering only — no agents, no tools, no multi-turn, no trajectories. Items with incomplete text were filtered out, so the corpus is the well-formed subset. One method, one paper, no external replication.
