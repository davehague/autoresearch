Reconstructing 97 tasks took 57 domain experts, 30 user interviews and a funding-and-paid-usage filter on the source products

Grounded in startupbench-market-validated-rubric-graded-workflows; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run and build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling.

Four stages. Survey 20+ startup agents, keeping only companies "with more than USD 1M in funding" and "evidence of real adoption, either through paid usage or substantial user traction." Interview "over 30 deep users," collecting at least one demo case per candidate. Recruit "over 50 domain experts to reconstruct benchmark tasks" — 57, median 5 years' experience. Quality-control by expert cross-validation, where "every task is independently reviewed by at least one additional domain expert," plus difficulty calibration through pilot executions. Output: 97 tasks, each carrying an average of 25.3 rubrics.

This is the most expensive corpus construction recorded in this vault, and the first with a market filter on the input. The previous high-water marks were three annotators over 200 sampled tasks against six criteria, and a 79.4% yield on model-drafted, human-reviewed questions. Here it is roughly 0.6 domain experts per task, plus interviews, plus a second expert reviewing every item, plus pilot runs — for a corpus one twentieth the size of the cheapest generated ones the vault holds.

Set against the generator side, the two curves are now both visible. A verified generator — build the executable tool layer first, generate against it — produced 10,400 tasks in one case because execution supplies the ground truth. Human reconstruction produced 97 because a person has to know what the right answer looks like. The entire difference between those two numbers is whether the correctness condition can be computed, and this corpus sits at the far end where it cannot: the deliverables are professional documents graded on domain compliance.

What the money bought that a generator cannot. The funding-and-paid-usage filter is the interesting expense, because it is spent before any task exists. It buys the claim that the tasks are ones somebody pays to have done — a difficulty and realism dial that no generator can synthesize, since a generator can only produce tasks in the shape of tasks it was told about. Whether that filter is worth its cost is unmeasurable from this paper alone, and it is the sort of thing a team building an internal eval gets for free, since their tasks come from their own product by construction.

The transferable read for a team. If your correctness condition is computable, build the executable layer and generate; the corpus is nearly free after the fixed cost. If it is not, expect to pay roughly half a domain expert per task plus a reviewer, and expect a corpus in the tens-to-low-hundreds rather than the thousands. Nobody in this vault has yet done the arithmetic on where the crossover sits.

Bounds. No hours, no rates, no elapsed time — expert counts are stated and expert cost is not, so this is a headcount figure being read as a cost figure. Nothing states how the 57 experts were divided across the 97 tasks or the 6 domains, so 0.6 experts per task is an average over an unreported distribution. The same expert pool wrote the tasks, the rubrics and the reference labels used to validate the judge, which makes their agreement figures partly self-referential.
