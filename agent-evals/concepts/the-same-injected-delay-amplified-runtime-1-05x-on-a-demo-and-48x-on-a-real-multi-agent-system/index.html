The same injected delay amplified end-to-end runtime 1.05x on a demo and 48x on a real multi-agent system, so latency sensitivity measures the orchestration's serial depth

Grounded in llmmas-otel-tracing-and-fault-injection; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run.

Across the 30-task ProgramDev benchmark, five runs per condition: an injected LLM-call delay amplified end-to-end runtime by 1.053× on a minimal Planner→Coder demo and 48.1× on ChatDev. An injected A2A delay reached 59.2× on ChatDev.

Read as a fact about delays these numbers are alarming and uninformative; read as a measurement of the system they are a cheap structural probe. A fixed per-call delay multiplies end-to-end time roughly in proportion to how many such calls sit on the critical path and how little of the work runs concurrently. So amplification factor is a proxy for serial depth — and the 45-fold difference between the demo and ChatDev under an identical perturbation is a statement about the two orchestrations, not about latency.

Which makes it a wall-clock cost model that needs no labels and no ground truth. Every method in this theme prices an eval in tokens or in human reading time. Wall clock has been treated as an afterthought, and it is the term that decides whether a suite can run per-commit. Injecting a known delay and reading the amplification tells you, in one experiment, how much a provider slowdown or a rate-limit episode will cost your suite in elapsed time — and by implication how much parallelism is available to buy back. A team whose amplification factor is near 1 has a concurrency problem solved; a team at 48 has a critical path that will dominate every scheduling decision they make.

It also reframes a recurring complaint. Long-horizon and multi-agent evals are expensive in wall clock, and the usual explanation is "the tasks are long". This says a large part of it may be orchestration shape: sequential agent handoffs, each waiting on a full model response, with no overlap. That is a fixable property of the harness rather than a property of the work, and it is measurable before anyone tries to fix it.

Limits worth stating plainly. One real system, one demo, one benchmark, five runs, and the paper reports amplification factors without the injected delay magnitude in the summary — so the numbers are ratios whose baseline the reader has to take on trust. No cost figure in money is given. The generalisable content is the method: inject a fixed delay at a known boundary, read the amplification, treat it as a measure of serial depth. That costs one instrumented run.
