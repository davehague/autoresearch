Agents fail by assuming successful execution implies successful delivery, which is the one failure a self-reported grader cannot see

Grounded in startupbench-market-validated-rubric-graded-workflows; belongs to evals-that-localize-the-fix and design-for-verifiability.

Named as one of three leading behaviour-level failure patterns across 97 professional workflow tasks: "many models fail not because they cannot complete the required workflow, but because they incorrectly assume that successful execution implies successful delivery." Alongside it, partial compliance — models "satisfy the most visible requirements while overlooking other equally critical constraints" — and domain-expertise gaps.

This failure is invisible to exactly the graders that are cheapest to build. An agent that believes execution implies delivery reports success. A grader that asks the agent whether it succeeded agrees. A grader that reads the agent's trajectory for evidence of completion finds it, because the steps did run. Only a grader that checks the artifact against a stated requirement disagrees. So the failure mode and the cheap grader are correlated by construction: the more your scoring depends on the agent's own account, the less of this you can see, and it is a leading failure cause.

It is the mechanism behind a warning this vault has carried without one. an-agents-recorded-plan-recovers-only-part-of-the-true-prerequisite-structure-so-grading-against-the-plan-grades-the-narration established that grading the agent's narration grades the narration. This says the narration is not merely incomplete but systematically optimistic, and names the specific inference that generates the optimism — ran therefore delivered. That is a testable belief rather than a vague unreliability, and it predicts where self-reports will be wrong: tasks with delivery requirements separable from execution steps, which is most professional work and almost no benchmark task.

It also explains the format result in the same paper. On the 56 tasks with explicit output-format requirements, "no model achieves perfect compliance," with rates from 97.6% down to 86.3%. Format is the most checkable delivery requirement there is, and even there the agents fall short while presumably believing they complied. If a requirement that a regex could verify goes unmet 2–14% of the time, the unverifiable ones are worse.

Practice. Score at least one requirement that is a property of the artifact and not of the run. It does not have to be sophisticated — a format assertion is enough to detect the pattern, and it is the same cheap-assertion layer that hundreds-of-per-feature-assertions-running-in-ordinary-ci-is-the-eval-layer-with-no-judge-and-no-label-budget-in-it describes. If your eval has no artifact-level check at all, you cannot distinguish an agent that delivered from one that is confident it did.

Bounds. A qualitative failure-mode classification with no rate attached — nothing says what fraction of failures were self-verification hallucination rather than the other two patterns, so its ranking among causes is asserted. One benchmark, 97 tasks, nine models, and the classification is produced by the benchmark authors reading trajectories rather than by an independent coding scheme with agreement statistics.
