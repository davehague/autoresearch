Two domains averaged 87.6 and 94.9 while passing 4% and 0% of their tasks, so the aggregate ranking hides slices where partial progress converts to nothing at all

Grounded in frontierchallenge-scientific-workflow-completion; belongs to what-the-headline-number-averages-away.

The suite-level gap between mean rubric score and strict completion (an-average-rubric-score-of-87-9-and-a-pass-rate-of-20-6-percent-came-from-the-same-graders) is not evenly distributed. Broken out by domain:

  • Analytical chemistry — best Avg. Score 87.6, highest Pass Rate 4%.
  • Electrochemistry/environment — best Avg. Score 94.9 (Kimi K3), highest Pass Rate 0%.

A 94.9 average that converts to zero completed tasks is the sharpest single number this theme has recorded. By contrast, quantum chemistry — where the same models scored lower on average (GPT-5.6 Terra managed 75.5) — produced Pass Rates of 55% and 60% for the leaders. The domain where the average looks worst is the domain where the work actually gets delivered. The mean and the conjunction are not merely different scales here; over these slices they are ordered oppositely.

The per-domain Pass Rate table is mostly zeros for respectable-looking models. Claude Opus 5 posts 55/31/5/0/0/0 across the six domains against averages of 87.5/87.4/85.5/86.1/66.4/94.1. GLM-5.2 posts 10/6/0/0/0/0 with averages up to 82.6. Six domains, and for most configurations three or four of them are shut out entirely while their averages sit in the eighties.

Why the conversion rate differs is legible from the failure signatures, and the mechanism is contract length rather than difficulty. Judge-assessed artifact shortfalls appeared in 97% of non-passing materials-characterization submissions, 95% of analytical chemistry and 85% of electrochemistry, against 43% in quantum chemistry, where deterministic Grader diagnostics (40%) were relatively more common. The domains with near-zero pass rates are the ones whose contracts demand many mutually-consistent artifacts, each an independent chance to miss. A strict conjunction over a longer contract is harder in a way that has nothing to do with the science being harder. The authors flag exactly this: the contrasts "partly reflect differences in task contracts and evaluator composition and therefore do not establish intrinsic domain-specific causes," and the six domains are descriptive slices of the release rather than probability samples.

Which turns the finding into a warning about your own suite rather than about chemistry. If your eval has heterogeneous contract lengths across slices, the aggregate pass rate is dominated by the short-contract slices, and a model can look competent on the whole suite while delivering nothing on any long-contract slice. The diagnostic is one line: plot mean score against pass rate per slice and look for the slices where the two disagree in rank. Those are the slices where partial progress is not progress.

It also sharpens the standing "which items belong in the eval set" thread. rotating-which-items-you-evaluate-beat-evaluating-all-of-them-so-subsetting-is-not-only-thrift and the outcome-variance sampling result both assume items carry comparable information. A slice with a 0% pass rate carries no information at that threshold no matter how many items it has — every item returns the same answer — while carrying plenty at the mean. Whether such a slice belongs in the suite depends entirely on which metric you are going to publish, and nobody in this vault has been asking that question in that order.

Single study, single runs, 97 of 300 tasks, and the small domains are very small — electrochemistry/environment is 6 tasks, so "0%" is zero out of six and "4%" in analytical chemistry is one out of 23. Those are not rates so much as counts, and the authors are appropriately careful about reading them as domain properties. The pattern survives the small-n objection because it appears across twelve models and three scaffolds in the same direction.
