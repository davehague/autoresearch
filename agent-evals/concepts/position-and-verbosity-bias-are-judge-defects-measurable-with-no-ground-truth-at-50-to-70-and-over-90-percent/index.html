Position and verbosity bias are judge defects measurable with no ground truth, at 50-70% and over 90%

Grounded in eugeneyan-effectiveness-of-llm-evaluators; belongs to design-for-verifiability.

Position bias: GPT-3.5 preferred the first-listed answer 50% of the time, Claude-v1 70%. Verbosity bias: both Claude and GPT-3.5 preferred longer responses more than 90% of the time. Separately, pairwise comparison "leads to more stable results and smaller differences between LLM judgments and human annotations" than direct scoring.

Every judge-validation procedure in this vault costs labels; these cost none. A judge needs 100+ labelled examples to build, a 25% holdout to accept, 931 annotated trajectories to study at population scale. Position and verbosity bias are detectable from the judge's own outputs: run each pairwise comparison twice with the order swapped and count the disagreements; correlate the judge's verdict with response length. At 50–70% and >90% these defects are large enough to surface on a few dozen items, before any annotation budget is spent.

They belong to the only family of cheap signals here that has not failed. Every free metric asked to stand in for outcome has a direct finding against it — step counts, tool calls, tokens, context volume, the agent's recorded plan, tool-call count, first-error position. The signals that survive are the ones asked to do something else: partition failures (nearly-one-in-five-confirmed-violations-happen-after-the-agent-states-the-constraint-and-an-instruction-level-fix-then-removes-74-points) or detect that the instrument is broken (label-count-correlating-with-input-length-at-rho-0-81-is-a-free-detector-for-an-agent-paraphrasing-instead-of-analysing). These are the second and third members of the broken-instrument class, and the family now has a stated shape: a cheap signal is useless as a score and good at catching a measurement process that is responding to the wrong feature of its input. Verbosity bias is literally the judge-side version of the ρ = 0.81 label-count detector — an instrument whose output tracks input length rather than input content.

The order-swap check was already recommended and is stronger with a magnitude attached. holding-out-25-percent-and-reporting-precision-recall-and-kappa-is-the-first-stated-acceptance-procedure-for-a-judge lists running pairwise comparisons twice with the order swapped as one line in a longer procedure. Nobody knew whether it was worth the second run. At a 70% first-position preference on one model it is the highest-yield line in that procedure and the only one that can be run before you have labels.

The stability claim underneath is the design decision. Pairwise comparison being more stable than direct scoring argues against 1–5 rubric scales as the default judging mode, and it agrees with the independent practitioner advice to stay on binary pass/fail. Two arrivals, different arguments — one from rater reproducibility, one from judge stability — landing on the same rule.
