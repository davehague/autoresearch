Accepting a harness change only on a strict selection-score gain, or a tie plus an improved verifier metric, is a stopping rule with an explicit tiebreak

Grounded in starharness-evolving-harnesses-stratified-search; belongs to build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling.

StarHarness states its acceptance rule in one line: a candidate change is accepted when it strictly improves the selection score, or ties it while improving an available verifier metric. Invalid candidates revert before selection is evaluated at all.

The tiebreak is the interesting half. A strict-improvement rule alone stalls: on a benchmark with a coarse score, most candidate edits tie, and a search that only accepts strict gains stops early with a lot of unexplored, harmless-or-better changes rejected. Adding "tie plus a verifier gain" gives the search a secondary gradient made of cheap signals — the deterministic checks already running inside the environment — without letting those signals override the primary score. That is the same structure this vault has recorded on the judge side, where a deterministic verifier priced at a tenth of a judge call becomes the first gate and the expensive signal only breaks what the cheap one cannot (a-deterministic-verifier-priced-at-a-tenth-of-a-judge-call-makes-verifier-first-stopping-the-default-panel-design).

It confirms rather than extends, because the shape already arrived. harness-continual-learning-guarded-evolution required a harness change to clear current improvement, historical retention and validity before commit. StarHarness's rule is the same discipline with the retention leg dropped and a tiebreak added, and it is the second independent arrival of the underlying point: an automated harness search is only as trustworthy as the written-down predicate for accepting a change. Without one, "we evolved the harness" describes a process nobody can audit, and the accepted changes are whatever the proposer talked itself into.

What it costs a team to adopt. Almost nothing, and it is the part of automated harness search that transfers to manual harness work. A team that changes a prompt, a tool description or a retry policy by hand can adopt the identical rule — accept only on a strict gain against a set you did not look at while writing the change, break ties on a deterministic check. That converts an ad-hoc sequence of tweaks into an auditable changelog, which is the thing this vault has repeatedly found missing from harness work at every scale.

Four to twelve changes were accepted per environment under this rule, which is also a useful piece of calibration: the rule is strict enough to reject most candidates.
