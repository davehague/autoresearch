A capped regression suite makes eval-set curation an admit, drop or swap decision per query

Grounded in who-belongs-in-the-eval-set-regression-curation; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run and build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling.

The framing is called a regression-economics paradox: a platform hosting third-party agents receives eval queries from every customer, its regression suite is capped by release cadence, so admission is zero-sum. The pipeline turns that into a per-query verdict — INCLUDE, EXCLUDE, REPLACE, KEEP-BOTH — emitted by a rule engine with no model in the decision path.

This is a different problem from the one this theme has been solving, and it is the more common one. Everything filed here on eval-set economics approaches selection from the corpus side: coreset methods, item-response models picking discriminative items, difficulty prediction without rollouts, the three method families that agree you should keep the items separating the candidates. All of them assume you own the corpus and are choosing a subset of it.

The situation described here is the one most production regression suites are actually in:

  • The cap is hard, set by how often you ship rather than by what you can afford to compute.
  • Inbound pressure is continuous and comes from people who are not you — customers who want their query in the suite.
  • Therefore the operative question is not "which items are informative" but "which item does this one displace."

The zero-sum framing changes what a good curation method has to do. A coreset method answers "what is the smallest set retaining the signal." It has no opinion about a new item arriving after the set was chosen, and re-running it on every arrival means re-scoring the whole suite continuously — which is why this pipeline splits its scoring into a cached intrinsic half and a re-derived relational half. A method that cannot answer the displacement question incrementally cannot run in this setting at all.

The objective is stated as coverage, and that is the part to watch. "The minimal set of queries capturing the maximal spread of capability signatures" optimizes over a capability taxonomy — a set of labels someone wrote down. This vault has already recorded a read-based coverage score reading 1.000 where nothing was available. Spread over labels is maximized by covering the labels, not the behaviour, and the pipeline has no defence against a capability that exists in the product and not in the taxonomy. The taxonomy is the unaudited artifact here, in exactly the way a rubric is elsewhere.

Practice. If your regression suite has a cap — and any suite that runs on every release has one, whether or not anyone has written it down — make the displacement decision explicit. The minimum viable version is a rule that no query is added without naming the query it replaces or the coverage gap it fills. That single rule converts a suite that grows until someone complains about runtime into one with a stated admission policy.

Bounds. No quantitative evaluation of any kind: the pipeline's verdicts are never compared against human curators and no downstream effect on regression detection is measured, so this is a design pattern with an industrial provenance rather than evidence that the pattern works. One platform, one agent-extensibility surface, one taxonomy. The size of the cap is never stated.
