Hundreds of per-feature assertions running in ordinary CI is the eval layer with no judge and no label budget in it

Grounded in hamel-your-ai-product-needs-evals; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run and build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling.

Level 1 of a three-level programme: "fast, cheap assertions" run on every code change, organized by feature and scenario, in ordinary CI (GitHub Actions, GitLab Pipelines). Rechat "maintains hundreds of these unit tests." Examples given are concrete and unglamorous — assert that a listing-finder returns 0, 1, or multiple listings as the scenario requires; assert by regex that no UUID appears in user-facing output. Test cases are generated synthetically: "Write 50 different instructions that a real estate agent can give to his assistant to create contacts on his CRM."

Almost nothing else in this vault has a zero on the label line. Every eval catalogued here carries an annotation cost — 30 examples with a saturation rule, 100 labels plus weekly maintenance, 200 tasks × 3 annotators × 6 criteria, a 79.4% yield on model-drafted human-reviewed items, 57 domain experts reconstructing tasks. Those costs are why what-an-eval-costs-to-run exists. Level 1 has none of them: no judge, no rater agreement, no gold set, nothing to be right or wrong about beyond an assertion someone wrote once. Per-run marginal cost is one model call per case and no human time at all.

The reason it is missing from this vault's evidence base is structural. A benchmark paper cannot ship your product's assertions — they are feature-specific by construction, which is precisely why they are cheap and precisely why nobody publishes them. So the cheapest eval layer available is the one systematically absent from the literature this vault sweeps, and the vault's cost picture is skewed upward as a direct result of where it reads.

What it cannot do, stated so the layer is not oversold. An assertion checks a property somebody already knew to check. It cannot find the failure mode you have not thought of, which is what error analysis and trace review are for, and it says nothing about quality on any axis that needs judgment. The three-level structure is explicit about this — level 1 is not a substitute for level 2, it is what runs on every commit so that level 2's expensive human attention is spent on things assertions cannot reach.

The number the source does not give, and it is the one that would settle the trade. "Hundreds of unit tests" is a count with no coverage claim: no figure for what fraction of production failures those assertions caught, and therefore no way to price level 1 against level 2. A team could get that number in a week by tagging incidents with whether an assertion could have caught them, and it would be the first real datum on where the cheap layer stops paying.

Bounds. One product, one team, 2024, from an author who now sells an evals course. The assertion counts and the 50-instruction generation prompt are illustrations, not calibrated recommendations. No comparison against a version of the programme without level 1.
