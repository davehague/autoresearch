Splitting skill use into trigger, compliance, and boundary exposes two independent bottlenecks that a single success rate merges

Grounded in skill-use-skills-in-agentic-harnesses; belongs to evals-that-localize-the-fix.

Rather than scoring whether a task succeeded, the benchmark scores three separable facets of the run — Trigger (did the agent invoke the relevant skill), Compliance (how faithfully it followed the prescribed procedure), and Boundary (did it avoid forbidden operations). Triggering and procedural compliance were found to fail as independent bottlenecks: being good at one does not predict being good at the other.

Why this confirms. It is the same shape as subtask-partial-credit-recovers-the-signal-binary-grading-throws-away-and-matters-most-when-every-model-scores-near-zero and separating-retrieval-gaps-from-utilization-gaps-turns-a-wrong-answer-into-a-repair-address, arriving from a third direction. The recurring finding is that the useful design choice is the one turning a score into an address. Here the three facets route to three different repairs: trigger failures are a retrieval and description-writing problem, compliance failures are a procedure-format and prompting problem, boundary failures are a permissions problem. Nothing about a combined score tells you which of those to work on.

The independence is what makes the split load-bearing. If trigger and compliance were correlated, the decomposition would be presentational — one underlying competence viewed twice, and the aggregate would lose nothing. That they fail independently means the aggregate is genuinely averaging two unrelated deficits, and any team optimizing against the combined number is working half-blind about which one it is moving.

The construction move underneath. Progressive disclosure — the agent sees only a skill's name and short description and must retrieve the procedure before following it — is what creates the trigger facet in the first place. Handing an agent the full procedure and then measuring compliance tests something no deployment does, since a real skill library is larger than the context. Making retrieval part of the task converts a precondition into a measurable stage. That generalizes past skills to any resource an agent must find before using.

Why single-study. One benchmark, one team. The specific claim that the two bottlenecks are independent is a negative correlation result on eight models, which is a small basis; the design argument for the split does not depend on it holding exactly.

What would move this. A team applying the trigger/comply/bound split to a non-skill resource — tool selection, retrieval-augmented context, documentation lookup — and finding the same independence. Or a report of what fraction of trigger failures are fixed by rewriting the one-line description, which would turn the diagnosis into a priced repair.
