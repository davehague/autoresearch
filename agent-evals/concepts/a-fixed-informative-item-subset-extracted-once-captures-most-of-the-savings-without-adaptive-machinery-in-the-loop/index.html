A fixed informative item subset extracted once and reused across models captures most of the savings without putting adaptive machinery in the run loop

Grounded in efficient-safety-benchmarking-via-item-response-theory; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run.

Alongside adaptive selection, the authors give a procedure for extracting a fixed, informative subset of items once and reusing it across models — up to 99.8% savings on AIR-Bench 2024, against 99.9% for the adaptive version. Nearly all of the benefit, none of the runtime apparatus.

Why this extends. It is the build-versus-adopt question applied to a piece of eval machinery rather than to a framework. Adaptive selection requires a per-model item chooser sitting between the runner and the benchmark: a live dependency, in the loop, on every run. The fixed subset needs that machinery exactly once, offline, and afterwards the CI job is an ordinary eval run over a smaller file. Same result, and the second version leaves nothing behind to maintain.

The decision it changes. It is the version a team can actually adopt this quarter. It also sets a shape worth generalizing: when a technique's value comes from an analysis rather than from a runtime behaviour, run the analysis once and ship its output, not the analyzer. Most of what looks like sophisticated eval infrastructure is a one-time selection problem wearing a service costume.

The catch. A fixed subset is fixed against the models that were in the pool when it was extracted. As models improve past the items that were discriminating, the subset silently stops separating them — the same ceiling problem that motivated the work, arriving later and quieter. Re-extraction has to be scheduled, and nothing in the source says how often.

The rebuttal, 2026-08-09. "Once, offline" turns out to be a data requirement rather than a one-time inconvenience: fitting item parameters needs a response matrix over many models and many items, which a benchmark maintainer has as a byproduct and an application team does not (item-response-theory-subsetting-has-a-cold-start-cost-in-historical-response-logs-that-a-single-team-cannot-pay). The claim that this is the cheap and general variant does not survive. What survives is the claim for whoever already ran the full suite against a fleet.

Why single-study. One paper, one family of benchmarks, and the 99.8% figure comes from the single benchmark with the most dead weight to remove.

What would move this. A report of subset staleness over time — how many model generations a fixed informative subset survives before its rankings decouple from the full benchmark. That number decides whether this is a technique or a treadmill.
