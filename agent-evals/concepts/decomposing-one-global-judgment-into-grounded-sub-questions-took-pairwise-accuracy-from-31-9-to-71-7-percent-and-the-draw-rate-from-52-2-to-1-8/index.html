Decomposing one global judgment into grounded sub-questions took pairwise accuracy from 31.9% to 71.7% and the draw rate from 52.2% to 1.8%

Grounded in harnesseval-w-agentified-world-model-evaluation; belongs to design-for-verifiability.

Same videos, same evaluation questions, two protocols. A fixed-rubric baseline (WBench's Event Edit and Causal Fidelity protocols) against an agentified pipeline that routes each case to a skill, decomposes the skill into sub-questions, and answers each with a dedicated sub-agent returning "a discrete score together with diagnostics."

Against 5,000 human A/B judgments aggregated by Bradley-Terry: ρ = 0.93 (τ = 0.82) on intentional transitions, ρ = 0.87 (τ = 0.74) on physical.

This is the second independent arrival at the same ablation, and it is four times larger than the first. judging-each-of-25-rubrics-separately-agreed-with-experts-92-8-percent-of-the-time-and-judging-them-all-at-once-dropped-to-83 held judge, rubrics and answers fixed and asked the criteria one at a time instead of together, buying 9.8 points of expert agreement. This holds videos and questions fixed and does structurally the same thing — many separately grounded sub-questions instead of one global judgment — buying 39.8 points of pairwise accuracy. Different modality, different team, different reference standard, no shared framing, same direction. The vault's most actionable judge-design finding is no longer single-study.

The draw rate says what was actually broken, and it is not what the first study suggested. A global rubric declares a tie on 52.2% of physical comparisons. Its "accuracy" is therefore dominated by abstention: it is not judging wrongly so much as failing to resolve. Decomposition drops draws to 1.8%. So the gain is not that many small judgments are individually smarter — it is that a single global question does not separate two candidates, and every sub-question that resolves adds separating power. That is a cleaner mechanism than StartupBench's (which attributed the loss to format contamination across bundled criteria), and it predicts where the technique pays: wherever your judge returns "about the same" a lot.

The two mechanisms are compatible and probably both real. Bundling twenty-five criteria into one output invites format failure and needs retries; asking one holistic question about a rich artifact invites abstention. Both are cured by the same move. A team should expect the gain to show up as either fewer invalid outputs or fewer ties, depending on which failure their judge currently has — and both are observable in logs they already hold.

Practice. Look at your judge's tie rate before anything else. If a meaningful share of comparisons come back as draws or as mid-scale scores, the instrument is not discriminating and no amount of prompt work on the global question will fix it. Split the question, score each part discretely, and combine in code.

Bounds. The baseline is two specific WBench protocols, not fixed-rubric evaluation in general, and a rubric engineered for pairwise discrimination might close part of the draw-rate gap. No ablation isolates decomposition from the other machinery — the planner, the route validator, the per-skill tooling — so 39.8 points is attributed to a bundle. No cost is reported anywhere: eight sub-agents per skill is plainly an order of magnitude more model calls than one rubric pass, and this vault has now recorded the same missing denominator twice for the same technique. Human reference covers nine of the eighteen models evaluated. World-model rollouts are far from the text and code artifacts most of this vault concerns, so the transfer rests on agreement with StartupBench rather than on domain similarity — hence suggestive rather than strong.
