A three-annotator audit of 200 sampled tasks against six criteria is the first countable human-labour line item on the build side

Grounded in worksurface-bench-multi-surface-knowledge-routing; belongs to build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling and what-an-eval-costs-to-run.

WorkSurface-Bench validated its own corpus with an independent audit: three annotators, 200 sampled tasks, six quality criteria. All 200 passed all six criteria by majority vote, and 192 of 200 received unanimous judgments on every criterion. The corpus is 1,151 tasks, so the audit sampled roughly 17%.

Why this matters to a theme that has been stuck. build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling has been held at emerging across twenty-three sources for one stated reason: the build column is entirely unpriced. Every source describes what building involves and none states what any of it costs. This is the first construction cost expressed in countable units — 3 × 200 × 6 judgments — attached to a corpus of known size. It is not hours and it is not dollars, and it prices the validation of a corpus rather than its creation. It is still the closest thing to a line item this theme has, and it is worth recording as such rather than describing yet another activity.

The more transferable finding is the sample rate. A 17% sample with three annotators was enough to report corpus quality with a number. That makes construction validation a bounded, plannable task rather than an open-ended one, which is the practical obstacle to doing it at all. Anyone building a few-hundred-task suite can now scale from a published precedent: three annotators, six named criteria, one in six tasks.

The unanimity rate is itself a result. 192 of 200 unanimous on every criterion says the six criteria were operationalized well enough that independent people applied them the same way — which is the thing an inter-annotator number is actually for, and which almost nothing in this vault reports. Compare judge-configuration-is-an-unpriced-construction-parameter-with-two-benchmarks-choosing-opposite-defaults: three judges are used all over this vault to score runs and never to validate the corpus, and the second use is where their agreement rate is cheap to obtain and most informative.

What it does not answer. evals-that-localize-the-fix carries an open question — how often does transcript auditing reject a scored success? This audit is adjacent and does not answer it. It audits task quality before any run, not scored outcomes after one. The two are different obligations and this vault should not let the first stand in for the second.

Why single-study. One benchmark reporting its own audit of its own corpus, with the criteria unnamed in the abstract. A 100% majority pass rate on a self-designed audit is the expected outcome and carries little information about corpus quality; what carries information is that the audit was specified, sampled, and reported at all.

What would move this theme. The thing still missing after twenty-three sources: hours. Somebody stating what their eval cost to build — annotator time, engineer time, maintenance since — against what adopting a framework would have cost. A judgment count is a step toward that and is not it.
