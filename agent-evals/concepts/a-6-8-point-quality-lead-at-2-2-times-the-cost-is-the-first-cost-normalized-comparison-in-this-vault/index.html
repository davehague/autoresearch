A 6.8 point quality lead at 2.2 times the cost is the first cost normalized comparison in this vault

Grounded in frontierfinance-rubric-scale-and-cost-normalized-results; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run.

Under a common harness restricted to publicly available data: "Samaya's in-house system leads at 56.0%, ahead of the strongest frontier model (Claude Fable 5, 49.2%) at roughly 2.2x lower cost," and "the best open-weight model (Kimi K3, 46.4%) nearly matches the best proprietary model at 4.5x lower cost."

This answers the theme's standing complaint about cost-normalized reporting, 52 sources in. The open question reads: almost nothing in this vault reports what a run cost alongside what it scored, which is why a 15-point harness gap cannot be priced, and drawai-image-to-editable-reconstruction was the lone exception. Here the ratios are in the headline rather than an appendix: 6.8 points of quality for 2.2× the spend, and an open-weight model within 2.8 points of the best proprietary one at 4.5× less. That is the first time this vault can put quality and money in the same sentence with real numbers on both.

The shape of the answer is more useful than the numbers. Both ratios say the same thing — the quality axis is compressed and the cost axis is not. A few points of quality cost multiples of money, which means for most deployments the ranking that matters is not the leaderboard order but the order after dividing. Any team choosing a system from a benchmark table without a cost column is reading half the result.

Read against the theme's other direction, it inverts the usual framing. This theme mostly prices the evaluation — items, judges, human labels, rollouts. This prices the system under test, and it does so through the eval, because the eval is the only place both numbers are measured under a common harness. That is a second reason to run an eval at all, and it is not the reason anyone here has given: not "is it better" but "is it better per dollar," which is only answerable if the harness records spend.

The important caveat is who is holding the pen. The leading system belongs to the benchmark's authors, so the 56.0% is a vendor reporting its own win. The cost ratios are the more trustworthy half, because they favour systems the authors did not build — the open-weight result at 4.5× lower cost is the finding least aligned with the authors' interest and therefore the one to lean on.

Bounds. One benchmark, one domain, one snapshot of model pricing, which decays fast. "Roughly 2.2x lower cost" is stated without the units or the measurement method — tokens, wall clock, or billed dollars is unspecified in the abstract. Single run, no intervals, and the two hardest use cases sit at 33% and 39%, low enough that ordering near the floor is noisy.
