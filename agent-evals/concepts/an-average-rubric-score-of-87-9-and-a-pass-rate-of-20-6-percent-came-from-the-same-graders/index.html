An average rubric score of 87.9 and a pass rate of 20.6% came from the same graders on the same 97 tasks, so a rubric mean over a conjunctive contract measures a different thing than the contract

Grounded in frontierchallenge-scientific-workflow-completion; belongs to what-the-headline-number-averages-away.

97 end-to-end scientific workflows, each with a task-specific executable Grader returning a native score in [0, 100]. Two metrics are computed from identical Grader outputs: Avg. Score is the mean of those scores; Pass Rate is the mean of 1[s ≥ 99.9].

Across twelve models and three scaffolds: Avg. Score ranged 67.5–87.9. Pass Rate ranged 3.1%–20.6%. Eight configurations scored above 80 on average and none completed more than 20.6% of tasks. The best configurations (GPT-5.6 Sol with Codex, Grok 4.6 with Claude Code) completed 20 of 97.

This is the cleanest un-averaging demonstration in the vault, because the confound every previous one carried is absent. Prior entries compared a mean against a per-item breakdown drawn from different scoring passes, different judges, or different suites, so a sceptic could always argue the two numbers were measuring different things through different instruments. Here they are the same instrument, the same run, the same rubric outputs, differing only in the final arithmetic step.

The mechanism is arithmetic, not statistical, which is why no amount of repetition or interval-reporting fixes it. A deliverable bundle satisfying nineteen of twenty rubric requirements scores 95 and passes nothing. The mean is an average over a conjunction; the conjunction is what the user cares about. A rubric mean is not a noisier version of the pass rate. On this suite, in two domains, it is nearly uninformative about it (two-domains-averaged-87-6-and-94-9-while-passing-4-percent-and-0-percent-of-their-tasks).

The paper says so about its own metric, in the methods section, unprompted. Avg. Score is labelled "a descriptive suite-level aggregate rather than a calibrated cross-task scale," because the task-specific rubrics are heterogeneous. That is a benchmark author declining to let the friendlier number be read as capability, which is rarer than it should be and is the behaviour this theme has been asking for since it opened.

The practical rule follows directly and is short. If your eval scores a bundle of requirements that must all hold, report the conjunction as the primary number and the mean only as a progress indicator — in that order, with the pass criterion stated. And note the corollary the paper does not spell out: Pass Rate at a strict threshold falls mechanically as the number of required deliverables per task rises, so pass rates are not comparable across suites with different contract lengths. A suite with three deliverables per task and one with twelve produce numbers that look alike and are not.

Two related results in the same paper make this worse rather than better. Non-passing runs claimed completion 75.5% of the time (75-5-percent-of-non-passing-agent-runs-ended-by-claiming-completion-and-the-rate-rose-with-the-score), and tool errors appeared more often in passing runs than failing ones (tool-errors-appeared-in-more-passing-runs-than-failing-ones-so-error-presence-is-not-a-triage-signal). So on this suite the three cheapest ways to tell whether an agent finished the job — its average score, its own account, and whether anything went wrong — all fail, and the only thing that works is checking the artifacts.

Single study, single runs per configuration with no repeat-run variance reported — which is the sharpest limit, because the primary metric is a 0/1 threshold and thresholds are where variance bites hardest. 97 of 300 tasks, the rest held out internally. Six domains that are descriptive slices of the release rather than probability samples, so cross-domain comparisons are not rankings of disciplinary difficulty and the authors say so. Confirms strict-pass-and-process-reward-diverge-in-every-configuration-so-partial-credit-is-a-second-question-rather-than-a-rescue-for-hard-benchmarks at larger scale and with the two numbers finally coming out of one grader.
