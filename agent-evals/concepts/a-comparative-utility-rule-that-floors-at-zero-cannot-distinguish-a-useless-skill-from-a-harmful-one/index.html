A comparative utility rule that floors at zero cannot distinguish a useless skill from a harmful one

Grounded in skilltester-paired-utility-and-security-probes; belongs to what-the-headline-number-averages-away.

SkillTester's utility score has three cases: the skill fails and scores 0; the baseline fails but the skill succeeds and scores 100; both succeed and the score is 20–100 scaled by efficiency.

The framework commits to a counterfactual and then measures it on an unsigned scale, which throws away the half of the counterfactual that matters most. "Skill fails" collapses two events with opposite meanings. Baseline succeeded and the skill failed is a regression — the capability package broke something that was already working, and it should be removed today. Both failed is a hard task, and says nothing about the skill at all. Both score 0. The scale therefore cannot rank the harmful skills against each other, cannot separate them from the merely inert ones, and cannot tell a catalogue owner which of the zeros is an incident.

A signed scale on the same design does surface the tail, which is how we know this is a choice rather than a limitation of paired trials. The same construction measured with a signed composite reports positive lift in 72.8% of 947 paired evaluations (seventy-three-percent-of-enterprise-skills-showed-positive-lift-so-the-mean-is-averaging-over-a-quarter-that-did-not-help) — a statement that has meaning only because "not positive" is expressible. The general rule: if your measurement is a counterfactual, the scale should be signed, because the interesting result of removing something is often that removing it helps.

A floor at zero is a specific kind of averaging-away and it is worth naming as its own failure. This vault's usual case is a mean hiding a distribution. Here the distribution is truncated before the mean is taken — the information is destroyed at scoring time, not at reporting time, so no amount of publishing the distribution recovers it. That is strictly worse, and it is invisible to a reader who sees only the rule and thinks it looks sensible.

The consequence in deployment is concrete because this rule is live. A public scoring service assigns skills a score and a three-level label that people use to decide what to install. A skill that reliably breaks working tasks scores 0 and sits alongside skills that were simply never useful, and nothing in the output distinguishes them. The user-facing simplicity principle the report invokes is a real design value, and it has been paid for here with the one distinction the audience most needs.
