Nine hundred labelled items with written rationales is what a production judge benchmark cost

Grounded in netflix-judge-lifecycle-recommendation-explanations; belongs to the-judge-as-a-maintained-dependency and what-an-eval-costs-to-run.

Phase I of the lifecycle is building the benchmark, and the authors' first stated lesson is "invest in the benchmark before the judge." What it cost:

  • ~900 human-labelled explanations, split close to evenly between pass and fail.
  • ~54% failures — deliberately over-sampled relative to production prevalence, so that the minority class the judge exists to catch is not a handful of items.
  • Three provenance streams mixed on purpose: expert-crafted examples, LLM-synthesized boundary cases, and production samples.
  • Every failure label carries a free-text rationale from the rater, not just the label.

This sits at the top of a ladder this vault has been assembling from below. Thirty examples with a saturation rule was the smallest judge dataset recorded here. A graduated ladder put 20 labels at "enough to score" and 50 at "enough to fit." A practitioner's rule of thumb put a working judge at about a hundred labels. Two hundred samples was priced at ±2.4 points on a 3% defect rate. Nine hundred is what a company serving hundreds of thousands of judged items a week decided it needed, and the gap between 100 and 900 is not a contradiction — it is the difference between a judge you use to steer your own iteration and a judge that gates what ships to members.

Two construction choices are worth copying independently of the count.

The deliberate class rebalance. At production prevalence the failure class would be small, and every agreement statistic computed on it would be dominated by the majority class — the problem this vault has recorded twice under "raw agreement is only admissible near balanced classes." Building the benchmark at ~54% failures fixes the measurement and breaks the prevalence, which means specificity and recall measured here do not transfer to production rates without a correction. The report does not perform that correction, and the practice note is to perform it.

The three-stream mix. Expert-crafted items encode what the team already knows to look for. Synthesized boundary cases probe where the rubric is ambiguous. Production samples are the only stream that can contain a failure mode nobody anticipated. Any one stream alone has a known blind spot; the mix is a hedge, and it is cheap to specify up front and expensive to retrofit.

The rationale column is the part with the second use. It is needed to debug the rubric. It also turns out to be the only artifact that makes reasoning-agreement tuning possible at all — see a-production-judge-was-tuned-on-why-it-failed-an-item-not-just-whether-it-failed. One annotator hour buying both the ground truth and the material for the judge prompt is a pattern already recorded in this vault; this is the same trade at production scale.

Bounds. One company, one task family. "Roughly 900" is the only size figure given; the split across the three provenance streams is not reported, so how much of the benchmark is production data is unknown. No annotator count, no hours, no cost — the label budget is stated in items, not in money or time, so this prices the artifact and not the effort.
