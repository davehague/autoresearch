Fixing grading bugs moved one model from 42% to 95%, so the measuring instrument can own more of a score than anything it measures

Grounded in anthropic-demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents; belongs to evals-that-localize-the-fix.

On CORE-Bench, Claude Opus 4.5 initially scored 42%. After grading bugs were fixed, the same model on the same benchmark scored 95%. Fifty-three points, attributable to nothing but the scoring code.

Why this extends. This theme has been accumulating terms the model-versus-harness frame has no slot for — reasoning effort, the risk carrier, seven unreported environment parameters, and the judge — and the standing complaint about all of them is that they were named and never quantified. The judge got an estimator when an-item-response-model-separating-agent-quality-from-judge-severity-is-a-fourth-panel-estimator-that-neither-votes-nor-averages arrived, and still no magnitude. Here is a magnitude for the instrument term, and it is larger than every other effect in this vault put together: 53 points against DataSpace's 15.36 for a harness swap, against the 11-point band across nine harnesses in final-answer-correctness-spans-eleven-points-across-nine-harnesses-while-planning-tool-use-and-efficiency-vary-much-more-so-the-headline-axis-is-where-the-harness-effect-hides.

It is a different mechanism from judge severity, and the difference matters. Severity is a systematic bias that a model can estimate out, and it moves everyone in the same direction. A grader defect is idiosyncratic — it mis-scores whatever it happens to mis-score, so it does not cancel across systems and cannot be corrected after the fact. It is also, unlike every other term in this list, fully within the eval author's control and free to fix.

Where it lands operationally. preserving-a-nan-score-instead-of-coercing-it-is-an-aggregation-decision-the-harness-was-making-silently argued for the cheapest integrity check on any eval run — compare scored n against submitted n. This is the reason that check earns its keep, and it generalizes: before you compare anything, establish that the grader can grade. A 42% that is really a 95% looks exactly like a hard benchmark, which is the failure mode — nobody investigates a low score, because low scores are what new benchmarks are supposed to produce.

The decision it changes. Run the grader against known-good outputs before you trust a single comparison. If you have a reference solution, a passing human transcript, or a previous run you believe, score it and check it comes out right. That is an afternoon, it is not in any construction skeleton this vault has recorded, and on this evidence it is worth more than any measurement decision downstream of it.

Why single-study. One example, mentioned in passing in a vendor guide, with no description of the bugs, no statement of whether other models on the same benchmark moved by similar amounts, and no independent confirmation. It is an anecdote. It is an anecdote about a 53-point swing on a public benchmark, which is why it is here — but nobody should generalize the magnitude, and the fair reading is "a grader defect can be arbitrarily large," not "grader defects are worth about 50 points."

What would move this. A survey of published benchmarks re-scored after grader review, reporting how many scores moved and by how much. Cheaper and nearly as useful: anyone reporting that they validated their grader before publishing, since as of now nobody in this vault says they did.
