Two open-weight judges recovered reviewer preference at about 70 percent for a tenth of the best judge's cost

Grounded in proofjudge-tool-grounded-formal-proof-quality; belongs to what-an-eval-costs-to-run.

Six judge models scored 218 accepted-versus-returned Mathlib pairs. "All six judge models evaluated recover the reviewers' preference well above chance, from 80.8% to 63.5%, and two open-weight judges reach roughly 70% at a tenth of the best judge's cost."

This is a quantified accuracy-for-price trade on a judge, which this vault has been asking for and mostly getting as an assertion. The cost thread here is dominated by results that shrink the corpus — coreset selection, IRT item pruning, variance-weighted validation sampling with its USD 117 to USD 30 receipt (sampling-validation-tasks-by-outcome-variance-cut-a-harness-search-from-117-to-30-dollars-and-landed-within-one-task-of-full-set-search). Those change how many items you grade. This changes what does the grading, and it prices the change: roughly 10.8 points of alignment given up for roughly a 90% reduction in judge inference cost. A team can now compute whether that trade is acceptable for their use rather than guess.

Combined with the panel control already on file, it specifies a configuration nobody in this vault has run. Most of a judge panel's gain turns out to be resampling rather than vendor diversity — a homogeneous three-sample jury of one model captured about 85% of a five-model cross-family jury's benefit (three-samples-of-one-model-gained-13-points-so-most-of-the-panel-effect-is-sampling-not-diversity). Put the two together and the cheap configuration is explicit: three samples of a 70%-alignment open-weight judge, for roughly a third of what one 80.8% frontier call costs. Both halves of that arithmetic are now published, in this vault, from independent sources. The arm itself remains unrun, and it is one afternoon on a holdout that already exists.

The 17-point spread across six models is the other half of the cost story and it is a warning about what "cheap" is being measured against. 80.8% to 63.5% on identical items under an identical rubric means the backbone is the dominant term — a finding this vault has now recorded three times, alongside the conservative/permissive backbone split (two-judge-backbones-produce-opposite-conservative-and-permissive-failure-profiles-so-a-mixed-panel-averages-to-a-rate-matching-neither). The floor at 63.5% is barely above the point where a judge stops being worth its own inference cost, so "an open-weight judge" is not a category with a price; it is six different instruments with a 17-point range, and the two good ones were identified by running this validation.

Bounds. No confidence intervals are quoted on any of the six rates, and on 218 items the difference between "roughly 70%" and the 63.5% floor may not be resolvable. The ratio is inference price at one moment, excluding the cost of hosting an open-weight model, which is the term that usually decides this question in practice. And a tenfold saving on the judge only matters where the judge is a material share of the bill — this vault's own cost work has repeatedly found the transcript, not the grader, holding the money.
