In 82.5% of 800 trajectories the agent diagnosed a critical problem and then reported the unrevised conclusion

Grounded in autoresearcheval-diagnostic-failure-taxonomy; belongs to design-for-verifiability.

Pattern F.4, uncorrected self-awareness, is the single most frequent failure across 800 trajectories from eight model–harness combinations: "in 82.5% of analyses the agent diagnoses a critical problem during self-review, then reports the unrevised conclusion." Two case studies show the shape. D.5: "The diagnosis is correct; the response is not." D.7: the agent writes that the "central claim rests on a baseline that is almost certainly broken," and proceeds to report the claim.

This is a check that fires and gates nothing, and it is the most important distinction this theme has been missing. design-for-verifiability has spent its whole run arguing about whether a check is possible — restructure the artifact, place the check locally, make it deterministic, validate the grader. It has implicitly assumed that a check which runs and returns a correct verdict has done its job. Eighty-two percent says otherwise. The self-review here is not broken: it produces the right finding, in writing, in the trace. What is missing is anything downstream obliged to act on it.

The second-order consequence is worse than the first. The trace contains a correct critical finding. So an eval reading that trace — or a human skimming it — sees evidence of careful self-assessment. The presence of the check makes the run look more trustworthy while changing nothing about the output, which is a strictly worse position than not checking at all. It is the same failure direction as fixing-grading-bugs-moved-one-model-from-42-to-95-percent-so-the-measuring-instrument-can-own-more-of-a-score-than-anything-it-measures and the reversal-approving similarity gate: the dashboard stays green because the instrument is producing output, not because the output is being obeyed.

The mechanism is structural, and the paper names it. "The agent is also its own reviewer, so a limitation can be written down in one section without disturbing the headline in another." Nothing enforces consistency between the review section and the conclusion section, because both are text the same agent emits and no gate sits between them. Compare the design that does work in this vault: a hidden ordered checklist that returns "feedback identifying only the first unsatisfied checklist item" and will not advance (agentrewind-mettlebench-recoverable-execution). That check is not smarter — it is binding.

Practice, two versions. For the eval: score whether the agent's self-identified problems appear in its conclusions, which is a mechanical text comparison over traces you already have and needs no judge. For the system: a self-review whose output is not wired to a gate, a retry, or a refusal to deliver is a logging feature, and should be priced as one.

Bounds. Single study. 82.5% is produced by an agent-as-judge validated on 50 of 800 trajectories with "no per-pattern or per-pillar agreement" reported, so this specific pattern's frequency carries an unmeasured judge error. F.4 has an unusually subjective definition compared to the executable-check failures elsewhere in this vault — it requires reading whether a diagnosis and a conclusion contradict each other. Six of the eight model–harness combinations use one harness (Claude Code), so the near-universality of the pattern may partly be a property of that harness's self-review prompting. All runs sit under "fixed wall-clock and token budgets" and the authors flag that some patterns "may correlate with resource pressure" — an agent out of budget has an obvious reason not to revise.
