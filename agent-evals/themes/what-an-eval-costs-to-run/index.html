What an eval costs to run

Status: emerging

Sub-threads: cutting the item count · the dials that push the bill the other way · levers that turn out not to be levers · what teams actually reach for when the bill lands · who can afford the method that cuts the bill · the dials that buy validity rather than thrift · sizing up from the precision you need rather than down from a benchmark · pricing the measurement instead of the system · grading strength traded against item count · what the cheap substitute for human reading actually delivers · a cheap method that buys nothing · quality per dollar rather than quality · cutting the label budget instead of the item count [reviewed 2026-08-17 @ 28 bullets]

Scope

Not which eval to run or how to build it, but what it costs every time it fires — and how much of that cost buys nothing. This theme collects evidence that eval set size is a design parameter rather than a fixed price, and the methods for setting it.

Through-line

  • 2026-08-07 — opened at emerging. Running a modern safety benchmark suite in full takes on the order of 10^5 model responses, most of which carry no ranking signal because every model in the pool passes or fails those items alike. Selecting items by how much they discriminate reproduced full-benchmark rankings at at least 80% lower cost, and up to 99.9% on one benchmark (adaptive-item-selection-reproduces-full-benchmark-rankings-at-80-to-99-percent-lower-cost-so-eval-set-size-is-a-dial, efficient-safety-benchmarking-via-item-response-theory).

  • 2026-08-07 — and the version worth adopting is the boring one: extracting a fixed informative subset once and reusing it captured up to 99.8%, against 99.9% for live adaptive selection — nearly all the benefit with no machinery left in the run loop (a-fixed-informative-item-subset-extracted-once-captures-most-of-the-savings-without-adaptive-machinery-in-the-loop). The generalizable shape: when a technique's value comes from an analysis rather than a runtime behaviour, ship the analysis output, not the analyzer.

  • 2026-08-07 — the bill acquires a second dial, and this one multiplies rather than adds. Judge count and judge family are set by fiat in every benchmark here: one source reuses its own generator model as its Faithfulness judge, another runs a cross-family panel of three on its taste axes, and neither reports what the choice bought (judge-configuration-is-an-unpriced-construction-parameter-with-two-benchmarks-choosing-opposite-defaults). Three judges is three times the judging spend on every item, forever. If a single cross-family judge captures most of what a panel does, that is the same shape of result as the fixed-subset finding above and worth as much.

  • 2026-08-07 — and the counter-pressure, stated plainly so this theme does not read as one-directional. The eval that answered the vault's contested-ground-truth gap costs 30 simulated days × 55 scenarios × 10 adapters × 3 model tiers, with a three-judge panel on three of five axes (educlaw-bench-pedagogical-agents-simulated-learners). The horizon-based designs arriving in evaluating-over-a-horizon are all like this. Item selection cuts the item count; measuring a trajectory instead of a point multiplies the run length, and nobody has looked for the shortest horizon that still surfaces the effect.

  • 2026-08-07 — a lever that looked free and is not. Sweep an explicitly stated, explicitly priced budget across the threshold where the right answer changes, and GPT-5.4's escalation rate moves from 0% to 3% (a-stated-budget-barely-reaches-agent-behaviour-so-eval-spend-has-to-be-cut-structurally-rather-than-by-instruction, ecoagent-bench-budget-constrained-agents). Prompt-level thrift — tell it the budget, tell it to prefer the cheap tool — is a request, not a control. Everything with evidence behind it in this theme changes what the harness executes rather than what the model is told, and that is now a distinction with a result behind it rather than a preference. The sharper version: to make an agent spend less, take the expensive action away rather than pricing it.

  • 2026-08-08 — the counterfactual to this theme's opening finding, observed in the wild. A team hit the closed-model cost wall against a full suite and cut it by declaring a random 300-item subset (declaring-a-random-300-item-subset-for-expensive-models-is-the-ad-hoc-version-of-informative-item-selection, simplewikisearch-specified-offline-search-environment). Random-n buys the cost saving with an unbiased estimate of the mean and no guarantee about ranking preservation, which is precisely the property the item-response-theory result optimizes for and the property a comparison needs. Two subsetting strategies are now visible in this vault and nobody has run them against each other on one suite. The cheap intermediate nobody does: run the full suite once with open models, report the subset-to-full rank correlation, and turn an assumption into a stated property.

  • 2026-08-08 — the judge dial picks up a third setting, so the spread on the judging line of every run is now a factor of several with no guidance anywhere. Self-judging at 1×, a cross-family panel of three restricted to taste axes, and three judges with majority voting across the scored axes (a-third-judge-configuration-in-one-month-three-judges-with-majority-voting-on-every-axis-widens-an-already-unjustified-range, stealthbench-operational-stealth-in-offensive-security-agents). Three papers, one month, three answers, no agreement rates reported by any of them.

  • 2026-08-08 — and a cost this theme had not been counting, from the human side. Validating a 1,151-task corpus took three annotators over a 17% sample against six criteria (a-three-annotator-audit-of-200-sampled-tasks-against-six-criteria-is-the-first-countable-human-labour-line-item-on-the-build-side, worksurface-bench-multi-surface-knowledge-routing). It is a one-time construction cost rather than a per-run one, so it sits at the edge of this theme's scope — recorded here because it is the only human-labour figure anywhere in this vault, and because the 17% sample rate makes the obligation bounded rather than open-ended.

  • 2026-08-09 — the theme's opening finding gets replicated, and the comparison it asked for arrives half-answered. A second, independent team fits IRT to eight safety benchmarks across 192 models and reports 97–99% cost cuts from roughly ten adaptively chosen items (a-second-independent-team-cuts-safety-evaluation-cost-97-to-99-percent-with-about-ten-items-so-the-item-selection-result-is-no-longer-single-source, item-response-theory-for-ai-safety). The same paper runs selected against random at equal size, and selected wins on score-recovery error (discrimination-selected-items-beat-random-subsets-of-the-same-size-which-settles-half-of-the-comparison-between-the-two-cheap-subsetting-rules). Score error is not rank preservation, which is the property this theme's open question named and the property random-n was suspected on, so the question narrows rather than closes. Worth sitting with the ten-item figure separately: a benchmark recoverable from ten items was measuring roughly one thing several hundred times, and a suite that compresses that far cannot see a regression outside what its ten items touch. Cheapness and blind spots are the same property.

  • 2026-08-09 — and the counter-bill, from a paper motivating its own method. Item-response selection has a cold start: the item parameters are fitted from a response matrix over many models and many items, which a leaderboard maintainer has as a byproduct and a single-product team does not (item-response-theory-subsetting-has-a-cold-start-cost-in-historical-response-logs-that-a-single-team-cannot-pay, cot-core-coreset-selection-for-eval-cost). That lands on the exact recommendation this theme opened with — extract a fixed subset once, offline — and splits it by reader. To extract the subset you must already have run the full suite against a fleet, which is the expense the subset exists to avoid. The theme's cheapest advice turns out to be unavailable to the team it was written for, and the disputed atom is now marked rebutted-by.

  • 2026-08-09 — a third subsetting rule, selecting on a third thing, and the only one available on day one. Roll out a zero-shot chain of thought per candidate item, embed the reasoning trajectory, cluster by logical equivalence, and take representatives — training-free, no history required (clustering-questions-by-reasoning-trajectory-rather-than-surface-text-is-a-third-subsetting-rule-whose-payoff-is-gated-by-task-complexity, cot-core-coreset-selection-for-eval-cost). Discrimination selection asks which items separate models; random-n asks nothing; this asks which items are redundant with each other. Carry the warning attached to it even if the method never pans out: deduplicating a suite by embedding question text has a surface lexical bias, so two cases with identical reasoning and different wording both survive and the suite looks more diverse than it is. And note what this bullet does not have — it is the only cost claim in this theme with no number against it, which is why it is a lead.

  • 2026-08-09 — the judge dial gets a fourth setting, and this one is an analysis rather than a spend. An IRT model that estimates judge severity as a separate latent from agent quality, over 28 agents scored by a cross-family panel (an-item-response-model-separating-agent-quality-from-judge-severity-is-a-fourth-panel-estimator-that-neither-votes-nor-averages, companionbench-judge-severity-separated-from-agent-quality). It does not cut the bill — the panel still runs on every item — which puts it alongside fixed-subset extraction in the same category: value that comes from an analysis after the fact rather than from a runtime behaviour. What it does supply is the first stated reason for a cross-family panel anywhere in this vault: same-family favouritism, named as a bias rather than assumed as good taste.

  • 2026-08-09 — and a use for the cheap subset that is not thrift. A ten-item probe run on a schedule against a hosted endpoint detects the model behind it changing (a-ten-item-psychometric-probe-detects-a-model-swapped-behind-an-api-which-turns-a-cheap-eval-subset-into-a-monitoring-instrument). Every team building on a vendor API has silent-swap exposure and almost none monitor it, because a full suite is too expensive to schedule and nothing smaller was thought to carry signal. No false-positive rate is reported, so this is a lead rather than a monitor.

  • 2026-08-10 — the third dial, and this theme has been reading it as pure expense. Trial count multiplies the bill linearly, which is why nearly every suite here runs k=1 — and at k=1 there is no observable difference between a 75% agent and a 99% one, because reliability only becomes visible across repeats (pass-at-k-and-pass-to-the-k-are-the-optimistic-and-weakest-link-endpoints-of-one-operator-family-applied-across-trials-of-a-single-task, anthropic-demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents). So k is a joint cost-and-validity dial rather than a thrift dial, and it sits opposite item count in an interesting way: cutting items costs coverage, cutting trials costs the ability to measure consistency at all. A team on a fixed budget is choosing between them and nothing here helps with that choice. The one free move: at 75% per trial, three trials all passing is 42%, and that conversion needs no runs at all.

  • 2026-08-10 — and the cost item this theme should have had first, arriving as an anecdote. On CORE-Bench one model scored 42%, then 95% once grading bugs were fixed (fixing-grading-bugs-moved-one-model-from-42-to-95-percent-so-the-measuring-instrument-can-own-more-of-a-score-than-anything-it-measures). Every optimization in this theme — informative subsets, coreset clustering, smaller panels — is spend on producing a number, and all of it is wasted if the grader is wrong, in the specific way that a broken grader produces a plausible-looking low score. Scoring one known-good output first is an afternoon and it gates the value of everything else here.

  • 2026-08-12 — the first sizing rule in this theme that goes up from the precision you need instead of down from somebody's benchmark. 200 samples at a 3% observed defect rate gives roughly 3% ± 2.4%; 400 gives 3% ± 1.7%; halving the margin of error costs four times the samples (two-hundred-samples-buys-plus-or-minus-2-4-points-on-a-3-percent-defect-rate-and-halving-that-error-costs-four-times-the-samples, eugeneyan-product-evals-in-three-steps). Every method here so far — discrimination selection, random-300, CoT clustering — subtracts from a large public suite, was demonstrated at benchmark-maintainer scale, and two of the three carry eligibility requirements a single-product team cannot meet. This one has no prerequisites: an estimate of your defect rate and a decision about the precision you need. It also prices the increment, which none of the subsetting rules do — the second 200 samples buy 0.7 points of precision and the fourth 200 buy less, so on a 3% base rate a team wanting to see a regression smaller than about two points is looking at four figures of samples per run. That is a different product than the one they thought they were building, and it is knowable before anything is built.

  • 2026-08-12 — and it sharpens the items-versus-trials question rather than answering it. Item count buys precision on the rate at a square-root rate; trial count buys visibility into consistency, which is invisible at k=1 no matter how many items you run (pass-at-k-and-pass-to-the-k-are-the-optimistic-and-weakest-link-endpoints-of-one-operator-family-applied-across-trials-of-a-single-task). 400 items at k=1 and 200 items at k=2 cost the same and measure different things. Both sides now have arithmetic against them and it is not the same arithmetic, which is progress on the framing and none on the choice.

  • 2026-08-12 — the second source in thirty-eight to report cost beside score, and the first to price a change rather than a grid: +7.5 points on Terminal-Bench 2.1 at 24% fewer tokens, model and backend fixed, harness swapped (a-score-gain-reported-alongside-a-24-percent-token-reduction-is-the-second-source-here-to-price-a-configuration-change, longhorizon-harness-loop-engineering). Every other cost result here is a trade — cheapness against coverage, consistency against linear spend, agreement against a multiplied judging bill. This claims both directions at once, and the plausible mechanism is avoided flailing rather than efficiency: blind-retry-reproduces-latent-faults-and-delays-detection-so-recovery-machinery-is-negative-value-without-attribution already established that undirected retry is where long runs burn budget, and tokens spent on a doomed trajectory are the waste that structure removes. Weak: a bare README figure from the harness's own authors, net over a loop that runs three agents where there was one, no per-role breakdown and no wall-clock. The pattern across both exceptions is worth naming — both report tokens, neither reports dollars or wall-clock, and both price the system under test rather than the measurement. What it costs to run the eval itself is still reported by nobody.

  • 2026-08-12 — the judge dial gets an instrument instead of a sixth setting, and a human floor underneath the whole sub-thread. krippendorff_alpha() ships in the runner (an-inter-rater-agreement-metric-in-the-runner-turns-judge-agreement-from-a-reporting-norm-into-a-one-line-call, inspect-ai-changelog-0-3-256-to-0-3-257), which makes this theme's open question about the marginal value of the second and third judge answerable for the first time — a high alpha is the strongest argument available for dropping a judge, and that is the ablation nobody has run. Against it: human raters on the same artifacts often agree at only kappa 0.2–0.3 (human-raters-agree-at-kappa-0-2-to-0-3-so-a-majority-vote-of-judges-converges-on-a-majority-rather-than-on-truth), so a low panel alpha may be reporting the rubric rather than the judges. And a cheap bias control appears for the first time anywhere here: running each pairwise comparison twice with the order swapped, which doubles the judging bill on pairwise items and, unlike a third judge, has a stated thing it detects.

  • 2026-08-13 — the theme's own question gets an answer, on the right axis this time, and the answer is a proportion rather than a price. Teams spend 60–80% of development time on error analysis and evaluation (error-analysis-and-evaluation-take-60-to-80-percent-of-development-time-which-is-the-first-figure-to-price-the-measurement-rather-than-the-system, hamel-llm-evals-faq). Every prior cost result here prices the system under test — tokens per configuration, dollars per grid, items per corpus. This prices the measurement, which is what the theme is named for, in the only unit a practitioner naturally holds. What it does to the build figures already recorded is more interesting than the number: four-weeks-to-a-working-eval-harness-is-the-first-build-figure-covering-criteria-labels-judge-alignment-and-runner-as-one-number and Bolt's three months describe projects with an end, and a standing 60–80% allocation says there is no end — four weeks buys you the state in which you can spend most of your time evaluating. If both are roughly right, the build cost is the small number and this theme has spent six runs chasing it. It also explains why the hours question kept coming back empty: there is no separable eval project to bill when error analysis is interleaved with development. Weak in the obvious ways — an unsourced range with an unstated denominator, from a practitioner with an eval course to sell.

  • 2026-08-13 — a three-tier price ladder for evaluators, and a judge turns out to have a recurring term. Cheap: assertions, regex, reference checks. Medium: structural validation, execution tests. Expensive: "LLM-as-Judge evaluators require 100+ labeled examples, ongoing weekly maintenance," to be built only for "problems you'll iterate on repeatedly" (an-llm-judge-costs-a-hundred-labels-plus-weekly-maintenance-so-it-is-a-dependency-rather-than-an-artifact). Every judge cost recorded here was a construction cost; a maintenance line makes a judge a standing liability and supplies the missing half of the decision rule — maintenance amortises over iterations, so a judge built for a one-off comparison is priced as if permanent. The three procedures now on file compose into an actual budget on one label set: ~100 labels is the entry fee, holding-out-25-percent-and-reporting-precision-recall-and-kappa-is-the-first-stated-acceptance-procedure-for-a-judge spends a quarter of them on the holdout, and validating-a-judge-is-shipped-as-a-separate-artifact-from-writing-one-and-adds-bias-correction-from-measured-tpr-and-tnr reuses the same set for calibration. That is the first end-to-end costing of a judge available here, and it is small enough to be encouraging. Ordering evaluators by price rather than by fidelity also inverts how every benchmark paper here justifies its grader.

  • 2026-08-13 — and a cost this theme should log against tonight's other findings rather than for them: the against-prior control in harness-if-against-prior-accuracy multiplies the run count by roughly the number of probe builds (nine), and the repeated-runs discipline in infrabench-durability-and-per-check-scoring triples it. Both are the right thing to do and neither paper prices it. The pattern across every construction improvement this vault has endorsed is now visible: they are all paid in runs, and runs are the one cost nobody reports.

  • 2026-08-14 — the first dollar figure and the first wall-clock figure in this vault, forty-eight sources in. "In a fully autonomous long-horizon loop, it executes 253 tool calls and 11 editing turns within 40 minutes for under $3" (a-long-horizon-run-priced-at-253-tool-calls-and-11-editing-turns-in-40-minutes-for-under-three-dollars-is-the-first-currency-and-clock-figure-here, autodesign-meta-harness-optimization). The 08-12 complaint was that the only two sources pricing anything both reported tokens, neither reported dollars or wall-clock, and both priced the system under test. Half of that is now closed, and a dollar figure is usable in a way a token count is not — every token number here needs a price list the reader has to supply and that changes monthly, whereas $3 against a 100-item suite is $300 a pass, a line a team can hold a decision against. The action decomposition is the more transferable half: 253 tool calls and 11 editing turns says where the money went, and transfers to any loop. It is still the wrong side of the ledger, which is what this theme is named for — what a graded pass of the benchmark cost, what the system-blind human study cost, and what the meta-optimizer's rollout loop cost to learn the harness are all unreported. The last of those is a new cost category and the interesting one: a harness improved from rollout feedback is paid for in evaluation runs, so it carries an eval bill as part of its construction, and the one paper that could have reported it left it blank. Weakest evidence class this theme accepts: the system's own authors, in the sentence advertising the system's efficiency, one loop, one task, no repeats, no human-evaluation cost at all.

  • 2026-08-14 — the exchange rate this theme keeps circling gets its far endpoint. Machine-checked proof of specification is the crispest grading condition available and it buys 43 hand-specified instances (the-crispest-available-grader-buys-the-smallest-corpus-here-at-43-hand-specified-instances, vero-repository-scale-verified-code-synthesis), against 50 to 410 for the curated corpora here and five figures for the generated ones. Grading strength and item count trade against each other through one budget, and this theme has only ever worked the cheap-grader end of it — every technique collected above (discrimination selection, random-n, trajectory clustering) cuts items from a suite that has items to spare, and none of them reach a suite of 43. The opposite regime, where the suite is small because each item is expensive, has no advice attached to it here. Note also that the paper prices nothing: 43 is an outcome, not a cost, and inferring curation effort from it is inference. The build column still has no figure in hours.

  • 2026-08-14 — the cheap substitute for the transcript-audit bill gets measured for the first time, and it reads a third of the corpus. Six agent conditions doing open coding over 451 items covered "around 25-35%" — best 67.8%, worst 5.5% — because instructed to work through the whole corpus, "agents don't do this"; "at some point they stop and declare the analysis complete" (six-agent-conditions-covered-25-to-35-percent-of-the-corpus-and-declared-the-analysis-complete-so-a-machine-error-analysis-pass-needs-a-coverage-denominator, shankar-agent-assisted-qualitative-analysis). This is the first measurement of parallel-error-analysis-agents-over-stored-traces-is-the-first-report-of-the-transcript-audit-obligation-being-paid-by-machine, recorded 2026-08-10 as unvalidated with a prior from do-automated-evals-work that automation would get the mechanical classes and miss the ones needing taste. The prior lands verbatim — "agents can do the mechanical parts of qualitative analysis fast, but they have no taste" — from an independent practitioner on a structurally identical task. Coverage is the worse finding and nobody was watching for it. A taxonomy built from a third of the traces looks complete and nothing downstream detects the rest, and it defeats this theme's own stopping rule: twenty-traces-with-no-new-failure-category-is-the-first-stated-stopping-rule-for-the-transcript-audit-obligation says stop after 20 consecutive traces with nothing new, which an agent that quits early satisfies trivially and wrongly, since traces never read cannot contribute a category. Saturation rules assume a reader who keeps reading. The repair is free and belongs on every automated pass: report the coverage denominator — count the traces that received at least one label and print it beside the taxonomy. Same class of check as scored-n against submitted-n. What this does not do is close the lead; the human-reviewed condition worked far better and cheaply. It prices the naive version correctly at a fifth to a third of the work, with no signal that the rest is missing.

  • 2026-08-15 — the first cheap method in this theme that buys nothing. Replaying a logged agent trajectory with one model's output substituted is cheaper than every cost cut catalogued above, because it spends zero model calls on the counterfactual. Forking the trajectories live instead — six paired runs, ~900 rollouts, 359 same-model control forks — leaves "only 3% of replayed states valid," and the replay evaluator "mispredicts every success-relevant outcome call and predicts patches with 0.00-0.11 similarity to reality" (replay-with-one-substituted-model-leaves-3-percent-of-states-valid-and-mispredicts-every-success-relevant-outcome, the-replay-gap-static-evaluation-of-model-switching). Every other saving here — item selection, coresets, fixed informative subsets, machine transcript audits — was priced against a validity cost that was measured or bounded. This one is total on the axis anyone cares about, and invisible in the output, because a replay evaluator returns a perfectly well-formed score. The generalisable claim is about locality, not routers: any method that splices a per-step intervention into a stored run assumes the effect stays local, and it does not.

  • 2026-08-15 — this theme's own open question, answered 52 sources in, and on the other side of the ledger. The complaint below reads: almost nothing here reports what a run cost alongside what it scored. FrontierFinance puts both in the headline — a system leading at 56.0% over the strongest frontier model at 49.2% and "roughly 2.2x lower cost," and the best open-weight model at 46.4% for 4.5× less than the best proprietary one (a-6-8-point-quality-lead-at-2-2-times-the-cost-is-the-first-cost-normalized-comparison-in-this-vault, frontierfinance-rubric-scale-and-cost-normalized-results). The shape matters more than the figures: the quality axis is compressed and the cost axis is not, so a few points cost multiples of money and the ranking that decides anything is the one after dividing. It also names a second reason to run an eval that nobody here has given — not is it better but is it better per dollar, which is only answerable if the harness records spend. Caveat in the same breath: the leading system belongs to the benchmark's authors, so the cost ratios, which favour systems they did not build, are the trustworthy half.

  • 2026-08-16 — a fourth subsetting rule, the only one available to a team that has run nothing, and it is the one that does not work. Predicting per-item difficulty from the task description alone reaches Spearman ρ = 0.399 in-distribution across 17 agentic benchmarks and "degraded significantly" out-of-distribution (predicting-item-difficulty-from-the-description-alone-reaches-spearman-0-399-and-degrades-out-of-distribution, predicting-task-difficulty-without-rollouts). Set it against the three already here — discrimination selection needs a response matrix and buys ranking fidelity, random-n needs nothing and buys an unbiased mean, trajectory clustering needs one pass and buys redundancy removal. This one needs no environment rollouts at all, which is exactly the cold-start case this theme identified as the one its reader is in, and at 0.399 it is not accurate enough to select a suite with. Recorded as a measured negative result, which this theme has been short of: three rules with published wins and one with a published ceiling is a more honest table, and the literature is selected against the fourth kind. One qualification worth carrying: "without rollouts" means without environment rollouts — the predictive signal is token-level entropy over a model's reasoning trace, so inference still runs on every item. In a stateful benchmark that is still most of the bill.

  • 2026-08-16 — and the same paper undercuts this theme's own strongest objection to its cheapest advice. item-response-theory-subsetting-has-a-cold-start-cost-in-historical-response-logs-that-a-single-team-cannot-pay says IRT subsetting needs a response matrix no single team can build; the objection assumed you would have to build one. A 1PL model fit over 415,470 agent-task outcomes across 17 benchmarks now exists (a-415470-outcome-response-matrix-across-17-benchmarks-exists-so-the-irt-cold-start-is-an-availability-problem-rather-than-a-cost-one). For public benchmark items the blocker is now a publishing decision rather than a capability gap, and released item parameters would make the 97–99% cost cut a sixth kind of adoptable artifact. Two things keep this from closing the gap. No release is stated in the paper. And it does nothing for a team scoring its own product on its own items unless parameters transfer across item pools — on which the same paper's out-of-distribution degradation is the only evidence, and it points the wrong way. The atom is marked rebutted-by; the request to any team fitting one of these is one line: publish the item parameters, not just the paper.

  • 2026-08-16 — the first price on a judge configuration, fifty-seven sources in, and it removes a trade rather than settling one. A panel of smaller judges outperformed a single frontier judge on correlation at one-seventh the cost (a-panel-of-small-judges-beat-a-single-large-one-at-one-seventh-the-cost-which-is-the-first-price-on-a-judge-configuration, eugeneyan-effectiveness-of-llm-evaluators). Six judge configurations are catalogued in this vault and every one is argued on correctness alone, while judge count multiplies the bill on every item of every run — judge-configuration-is-an-unpriced-construction-parameter-with-two-benchmarks-choosing-opposite-defaults has stood unanswered since 2026-08-07 for exactly that reason. If a cheap panel is both better-correlated and 7× cheaper, the expensive single judge retains only operational simplicity, which is worth something and not worth 7×. It composes badly with two-judge-backbones-produce-opposite-conservative-and-permissive-failure-profiles-so-a-mixed-panel-averages-to-a-rate-matching-neither though: a diverse small-model panel is the configuration most likely to mix opposed biases, so it may be buying correlation by cancelling errors that should have been reported separately. Weak evidence — a survey's secondhand summary of one paper, older model generation, and "one-seventh" is a token-price ratio with no absolute base behind it. Still the fourth cost-bearing item here and the second to give a ratio, and unlike almost all the others it prices measurement machinery rather than the system under test.

  • 2026-08-17 — the first method here that cuts the label budget instead of the item count, which is the line this theme's own open question names as untouched. "A stronger teacher model provides sparsely sampled corrections to a smaller student with a continual learning harness. We score a harness by how much its student converges toward the teacher over time," with teacher-relative lift shown to correlate with lift against a held-out gold standard across security tasks, model families and harness designs (scoring-a-harness-by-how-far-its-student-converges-toward-a-stronger-teacher-removes-the-label-budget-rather-than-the-item-count, label-free-harness-evaluation-teacher-relative-lift). Four subsetting rules, a trial-count dial and a judge-panel price all reduce inference and all assume the labels exist; the standing question where does this collide with the expensive-metric problem, where the cost is in labelling rather than in inference now has an answer, and the answer is to substitute a stronger model's correction for the label. Read it on eligibility, which is how this theme learned to read the other four: this one needs a model strictly stronger than the one you are running, which is the ordinary deployment shape, and it is unavailable at the frontier where there is no stronger teacher. What you buy is a comparison and not a level — which is usually the question, since the paper's motivating complaint is that a practitioner "cannot tell whether a given harness helps at all or which of two is better." One structural caveat that matters: the validation needed a held-out gold standard, so the method has been checked only where labels existed, and in the regime it is designed for that check cannot be run. Same shape as the difficulty-prediction ceiling in predicting-item-difficulty-from-the-description-alone-reaches-spearman-0-399-and-degrades-out-of-distribution — in-distribution performance that has not been shown to survive the move out. One domain, correlation reported with no coefficient, no task counts, no model names.

  • 2026-08-17 — the label counts scattered across five sources turn out to be three jobs rather than five estimates, and the entry price is an order of magnitude below what this theme has been quoting. ~30 examples with a saturation stop (thirty-examples-with-a-saturation-rule-is-the-smallest-judge-dataset-here-and-it-sorts-four-conflicting-label-counts-into-three-jobs, hamel-using-llm-as-a-judge-complete-guide) and 20 labels to unlock scoring, 50 to unlock fitting, 50–100 before criteria are worth writing (a-graduated-label-ladder-of-twenty-to-unlock-scoring-and-fifty-to-unlock-fitting-puts-the-first-useful-judge-state-at-a-few-dozen-labels, eugeneyan-aligneval-building-an-eval-app), against the 100+ in an-llm-judge-costs-a-hundred-labels-plus-weekly-maintenance-so-it-is-a-dependency-rather-than-an-artifact and the 200+ with a 25% holdout in holding-out-25-percent-and-reporting-precision-recall-and-kappa-is-the-first-stated-acceptance-procedure-for-a-judge. Sorted: ~30 to discover what the judge should look for, ~100 to build it, ~200 with a holdout to accept it. That matters for this theme specifically because the reason teams never start is that the stated entry fee reads like a week of labelling, and the first useful state is an afternoon. The gate framing is the more transferable half — AlignEval states what each increment unlocks rather than what you should reach, and it puts criteria after the first fifty labels, because labelling is how the criteria get discovered. Both are practitioner product decisions rather than measurements: nothing shows 20 is sufficient or 50 necessary, and the same author reports his own 25-per-split optimisation as too small to trust.

Status note, 2026-08-09 — held at emerging, with the theme's own question answered on the wrong axis

Three arrivals on cost in one night, and the status does not move. The reason is the same discipline this vault applied when it walked build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling back from consolidating: the named object, or nothing.

The object this theme named was "Does a random subset preserve rankings as well as a discrimination-selected one, on a suite of a few hundred items?" What arrived answers a nearby question — selected beats random on score-recovery error, on eight large public safety benchmarks. Two of three qualifiers are unmet. Ranking is the property random-n was always suspected on, since its known virtue is an unbiased mean and its known silence is about order. And the scale is again the benchmark-maintainer scale, not the application scale where the theme claims its value.

What did genuinely change is the evidence base under the opening finding. It was one paper, one team, one benchmark family. It is now two independent teams at the same order of magnitude — still inside the safety-benchmark family, whose heterogeneous adversarial item pools are the condition that makes selection pay. Two studies of the easy case.

Against that, the night also produced the first real cost of the cost method. The cold-start requirement is not a caveat on a finding, it is an eligibility rule, and it excludes the reader this theme is written for. A theme cannot firm up on the same night its central recommendation is shown to be unavailable to most of its audience.

The useful reframing to carry forward: there are now three subsetting rules and they are not competitors on one axis. Discrimination selection needs a response matrix and buys ranking fidelity. Random-n needs nothing and buys an unbiased mean. CoT clustering needs one pass over the item pool and buys redundancy removal. The question a team should ask is not which is best but which one it is eligible for, and that is decided by what data it already has rather than by what it wants.

Why this is its own theme

It is orthogonal to the other three rather than nested under any of them. build-vs-adopt-eval-tooling asks who writes the runner; this asks how many items the runner should execute, and the answer is the same whoever wrote it. evals-that-localize-the-fix asks what a score points at; this asks what the score cost to produce. And design-for-verifiability is about the artifact being measured, not the size of the measurement.

It also earns a theme because it is the first thread here with a lever big enough to change behaviour immediately. An 80% cost reduction that preserves rankings is not a refinement — it is the difference between an eval suite that runs on every change and one that runs when someone remembers.

The evidence is thin and one-sided so far: a single paper, demonstrated entirely on safety benchmarks, whose heterogeneous adversarial item pools are precisely the condition that makes item selection pay. Nothing here has tested a hand-built task-specific suite, which is what an application team actually maintains.

Open questions

  • Does teacher-relative lift rank two harnesses the same way a labelled benchmark does, in a setting where nobody has the labels? The correlation was validated where a gold standard existed, which is not the regime the method is for, and no coefficient is published even there.
  • How many correction rounds before a convergence-based harness ranking stabilises? "Sparsely sampled" is what makes the method cheap and what makes an early reading noisy, and experience-reuse-can-help-or-mislead-later-decisions-so-carrying-history-between-tasks-is-a-parameter-with-an-uncontrolled-sign says a truncated horizon can see a different sign rather than a smaller effect.
  • What does it cost to run the measurement? Two sources in thirty-eight now report tokens beside a score and both are pricing the system under test. Nobody reports the eval's own bill — inference for the graded runs, judge calls, human review time — which is the quantity this theme is named after.
  • Does the sizing arithmetic survive contact with a real suite? 200 samples at ±2.4 points assumes independent samples and a stable defect rate, and an eval suite drawn from clustered real failures satisfies neither. Somebody needs to report the observed variance across reruns of the same suite.
  • On a fixed budget, is a suite better spent on more items or more trials per item? Item selection and trial count trade against each other — one buys coverage, the other buys the ability to see consistency — and no source here has run the comparison or even framed it as a choice.
  • Does a hand-built application eval of a few hundred cases also carry 80% dead weight, or is that a property of large scraped benchmark suites?
  • How long does a fixed informative subset stay informative? As models improve past the items that discriminated, the subset quietly stops separating them, and nothing here says how often to re-extract.
  • Does cheapening the rerun change what teams measure, or only what they spend? The interesting version of this theme is whether a cheap suite gets run on changes that currently ship unevaluated.
  • Where does this collide with the expensive-metric problem — cumulative retrieval recall, human relevance labels, transcript audits — where the cost is in labelling rather than in inference?
  • What is the marginal value of the second and third judge? This is the item-selection question asked about the judging axis instead of the item axis, and it has the same shape: most of the spend may be buying agreement that was never in doubt. Four published configurations now (an-item-response-model-separating-agent-quality-from-judge-severity-is-a-fourth-panel-estimator-that-neither-votes-nor-averages), none reporting an agreement rate.
  • Can judge severity be corrected for after the fact cheaply enough to justify a smaller panel? If a severity model recovers what a three-judge panel buys from a two-judge one, the correction is the cheapest thing in this theme — it moves spend from inference into arithmetic. Nobody has tried it, and the method as published needs many agents scored by the same judges to identify the parameters, which a single team does not have.
  • Does a random subset preserve rankings as well as a discrimination-selected one — answered on score error, 2026-08-09, and still open on ranking. Selected recovers full-benchmark scores with lower error than random at the same size, across eight safety benchmarks and 192 models (discrimination-selected-items-beat-random-subsets-of-the-same-size-which-settles-half-of-the-comparison-between-the-two-cheap-subsetting-rules). Nobody has reported the same head-to-head as a rank correlation, and nobody has run it on a suite of a few hundred items. Both remain open, and the second is the one that decides whether any of this reaches an application team.
  • What is the smallest response matrix — how many models × how many items — from which item parameters recover the full-suite ranking? "Massive historical logs" is the stated blocker and the word is doing all the work. If the honest answer is twenty models, the cold-start objection mostly dissolves; if it is two hundred, item selection is permanently a benchmark-maintainer's tool.
  • Which of the three subsetting rules is a given team eligible for? This is the version of the comparison question that a team can act on, and it turns on what data they already have rather than on which method is best.
  • Does clustering by reasoning trajectory find redundancy in a hand-built product suite, where items differ by domain fact, input format, or customer segment rather than by reasoning structure? The method's own authors gate its efficacy on task complexity, and most product suites are on the wrong side of that gate.
  • What is the shortest horizon that still surfaces a regression or a forgetting event? Every long-horizon design so far picked a length by intuition.
  • Does an enforced budget — a hard cap at the harness, with running totals visible to the agent — move behaviour where a stated one does not? The negative result here is about instruction, and the obvious repair has not been tested.
  • Almost nothing in this vault reports what a run cost alongside what it scored, which is why a 15-point harness gap cannot be priced. drawai-image-to-editable-reconstruction reports cost variation across thirteen models and five harnesses and is the exception; why is that not standard, and what would it take to make comparisons cost-normalized by default?
