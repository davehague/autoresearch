Build vs adopt eval tooling

Status: emerging

  • 2026-08-07 — the choice finally gets a magnitude, and it is on the harness rather than the eval framework: six frontier backbones against five widely used agent harnesses on 410 deterministically graded tasks, and with the backbone fixed the harness was worth 15.36 points (harness-choice-moved-agent-accuracy-15-points-with-the-model-backbone-held-fixed, dataspace-verifiable-analytics-benchmark). Adopting a harness is an accuracy decision, not a convenience one — and a harness comparison belongs inside the evaluation rather than downstream of it.

  • 2026-08-07 — the same benchmark also sharpens where the build line falls. Its deterministic grader is three named tolerance rules, and no framework can supply them because they encode what counts as the same answer in your domain (a-deterministic-grader-for-tabular-answers-is-three-named-normalizations-not-an-equality-check). A team estimating build cost should be pricing the grader's tolerances, not the runner. Against that, the build side gains its first argument for durability: a long-horizon harness carried across five backends from three model families untuned, which is what an investment that amortizes looks like (a-single-harness-carried-across-five-backends-from-three-model-families-without-per-model-tuning, onedayagent-long-horizon-harness). Weak evidence — a system paper's secondary claim, no per-backend numbers — and still the first thing on the build side that is not a cost.

  • 2026-08-07 — moved emerging → consolidating on the first run, then reverted to emerging the same day (David's call, on review). The move is recorded here rather than erased, because the reason it was wrong is the more useful artifact. See below.

  • 2026-08-07 — the deterministic-grader leg finally gets its transfer test, in a domain the vault flagged as the one that would break it. Tutoring has no crisp success condition; the skeleton survives with two substitutions — the grader replaced by a simulated learner whose mastery state comes from a knowledge-tracing model fit to real student data, and the taste axes handed to a cross-family panel of three judges rather than one (where-ground-truth-is-contested-the-construction-move-is-a-simulated-counterpart-fit-to-real-data-plus-a-cross-family-judge-panel-on-the-soft-axes, educlaw-bench-pedagogical-agents-simulated-learners). The transferable rule is the split — deterministic where you can, panel where you cannot, never one judge — and every expensive part of it is task-specific, which is the sharpest picture yet of what the build column actually contains.

  • 2026-08-07 — the comparison an adopting team actually needs, demonstrated: two published memory systems (MemGPT/Letta, HippoRAG) evaluated inside the evaluator's harness alongside five hand-rolled strategies, rather than each in its own (agentmembench-memory-strategy-comparison, swapping-one-component-under-a-fixed-harness-model-and-decoding-attributes-the-difference-to-that-component). The adopt question is never "is this published system good," it is "is it better than the two hundred lines I would otherwise write," and that only becomes answerable when the adopted thing is dropped into your harness.

  • 2026-08-07 — and a knob nobody has priced, sitting right on the build/adopt line. Two benchmarks days apart chose opposite judge configurations — one model serving as its own judge, versus a cross-family panel of three applied only to the taste axes — and neither justified the choice or reported what it bought (judge-configuration-is-an-unpriced-construction-parameter-with-two-benchmarks-choosing-opposite-defaults). The rubric is task-specific, but judge count and family selection are generic and are exactly what a framework could supply well, if anyone knew the right default.

  • 2026-08-07 — the first mechanism that makes the build side scale, which is a different thing from making it cheap. 84 executable tools built once, then a generator composing them into 10,400 tasks whose ground truth is whatever executing the composition returns (building-the-executable-tool-layer-first-lets-a-generator-produce-ten-thousand-tasks-whose-ground-truth-comes-from-execution, gabench-agentic-graph-analysis). Every other corpus in this vault sits between 50 and 410 tasks, and the entire difference is whether a human has to state the answer. Where execution states it, corpus size is nearly free once the substrate exists — and the substrate is the build item, as domain-specific as DataSpace's tolerance rules. The build column keeps turning out to be whatever encodes what your domain means.

  • 2026-08-07 — and the third adopt-side artifact pointing at plumbing, this one from governance rather than eval tooling: an open-source harness for auditable agent evaluation, shipping authorization, monitoring and audit surface under a readiness index (proofagent-index-capability-is-not-readiness, a-composite-over-non-substitutable-release-evidence-describes-no-real-state-so-the-dimensions-have-to-stay-un-averaged). Single-author framework paper validating its own index with its own tool — this vault has already ruled that stacking artifacts like this counts as one observation about roadmaps, not three about outcomes. It widens the picture of what the adopt column contains and moves nothing.

  • 2026-08-08 — the build column finally gets a number, and it is the wrong number honestly reported. A corpus of 1,151 tasks was validated by three annotators over 200 sampled tasks against six criteria — all pass by majority, 192 of 200 unanimous on every criterion (a-three-annotator-audit-of-200-sampled-tasks-against-six-criteria-is-the-first-countable-human-labour-line-item-on-the-build-side, worksurface-bench-multi-surface-knowledge-routing). Twenty-three sources in, this is the first construction cost stated in countable units rather than described as an activity. It is not hours, and it prices validating a corpus rather than creating one. The transferable part is the sample rate: a 17% sample with three annotators was enough to report corpus quality as a number, which turns construction validation into a bounded task instead of an open-ended one.

  • 2026-08-08 — and the same source shows what the grader costs when the domain is not one domain. Three surfaces, three non-interchangeable oracles: executed DuckDB queries for tables, verified text spans for documents, traced dependency annotations for graphs (one-oracle-per-data-surface-is-what-auditable-ground-truth-costs-in-a-heterogeneous-domain). The substrate fork this theme wrote last night — execution if your domain computes its answers, modelled counterpart if it does not — has no branch for a domain sitting on both sides at once, and the answer is that grader cost scales with the number of surfaces rather than the number of tasks. A team estimating a grader for a workspace product should be counting surfaces.

  • 2026-08-08 — a fifth kind of adoptable artifact, and the one with the worst lock-in. Not a runner, not a metric library, not governance plumbing, not a task corpus, but an environment: corpus construction, retrieval indexes, a three-tool contract, and an evaluation protocol as one runnable unit (a-runnable-reference-environment-is-a-third-kind-of-adoptable-artifact-alongside-runners-and-task-corpora, simplewikisearch-specified-offline-search-environment). A runner executes your tasks against your world; an environment is the world. The trade is stark and inseparable: you cannot get comparability with published numbers without taking on someone else's chunking policy and observation format, because those are the same property. Useful reframing — the fork is about purpose, not specificity: adopt an environment to compare against published results, build one to measure your own product, and do not expect one artifact to do both.

  • 2026-08-08 — the unpriced judge knob picks up a third default and its first stated resolution rule. Three judges with majority voting, applied across the scored axes, which alongside self-judging and a taste-axes-only panel of three makes three papers, three answers, one month (a-third-judge-configuration-in-one-month-three-judges-with-majority-voting-on-every-axis-widens-an-already-unjustified-range, stealthbench-operational-stealth-in-offensive-security-agents). Three arrivals rules out the charitable reading that the field has a convention and this vault caught two outliers. The new information is the rule: majority voting is a gate, and neither prior source says whether its judges vote or average — so "three judges" in the literature covers two different estimators.

  • 2026-08-09 — a sixth kind of adoptable artifact, and it is not measurement at all. Inspect AI extended approval policies to the tool calls of bridged agents: a rejected call never runs, and the model is told to try something else rather than handed an error (approval-policies-over-a-bridged-agents-tool-calls-move-the-adopt-column-from-running-the-eval-to-governing-what-the-agent-may-do-inside-it, inspect-ai-changelog-0-3-253-to-0-3-254). Everything the adopt column has offered so far either runs the eval or records it. This governs the system under test. The bridged-agent qualifier is the substance: a gate over tools you declared yourself is a gate over code you wrote, while a gate that also covers an off-the-shelf agent you wrapped covers the surface you did not write, which is where the unanticipated calls come from. Expensive, generic, safety-relevant — the worst combination to hand-roll. And the lock-in note is sharper than the trace-schema version: a gate that lives in the runner is lost when you leave the runner, and you find out on the day something destructive executes.

  • 2026-08-09 — the substrate fork gains a third branch, from the largest generated corpus this vault has seen. 2,000 public skills → 4,000 task packages → 27,164 verified trajectories, kept or discarded by rule-based checks plus agent-based checks with feedback-guided repair, and a coverage criterion requiring each trajectory to substantially exercise every skill its task names (a-two-tier-verifier-with-feedback-guided-repair-is-the-build-item-that-makes-a-generated-task-corpus-keepable-at-thousands-of-packages, skt-verified-synthetic-task-generation). GABench's generation worked because execution states the answer; this one never computes an answer at all. Execution asks what is correct; a verifier asks is this instance worth having, and the second question is answerable in far more domains than the first. So the transferable rule sharpens again: for a generated corpus the expensive build item is the acceptance test, not the generator — and writing down what makes an instance acceptable is a much smaller job than writing the instances, reusable on every future batch.

  • 2026-08-09 — and the cost of generating your own corpus, which is not a cost in hours. SkillEval is spun out of the same pipeline that produced the training data, from a disjoint pool of source skills (building-the-eval-from-the-same-generator-as-the-training-data-makes-generator-coverage-the-ceiling-on-what-either-can-find). Disjoint inputs are a real precaution and are not an independent construction: the generator's composition rules are identical on both sides, so whatever it cannot imagine is missing from the corpus and from the benchmark at once. That reshapes this theme's standing question about generated tasks. The worry was never that they are easy — a verifier enforces difficulty. It is that they are easy exactly where the generator is blind, and volume, verification and pool disjointness all run downstream of the same imagination. The cheap countermeasure, which nobody reports doing: keep a small hand-curated holdout authored by someone who did not write the generator, and check that model rankings agree across the two.

  • 2026-08-10 — the build side gets a size and a calendar, from the first source here written for someone building an eval rather than publishing one. A team gets "surprisingly far" on 20–50 simple tasks drawn from real failures, and a named team built an eval system spanning static analysis, browser agents and judges within three months of launch (twenty-to-fifty-tasks-drawn-from-real-failures-is-the-first-stated-starting-size-for-an-eval-suite-and-it-is-an-order-of-magnitude-below-the-benchmark-corpora, anthropic-demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents). Neither is hours and this theme should keep saying so. What they do supply is the correction that thirty-three benchmark-shaped sources have been quietly making necessary: every corpus here runs 50 to 410 tasks because it is a product built to rank many systems, while a team's suite is an instrument built to detect change in one, and the second job needs an order of magnitude fewer items. The qualifier carries as much as the number — a suite drawn from real failures has no generator, so it cannot be blind where a generator is blind, and it is the one construction route needing no substrate, no verifier and no modelled counterpart. Its precondition is a shipped product, which is why the same guide says start early: the failures are perishable. Vendor guide, no methodology, secondhand on the three-month figure.

  • 2026-08-10 — a seventh kind of adoptable artifact, and the dullest one yet: an adapter contract. A task protocol carrying instruction, state, expected actions and outputs, plus a per-harness binding supplying tool schemas, execution and prompt construction — nine harnesses against 23 benchmarks with no per-combination integration code (a-task-protocol-with-per-harness-bindings-removes-the-per-combination-integration-code-that-makes-a-cross-harness-comparison-unaffordable, a2e-agent-auditing-engine). Its value is arithmetic: N harnesses against M suites is N×M pieces of work by hand and N+M with a contract, which is why every cross-harness comparison in this vault covers two or five and this one covers nine. That reframes a standing puzzle in evals-that-localize-the-fix — the sweep nobody runs may be missing for a duller reason than incentives, namely that the integration code is the expensive part. Same lock-in caveat as every schema here, milder than an environment's and not zero. And no hours: "no integration code written" is the authors' description of their own design, not a measured cost.

  • 2026-08-10 — the OSS side does the cheapest possible thing about an unwritten variable: change the default. Inspect AI now denies network egress to the agent under evaluation unless the eval grants it, including the smuggled forms — a URL inside image or document content (defaulting-network-egress-to-deny-in-the-runner-converts-an-unwritten-environment-parameter-into-one-the-eval-must-declare, inspect-ai-changelog-0-3-255). Egress belongs to the class named in seven-unreported-environment-parameters-sit-underneath-every-agentic-search-result-so-the-environment-is-a-fourth-term-beside-model-harness-and-inference-settings and is worse than the examples, because it is not chosen at all — it is whatever the machine allowed. Every other proposal for surfacing hidden variables is a reporting norm needing everyone's cooperation; a deny-by-default needs nobody's, because the grant ends up in the config file. Third Inspect changelog in five days, so it counts for less rather than more, and the comparability point is larger than the security one: an agent with live web access is scored partly on what the web contained that day, and not one source in this vault states whether its agents had egress.

  • 2026-08-12 — the buyer's side of the split, from two practitioners who have nothing to sell, and it lands where the vendor changelogs said it would. One states it outright: "I tend to use these tools as a backend data store and use Jupyter notebooks as well as my own custom built annotation interfaces for most of my needs" (two-independent-practitioners-adopt-the-platform-as-a-store-and-build-the-annotation-layer-themselves, hamel-selecting-the-right-ai-evals-tool). The other describes the same arrangement without naming it — own labels, own judge alignment, own experiment harness, no platform in the account at all (eugeneyan-product-evals-in-three-steps). This theme derived its division by reading what vendors shipped and then correctly discounted it as "a fact about vendor roadmaps, not about outcomes." Here is the same line drawn by people buying rather than selling. It also relocates the line more precisely: the changelog reading put the split at specificity, generic plumbing versus domain logic. The practitioner version puts it at the human interface — the boundary is wherever a person has to look at a failure and decide something. Annotation UIs are not domain-specific in any technical sense and both built one anyway, because ill-fitting friction there is paid on every trace. Two consultants with unusual notebook fluency is suggestive and not more; what would strengthen it is a team without a data-science bench reporting the same split, or reporting that the vendor's annotation UI was fine.

  • 2026-08-12 — and the same review's four selection criteria contain nothing about metrics. Workflow friction between a failing trace and a playground; first-class manual annotation, because "error analysis is the highest ROI activity in AI engineering"; resistance to automation-without-validation; export over walled gardens. The entire analytical surface that oss-eval-frameworks-are-shifting-to-deterministic-trajectory-metrics-so-the-adopt-case-rests-on-trace-schema-and-integrations-rather-than-on-the-metrics predicted was commoditizing does not appear in a buyer's criteria at all. This theme has been framing build-vs-adopt as a cost comparison with an unpriced build column; on this account it is not a cost comparison — it is a question about whose error-analysis workflow you are going to live inside, and the post contains no cost figure of any kind. The standing request for hours should probably be re-pointed: not "what did it cost" but "what did you keep doing by hand after you bought the tool."

  • 2026-08-12 — the named anti-pattern on the adopt side, which is automated-eval-platforms-recall-74-87-of-expert-labeled-failures-but-systematically-miss-the-ones-requiring-domain-taste-so-adopting-one-buys-breadth-rather-than-judgment with a mechanism instead of a recall statistic. A feature that generates evaluation criteria and immediately scores against them "often hides flaws behind a high score" (a-tool-that-writes-the-rubric-and-then-scores-against-it-removes-the-only-step-where-a-human-would-notice-the-rubric-was-wrong). The loop is closed on itself: the criterion is never seen by anyone who knows the domain, and the score is high because the criterion was drawn from the same distribution as the output. The score direction is the trap — a broken grader that scores low eventually gets investigated, and one that scores high never does. It does not argue against automating error-analysis labour (parallel-error-analysis-agents-over-stored-traces-is-the-first-report-of-the-transcript-audit-obligation-being-paid-by-machine); it argues against automating the judgment about what counts as a failure. The checkable question for anyone evaluating a platform: where did the criteria come from, and who read them before the first score.

  • 2026-08-12 — the build side gets a fifth figure, and for the first time it covers the whole build as one number: ~4 weeks at one team for criteria, human annotations, judge alignment and the experiment harness, after which they ran dozens of experiments in two weeks and hundreds over the following months (four-weeks-to-a-working-eval-harness-is-the-first-build-figure-covering-criteria-labels-judge-alignment-and-runner-as-one-number). Still not hours, still an anecdote, no team size, no breakdown. What it settles is the order of magnitude by being the second independent arrival at it — Bolt's three months for a full system, this team's four weeks for a working harness, both practitioners describing real teams, both weeks-to-months. And the sequencing inside those four weeks is the transferable part: the time goes into labels and judge alignment, and the runner is the last and smallest piece, which is the opposite of where a team reading the benchmark literature would start.

  • 2026-08-12 — an adopt-side argument this theme did not have, from a security disclosure. Inspect AI's math() scorer was parsing model output in a way that could execute Python on the evaluator host, now fixed with a non-evaluating grammar in a bounded thread (the-math-scorer-was-executing-model-output-on-the-evaluator-host-so-the-grader-is-an-untrusted-input-surface, inspect-ai-changelog-0-3-256-to-0-3-257). Every sandbox story in this vault contains the agent's actions; nobody has asked what the scoring code does with the agent's text, and an agentic eval's input source is a system selected for finding unanticipated routes. The general shape covers anything that evaluates, compiles, deserializes or interpolates model output — including every LLM judge here, where injection runs through the graded artifact into the grading prompt. This is the strongest single argument for adopting a maintained runner recorded so far: the bug was found, fixed and published, and a hand-rolled eval(answer) gets none of the three. The same changelog also discloses that unseeded shuffles gave irreproducible item ids and "silently corrupted eval_set retries" — same organ, same silence, a plausible number either way.

  • 2026-08-12 — and the judge knob gets an instrument rather than a sixth default: krippendorff_alpha(), inter-rater agreement across multiple judges on nominal, ordinal or interval scales, shipping in the runner (an-inter-rater-agreement-metric-in-the-runner-turns-judge-agreement-from-a-reporting-norm-into-a-one-line-call). Five configurations catalogued, none reporting an agreement rate; this removes the last practical excuse the same way the egress default did, by putting the calculation where the evals already run. Alpha rather than kappa is the right call — three-plus raters, missing ratings, ordinal rubrics — and it measures agreement, not correctness, so a panel agreeing at 0.9 on a rubric nobody checked is a well-calibrated instrument pointed at the wrong thing. Fourth Inspect changelog in six days; the one-vendor discount stands.

Why the status was moved and then walked back

The move's reasoning: opened on a single study saying accuracy is roughly a wash between platforms and general coding agents, so the decision turns on workflow and integration. Three further sources arrived by independent routes — a survey of academic benchmark construction, and two OSS changelogs — and none contradicted it. They appeared to resolve the question into a division rather than a contest: the task-specific parts (target, difficulty dial, grader, domain taste) stay yours, and the non-task-specific parts (run control, trace schema, framework adapters, audit trail) are what adopting buys.

Why that does not earn consolidating:

  1. Two of the four sources are vendor changelogs. A changelog establishes what a vendor shipped. It is not independent evidence that the thing works, and treating "Inspect shipped pause/resume" and "DeepEval shipped trajectory metrics" as two corroborating arrivals double-counts a single observation: the adopt side is investing in plumbing. That is a fact about vendor roadmaps, not about outcomes.
  2. The build column is entirely unpriced. This is a two-sided question and every source so far measures one side. Nothing here has measured what building costs — not in hours, not in maintenance, not in the switching cost of a hand-rolled trace schema. A picture cannot firm up while half of it is missing.
  3. Non-contradiction is not confirmation. Three sources failing to contradict an opening read is weak evidence when none of them set out to test it.

Status is therefore back at emerging pending something that prices the build side, or an independent (non-vendor) measurement of the adopt side.

Status note, 2026-08-07 (evening) — held, and the build side is now shaped rather than priced

GABench is the closest this theme has come to the missing half, and it is worth being precise about what it supplies. It shows that build cost is not linear in corpus size when the domain can compute its own answers: pay once for an executable substrate, then generate tasks indefinitely. That is a structural fact about the build column and a genuinely new one. It is not a price. Nobody reports what the 84 tools cost in hours, nor whether generated tasks discriminate as well as curated ones — volume is not signal, and a generator inherits its composition rules' blind spots.

So the build column now has a shape — substrate, tolerance rules, difficulty dial, judge configuration — and not one line item with a number against it. Eighteen sources in. Held at emerging.

A useful reframing did arrive with it. The build decision forks on a domain property rather than on team preference: if your domain has a programmable substrate that computes right answers, generate from execution; if it does not, fit a modelled counterpart and panel the soft axes. Graphs, databases, compilers and test suites are on one side; tutoring is on the other. That is a question a team can answer about itself in an afternoon, which is more actionable than anything else this theme has produced.

Status note, 2026-08-08 — held at emerging, with the first number on the board and the fork amended

Twenty-three sources, and the build column has exactly one countable figure against it: 3 annotators × 200 tasks × 6 criteria, and that prices validation rather than construction. Holding at emerging is not a close call.

Two things did change, and both are structural rather than evidential.

The fork acquired a third branch. "Execution or modelled counterpart" assumed a domain sits on one side. WorkSurface-Bench's three surfaces sit on three sides — one computes its answers, one can only be grounded in evidence, one has pre-existing annotations to trace to — and the practical consequence is a cost model nobody had: grader cost scales with surface count, not task count. That is the second question a team can answer about itself without running anything, after the substrate question, and it is the one that determines whether the grader is a week or a quarter.

The adopt column acquired its most consequential entry and its worst trade. An environment is the highest-value thing on offer for comparability and the deepest coupling available, and the two are the same property rather than a pro and a con to weigh. This theme's long-running open question about trace-schema lock-in now has a strictly worse version: a trace schema constrains how you record runs, an environment constrains what the agent can see and do.

What is still missing is what has been missing since the theme opened, and the phrasing should stay blunt: hours. Nobody has stated what an eval cost them to build against what adopting one would have cost. A judgment count is progress toward that and is not it.

Status note, 2026-08-09 — held at emerging, twenty-eight sources, still no hours

Nothing tonight prices the build side, so nothing moves. Two structural additions and one correction of emphasis.

The adopt column now contains six kinds of thing: runners, metric libraries, governance plumbing, task corpora, environments, and — as of tonight — a control plane over the agent under test. The trend across four consecutive nights of OSS releases is unbroken and worth naming plainly, because it is the whole adopt-side case: the vendors are building everything that is not domain-specific, and doing it faster than a team could. That remains a fact about roadmaps rather than about outcomes, and this is the second Inspect changelog in five days, which counts for less rather than more.

The build column gained its second scaling mechanism, structurally different from the first. GABench's route requires a domain that computes its own answers. SKT's requires only a domain where you can check an artifact you cannot compute — a far larger set. Both say the same thing about cost shape: a fixed price in the substrate or the verifier, then near-zero marginal cost per task. Neither states the fixed price. Twenty-eight sources in, every one describes construction as an activity rather than counting it.

The correction of emphasis is on generated corpora. This theme has treated generation as a straightforward win for the build side — cheaper at scale, ground truth for free. Tonight adds the bill: a generated eval inherits the generator's blind spots, and no amount of verification finds them, because verification checks the instances that exist. A team generating its own suite is buying volume and taking on an unmeasured coverage risk, and the only available check is a small human-authored holdout scored beside it.

Status note, 2026-08-10 — held at emerging, and the build column is priced in the wrong units for the first time rather than not at all

Thirty-three sources. Still no hours, so nothing moves. But the shape of what is missing changed tonight, and it is worth naming precisely, because "no price" has been this theme's whole blocker since the first run and it is now closer to "no conversion."

Two figures arrived: 20–50 tasks for a first suite, and three months to a full eval system at one named team. Both are secondhand assertions in a vendor guide. Both are the kind of number a practitioner states casually and a paper never states at all — which is the sampling problem this vault has been flagging about its own intake, showing up as an actual gap in the record rather than as a worry. The intake has been arXiv benchmark papers plus one actively released harness, and benchmark authors report construction while never reporting cost. The fix is not more arXiv.

The starting-size figure also does something the theme did not expect: it makes most of the corpora here irrelevant as reference points. This theme has implicitly been asking what it costs to build something like WorkSurface-Bench's 1,151 tasks or GABench's 10,400. A team does not need those. They are products for ranking many systems across a wide ability range; an application suite is an instrument for detecting change in one system, and 20–50 items drawn from things that already broke is the stated bar. If that is right, the build-versus-adopt question has been asked at the wrong scale for its own audience, and the expensive parts this theme catalogued — substrate, generator, verifier, oracle-per-surface — are what you pay to build a benchmark, not what you pay to build an eval.

Held at emerging, and the standing request narrows: not "what does an eval cost to build" but what did your first twenty tasks cost, and what did they catch?

Status note, 2026-08-12 — held at emerging, and the theme's own bar is the reason

This was the closest call since the walk-back. The bar set on 2026-08-07 has two clauses — something that prices the build side, or an independent (non-vendor) measurement of the adopt side — and tonight brought a candidate for each, from the two practitioner sources that are the most on-point material this theme has ever read.

Neither clears its clause, and the discipline that walked this theme back from consolidating on day one is the same discipline that applies now.

On the build clause. Four weeks for criteria, labels, judge alignment and a harness is an anecdote in a blog post. No team size, no breakdown, no statement of what the harness covered, and the payoff half is throughput — dozens of experiments in two weeks — rather than defects caught. It is the same evidence class as Bolt's three months, and two anecdotes of the same class do not become a measurement by being consistent with each other. What they do establish is the order of magnitude, which is worth having and is not what the clause asks for.

On the adopt clause. A comparative review of three platforms against four criteria, by a non-vendor with unusual exposure to the decision, is an assessment and not a measurement. The clause says measurement, deliberately, because the theme's whole opening problem was reasoning about outcomes from things that were not outcome evidence.

Three things did change, and two of them are worth more than a status letter.

The vendor-derived split now has buyer-side confirmation, and the line moved. For four runs this theme's central claim rested on reading changelogs — an inference about outcomes from evidence about roadmaps, flagged as such every time. Two practitioners independently describing the same arrangement is the first evidence of the other kind. And they put the boundary somewhere the changelog reading could not see: not at domain-specificity but at the human interface. Annotation and error-analysis views are generic by any technical measure and both practitioners built their own anyway. The prediction that follows is sharper and testable: expect to build wherever your eye lands, expect to adopt everything behind it.

The framing may have been wrong the whole time. The most on-point source in this vault contains no cost figure at all, and its four criteria never mention metrics, scorers, or anything analytical. If the people making this decision do not experience it as a spend question, then five runs of asking for hours have been asking a question the field does not answer because it is not the question being decided. The re-pointed request: what did you keep doing by hand after you bought the tool, and why?

And the adopt column gained its first argument from a security disclosure rather than a feature. A grader that parses model output is running untrusted input from a system selected for finding unanticipated routes. That bug was found, fixed and published in a maintained runner; the same line in a team's own scorer gets none of that. It is the strongest single pro-adopt argument here and it arrived from a changelog's Security section, which is not where this theme has been looking.

Open questions

  • What did you keep doing by hand after you bought the tool, and why? Two practitioners kept the annotation interface and the error-analysis view. A third case either confirms the boundary sits at the human interface or shows it is a habit of consultants who like notebooks.
  • Does anything in your scoring path evaluate, compile, deserialize, or interpolate model output — and is it isolated when it does? A structural read of the grader, not a run, and no source here reports having done it.
  • What did a first suite of twenty to fifty tasks cost in hours, and what did it catch that would otherwise have shipped? A single team answering that would be worth more to this theme than any benchmark paper it has read.
  • When I can change anything about how an agent runs, what do I measure to know whether it actually got better - and do I build that measurement or adopt one?
  • Does the deterministic-grader leg of the skeleton survive transfer out of domains with crisp success conditions? Answered 2026-08-07: it survives in altered form — a modelled counterpart supplies the ground truth the domain will not, and only the genuinely soft axes go to judges. Tested once, in a domain that happens to have a mature modelling tradition to fit the counterpart to; what happens without one is the follow-on question.
  • What is the actual switching cost of adopting a harness's trace schema, and when does it become the thing you cannot leave? The environment version of this question is strictly worse and now live: has any second team published results against someone else's released environment? That is what would make a reference setup a reference rather than an artifact.
  • What does a three-judge cross-family panel buy over one judge, or over one cross-family judge? Nobody has run the ablation, judge count multiplies the bill on every item, and as of 2026-08-08 the ablation needs a third dimension: the disagreement-resolution rule, because majority-vote-of-three and mean-of-three are different estimators that the literature reports identically.
  • Does grader cost scale with surface count the way one source suggests, and does a fourth surface — email, chat, source code — need a fourth oracle or reduce to one of the three?
  • Task corpora are now part of what adopting buys, not just runners — 112 tasks and 5,300+ tested units released at microsoft/Loopsbench (loopsbench-loop-engineering-benchmark). Does adopting someone else's task corpus carry the same lock-in as adopting their trace schema?
  • What does an executable tool substrate cost to build, and do generated tasks discriminate as well as curated ones? The first is the build price this theme has been asking for since it opened; the second decides whether the scale is real or just volume — and as of 2026-08-09 the second question has been re-pointed: not whether generated tasks are too easy, but whether they are blind where the generator is (building-the-eval-from-the-same-generator-as-the-training-data-makes-generator-coverage-the-ceiling-on-what-either-can-find). The experiment is a generated corpus and a curated one over the same capability, run against the same models, rankings reported side by side.
  • What fraction of generated candidates does a verifier reject? Without it, "verified" is a label rather than a measurement, and there is no way to tell a verifier doing real work from one rubber-stamping a generator that was already fine.
  • Now that a runner can gate the agent's tool calls, what else moves from build to adopt on the safety side — and what is the exit cost of a gate you did not write? A trace schema constrains how you record runs; an approval policy constrains what executes, and losing it is not a migration inconvenience.
  • Which side of the substrate fork is a given team on? The question is whether the domain will compute its own answers — and unlike everything else here, a team can answer it about themselves without running anything.
