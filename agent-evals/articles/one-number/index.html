The top five models by average score and the top five by weakest score share no models at all

Every evaluation ends with an arithmetic step nobody argues about. You have per-item results, or per-axis scores, or a panel of judges, and you combine them into the number that goes on the slide. Almost always by taking the mean, because that is what the tooling does.

A 2026 position paper took ten HELM scenarios and 54 frontier models and ranked them two ways: by mean score across the scenarios, and by weakest score across them. The top five under each rule are completely disjoint. Not reshuffled — disjoint. No model appears in both.

Position: Evaluation Scores Are Perishable Knowledge Claims (arXiv 2607.26191) reports this as one part of a broader argument. It is the cleanest available demonstration that the combining step is not a formatting decision.

Why a mean is the wrong default more often than it looks

The mean is correct when the components are substitutes — when being better at one thing genuinely compensates for being worse at another. That describes some evaluations. It does not describe most of them, and there are two other cases with different right answers.

Case two: the components pull against each other, and a one-sided policy can bank the ones it likes.

EcoAgent-Bench: Evaluating Economic Decision-Making in Budget-Constrained LLM Agents (arXiv 2608.05519) built a benchmark for agents that have to spend money wisely, then did something almost nobody does: it scripted the degenerate policies and scored them as controls. A policy that always escalates — no decision-making in it at all — posts a high micro-averaged success rate on a benchmark built to measure decision-making, because it wins every escalate-shaped task and the mean absorbs the ones it fails.

Their fix is to aggregate as the worse of the two opposing task families rather than as a mean over all items. Under that rule, micro strict success runs 3.9–24.0% while economic consistency tops out at 7.3%. The gap is exactly the one-sidedness the mean was hiding.

The control-policy trick is worth stealing on its own. It needs no labels, no judges, and no model calls — write the dumb policies, run them, and see what they score. If a policy with no intelligence in it lands mid-table, the metric is measuring task mix rather than capability.

Case three: some components are gates, and a failed gate is not offset by anything.

This was an argument until mid-2026, and then it got measured twice by unrelated teams within a fortnight.

AgentS4D: Benchmarking Runtime Risks across the Execution Lifecycle of LLM-Based Workspace Agents (arXiv 2607.27294) ran 6,560 agent runs across four harnesses and five model backends. 4,344 of them — 66.22% — were unsafe and completed their task anyway. Two thirds of that dataset would read as wins on a completion dashboard.

StealthBench: Measuring Operational Stealth in Autonomous Offensive-Security Agents (arXiv 2607.26314) scored a "reckless solve rate" beside plain success, catching runs that achieved the objective at a cost the objective never priced. No model exceeded 54% safe success.

Different teams, different task families, days apart, neither citing the other. Both found the same thing: an outcome score and a constraint score decouple, and folding them together puts an agent that finishes everything badly level with one that refuses everything.

The transferable version has nothing to do with security. For any agent that acts on the world, name the constraint and score violations as their own rate. Two named rates cost nothing over one, and they cannot be averaged into each other by accident.

Mean and minimum are the same operator at two settings

The most useful idea in the HELM paper is not the disjoint top-fives. It is that the mean and the weakest-link score are endpoints of a single operator family governed by one pessimism parameter. You are not choosing between three named cases and hoping you diagnosed yourself correctly. You are setting a dial and writing down where you set it.

That makes this a config value and a line in a methods section rather than a taxonomy exercise, which is a much lower bar for a team to clear.

Two honest discounts. The HELM result comes from a position paper making its own case, and weakest-link is the maximally pessimistic end of the range — showing that the two extremes disagree is weaker than showing a defensible middle setting reorders things. Whether a mid-range setting also reorders appears to be unmeasured. That is the experiment worth running, and it is a weekend of work against public leaderboards.

Two axes beat one score, in four unrelated domains

The alternative to picking a combining rule is not combining. Four benchmarks in unrelated fields independently declined to produce a single number.

DrawAI: Agentic Benchmark and Workflow for Making Raster Images Editable (arXiv 2608.00548) reports fidelity and editability separately because they trade off, and a combined quality score would be a weighting nobody could see or argue with.

AgentS4D and StealthBench, above, each report a constraint rate beside a success rate.

And CompanionBench: A Theory-Anchored, Real-World-Grounded Benchmark for AI Emotional Companionship (arXiv 2608.02046) does it in the domain where you would least expect it to be possible. Its stated complaint about prior work is that it "aggregate[s] empathy into one score." Its replacement is ten capabilities derived from 25 theories in psychology and counselling, reported un-averaged, plus a deterministic second axis: whether the agent earned deeper disclosure from the simulated user, which branches on the agent's own behaviour and needs no judge to score.

That last one matters because "our domain is too soft to decompose" is the standard defence of a single quality score. A benchmark in about the softest domain going decomposed to ten axes and then found a mechanically checkable eleventh. The move to copy is the search itself: look for the one thing in your soft domain that can be checked mechanically, and report it beside the rubric instead of inside it.

The obvious objection to ten axes is that a reader will just build their own composite in their head. True, and CompanionBench does not say what the recommended reading is. Un-averaged is the better default and it is not a complete answer.

Judges are not measurements

There is a fourth case that the three-way split above misses entirely.

When a panel of LLM judges scores an agent, each score is the agent's quality plus that judge's severity. Averaging over judges, or taking a majority vote, treats them as interchangeable instruments and carries the severity term straight through into the result.

CompanionBench fits an Item Response Theory model that estimates agent quality and judge severity as separate latents, over 28 agents scored by a deliberately cross-family panel. The panel is cross-family for a named reason — same-family favouritism, judges preferring outputs from their own model family — rather than as a stylistic preference, which is itself unusual; most benchmarks that use cross-family panels do not say why.

Correcting for a harsh judge and outvoting one are different operations and they produce different rankings. Worth being clear about the limits, though: the paper reports this as a construction choice and gives no figure for how far the rankings moved once severity was factored out, so treat it as a technique rather than a finding. And identifying severity parameters requires many agents scored by the same judges, which makes it available to whoever runs a leaderboard and not to a team scoring one system against a rubric.

The experiment that would settle it is cheap for anyone already running a panel: publish the leaderboard twice, pooled and severity-corrected, and report the rank changes.

The decision your harness already made for you

One line in the Inspect AI changelog for 8 August 2026: NaN score values are now preserved in evaluation logs rather than lost.

A sample the scorer could not score — grader crashed, judge refused, output unparseable, sandbox died — has three possible fates, and each asserts something different:

  • Coerce to zero. "Not measured" silently becomes "measured and failed." The mean drops, and flaky infrastructure looks like a model getting worse.
  • Drop the sample. The denominator changes silently. The mean rises.
  • Preserve it. Whoever computes the summary has to decide.

The bias is not symmetric. Coercion punishes flaky graders; dropping rewards them. And dropping is the more dangerous of the two, because the samples a grader fails on tend to be the same samples the agent struggles on — so the runs that vanish are not a random sample of the run set.

Which gives the cheapest integrity check available on any evaluation: compare the number of samples scored against the number submitted. None of the benchmarks above reports both.

What to actually do

Before choosing how to combine, decide what your components are to each other. Substitutes take a mean. Opposing groups that must all hold take a minimum, or a stated pessimism setting between the two. Gates stay outside the arithmetic entirely.

Write the degenerate policies and score them. Always-escalate, always-refuse, always-pick-the-first-option. It costs an afternoon, needs no labels, and if one of them places respectably your metric is measuring your task mix.

Name your constraints and score them as their own rate. Two thirds of AgentS4D's runs finished the task and tripped a safety signal. If your agent has side effects, that is two numbers, and they do not average.

If you use a judge panel, say how you resolve disagreement. Vote, mean, and severity-corrected are three different estimators that the literature currently reports with the same phrase — "three judges." A mean over judges reintroduces the entire problem above, one level down.

Check that your scored n equals your submitted n. If it does not, find out what your harness did with the difference before you quote the number.

None of this is expensive. The whole list is arithmetic and bookkeeping, and on one public leaderboard the first item alone was the difference between two top-fives with nothing in common.
