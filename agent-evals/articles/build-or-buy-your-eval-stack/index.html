Build or buy your eval stack? The people who've done it say the platform is a database and the part you look at is yours

If you are shipping an LLM product and someone asks whether to buy an eval platform or roll your own, the honest answer from people who have done it is neither. Buy the storage. Build the screen you stare at when something breaks.

That is a smaller decision than the framing suggests, and it is worth being precise about where the line falls, because the obvious guess — buy the generic parts, build the domain-specific parts — turns out to be wrong in an instructive way.

What the buyers actually keep

Hamel Husain, who has spent several years consulting on this, reviews LangSmith, Braintrust, and Arize Phoenix in Selecting The Right AI Evals Tool and describes his own setup plainly: "I tend to use these tools as a backend data store and use Jupyter notebooks as well as my own custom built annotation interfaces for most of my needs."

Eugene Yan's Product Evals in Three Simple Steps describes the same arrangement without naming it. His recipe — label a few hundred samples, align an LLM judge against those labels, run a harness on every config change — has no platform in it anywhere. The team labels its own data, builds its own judges, and builds its own experiment runner.

Two people, two posts a year apart, same split.

Notice what they kept. An annotation interface is not domain-specific in any technical sense. Every team wants roughly the same thing: show me the trace, let me mark it good or bad, let me group the bad ones. It is exactly the kind of generic component you would expect to buy. Both of them built one anyway.

So the line is not specificity. The line is where a human has to look at something and decide. Everything behind that — trace ingestion, run control, dataset versioning, the audit trail — you rent. Everything at the surface where your eye lands, you own, because ill-fitting friction there is a tax you pay on every single trace.

Husain's four criteria for choosing a platform make the same point from the other direction, and what is missing from them is the striking part. He weighs: how quickly you get from a failing trace to a playground; whether manual annotation and error analysis are first-class; whether the tool resists "magic"; and whether you can export your data. Not one criterion mentions metrics, scorers, or statistical machinery. His stated reason: "error analysis is the highest ROI activity in AI engineering." The analytical layer that eval vendors compete on is not what a buyer is buying.

What it costs to build the part you keep

Nobody publishes this, which is why the two available numbers are worth quoting exactly.

Yan describes a team "~4 weeks into building their evaluation harness," where those four weeks covered defining criteria, collecting human annotations, aligning evaluators, and building the experiment harness. The payoff he reports: dozens of experiments in the following two weeks, and a few hundred over the next few months, because nobody was waiting on human annotation between config changes.

Anthropic's Demystifying evals for AI agents says 20–50 tasks drawn from real failures gets a team "surprisingly far," and cites the Bolt team building an eval system spanning static analysis, browser agents, and LLM judges within three months of launch.

Both are anecdotes. No team sizes, no hours, no breakdown. Treat them as an order of magnitude and nothing more: weeks to months, not quarters, and not days.

The sequencing inside those four weeks is more transferable than the total. The time goes into labels and judge alignment. The runner is the last and smallest piece. If you have read a few benchmark papers and concluded that eval work means building infrastructure, that is backwards — the infrastructure is the cheap end.

It is worth knowing why benchmark papers give that impression. They describe genuinely expensive machinery: DataSpace grades 410 tasks with a deterministic evaluator that turns out to be three carefully specified tolerance rules; WorkSurface-Bench needed a different ground-truth oracle for each of its three data surfaces, and validated its 1,151-task corpus with three annotators over a 200-task sample against six criteria; GABench built 84 executable tools before generating 10,400 tasks from them. All real, all expensive, and all beside the point for a product team. Those corpora exist to rank many systems across a wide ability range. Your suite exists to tell you whether last week's change made things worse. Different job, roughly one order of magnitude fewer items.

How big does your suite have to be

Yan gives the only sizing rule anywhere that starts from the precision you need rather than from someone else's item pool. At 200 samples with an observed 3% defect rate, the 95% interval is about 3% ± 2.4%. At 400, it narrows to 3% ± 1.7%. Halving the margin of error costs four times the samples.

That is worth sitting with before you commit to a suite size. On a 3% base rate, detecting a regression smaller than about two points means four figures of samples per run, which is a different product than the one most teams think they are building.

His labelling advice is the other half: at least 50–100 failures out of 200+ total samples, and use a weaker model to generate the failures, because synthetic defects come out "too exaggerated or too subtle in ways that don't reflect what happens in production."

Two things you should not build

A grader that parses model output. In August 2026 the Inspect AI project shipped a security fix disclosing that its math() scorer had been parsing model answers in a way that could execute Python on the evaluator host (CHANGELOG 0.3.256). The fix parses with a non-evaluating grammar inside a bounded worker thread.

This is worth more than its size as a bug. Sandboxing discussions around agent evals are always about containing what the agent does. Nobody asks what the scoring code does with what the agent writes — and in an agentic eval, the text arriving at your grader comes from a system selected for finding routes nobody anticipated. The general shape covers anything that evaluates, compiles, deserializes, or interpolates model output. That includes every LLM judge, where the injection path runs through the graded artifact into the grading prompt.

The bug was found, fixed, and published, which is the strongest available argument for adopting a maintained runner. A one-line eval(answer) in your own scorer gets none of those three.

The same changelog also notes that unseeded dataset shuffles had assigned irreproducible IDs, which "silently corrupted eval_set retries." Both failures produce a plausible number rather than an error.

Anything that writes your rubric and then scores against it. Husain is blunt: be "deeply skeptical of features that promise full automation without human validation, as these can create a powerful and dangerous illusion of confidence," and a feature combining rubric generation with immediate scoring "often hides flaws behind a high score."

The mechanism is worth naming because it is not obvious. A system that authors the criterion and then applies it has closed a loop on itself. The criterion is never read by anyone who knows the domain, and the score comes out high precisely because the criterion was drawn from the same distribution as the output. Compare a grader that is merely broken: it produces a low score, and eventually somebody investigates a low score. Nobody investigates a high one.

This is not an argument against automating error-analysis labour — reading a thousand traces and grouping them is exactly the kind of work worth handing to a machine. It is an argument against automating the judgment about what counts as a failure.

The empirical backing is thinner than the argument deserves but points the same way. Husain's Do Automated Evals Work? found automated eval platforms recalling 74–87% of expert-labelled failures while systematically missing the ones that required domain taste. Adopting one buys breadth, not judgment.

The thing you adopt has a defect rate, and it finally has a number

The build column of this decision has always carried a visible bill — labels, annotation time, a grader somebody has to write. The adopt column has been priced as a subscription. Two results from late August 2026 say the adopt column also carries an item-quality bill that nobody was itemising.

The larger one is an audit. Building a training set for terminal agents, the authors of Learning Generalizable Behaviors for Terminal Agents checked three publicly released collections of graded agent tasks — the kind of corpus a team would happily adopt — against a single rubric. The rubric reads three files together: the instruction shown to the agent, the checker that scores the run, and the container. Then it asks one question: would a competent agent that faithfully follows the instruction actually pass the checker?

The answer, per collection: 35.8% clean for TMax (14,399 tasks), 10.1% for TermiGen, 3.3% for TerminalTraj-5K. The dominant defect in all three is the same one — a checker that "passes without the task actually being solved: it checks only output shape/format/types/count, trusts a self-reported value, greps the source for keywords, checks a hardcoded subset, or tests only fixed inputs so the answer can be hardcoded." That runs at 40.4%, 51.4% and 67.2% respectively.

One worked example shows what that means in practice. A security task's instruction includes an example output that happens to be the exact correct answer for the binaries shipped in the container, and the checker compares against those same values. The agent fails to build the requested scanner, notices the answer is sitting in the prompt, writes it to a file — narrating, in its own trajectory, "I've just created a hardcoded output file" — and collects full marks. "The final-output verifier cannot distinguish this shortcut from a working scanner."

Two things make this usable rather than merely alarming. First, the audit is static: no sandbox is executed, so the price is one model call per task rather than one rollout per task, and the eight verdict categories are specific enough to hand to someone. Second, the authors audited their own auditor — a blind re-judge by a different vendor's model on 120 sampled tasks agreed with 94 of 105 "defective" calls but only 6 of 15 "clean" ones. So 35.8% is a ceiling, not an estimate. That is more honesty than most instrument papers offer, and it points the same way: a suite that comes back clean under this rubric has cleared a low bar.

The smaller result is that the scoring code has the same problem one layer down. An outside contributor auditing DeepEval's benchmark answer-scoring paths found three cases where a correct prediction scores zero with no error raised. The worst: MathQA questions always have five options, a through e, and e is often "none of these" — but the schema constraining the model's answer declared only a through d. So "every MathQA item whose gold answer is e (~1/5 of the dataset) is scored 0, even for a perfect model." Alongside it, DROP packed multi-span answers with a comma delimiter, so any answer containing a comma — every number written with a thousands separator — could never match; and BigBenchHard's batched and unbatched prediction paths scored the same input 1 and 0.

This is not one library having a bad month. promptfoo has shipped fixes for assertion types scoring wrong, and Inspect AI published a run of scoring-path defects in the string handling between model text and a structured value, including a grader that picked up an off-menu verdict from an earlier mention in its own reasoning. Three widely used runners, three sets of silent wrong-scoring defects, and every one found by somebody reading the scoring code rather than by a test failing.

The conclusion is not "hand-roll it." It is the opposite of comforting: the defect rate of a scorer written by one team, read by nobody, and never audited is not lower than these — it is unmeasured. What the maintained projects are actually selling here is that somebody eventually looks.

Before you believe a harness comparison, ask whether anyone reproduced the baseline

One more caution, from a paper making a claim rather than auditing one. Prime Agent reports 95.5% on ARC-AGI-3 with Claude Opus 5 against a published 30.2% for the same model on the ARC harness. Before quoting that, read what the authors say about it. They reran Claude Code and Codex themselves, "with matched prompt and settings," and came in below both vendors' self-reported numbers — so they used the vendors' published figures instead, and labelled the comparison as one that situates the result "rather than isolate[s] a causal harness effect."

That disclosure is more useful than the score. If a third party running two of the most widely deployed coding harnesses on a public benchmark split cannot reach the published numbers, then the reference point in every harness comparison is softer than it looks, and a 65-point gap between two differently-configured measurement stacks is not a 65-point harness effect.

The same paper supplies the counterweight, and it is the part worth acting on. On the nanoGPT speedrun — where the outcome is a training record verified as an eight-seed mean — "the choice of harness has little effect on final records compared to the noise of the experiment," even though the agents worked completely differently: under Prime Agent, one model ran roughly six times more side experiments per training run than under Claude Code, and another built a reusable probe function through which it ran ninety screening experiments and all nineteen of its validated records, where the same model on its own CLI edited files directly and built no such machinery.

So whether a harness is worth points depends on the benchmark, not the harness. Which means "we tried two harnesses on our eval and saw no difference" is not evidence of anything until you know your eval's run-to-run noise — and if you are running an A/B of this kind, measure that first.

Validate the judge before you trust anything it says

The cheapest high-value thing on this list, and almost nobody does it.

Yan's procedure: label 200+ samples binary, use 75% to iterate the judge prompt, hold 25% out, and report precision, recall, and Cohen's kappa on the holdout. His acceptance bands are 0.4–0.6 for substantial agreement and above 0.7 for excellent.

Two of his calibration figures deserve their own attention, because they undercut a lot of received practice about judge panels. Human annotators, he writes, "can miss as many as 50% of the defects," and human inter-rater reliability is often "as low as 0.2 - 0.3."

If two people scoring the same artifacts agree at kappa 0.3, then a three-model judge panel voting to a majority is not converging on a truth. It is converging on a majority. That is also the strongest argument for his insistence on binary pass/fail over Likert scales: binary labels are not chosen for simplicity, they are chosen because they are the only labels two people reproduce. These are round numbers asserted from experience, not measured, and they are the only estimate of the human floor available.

Inspect AI's unreleased changelog adds a krippendorff_alpha() metric computing inter-rater agreement across multiple judges, which is the right statistic for the job — it handles more than two raters, missing ratings, and ordinal scales, where Cohen's kappa does not. Having it in the runner does not make anyone report the number, but it removes the last excuse.

One caution on what the check covers. Agreement testing tells you whether your judge matches a person. It says nothing about whether your scoring code works, and those fail differently. Anthropic reports a model scoring 42% on CORE-Bench and 95% after grading bugs were fixed — a 53-point swing from ordinary defects in scoring logic, which would be perfectly self-consistent across both halves of any holdout split. Score one known-good output first. Then check agreement.

The short version

Rent the store. Build the screen. Budget weeks, not a quarter, and spend most of them on labels rather than infrastructure. Size your suite from the precision your decision needs, and know that the next increment of precision costs four times the last one. Do not let anything write your rubric and grade it in the same breath. And before you compare two systems, prove that your grader can grade — feed it something you know is correct and check that it says so.

If you adopt somebody's task suite, budget one model call per task to audit it before you trust a number that comes out of it: read the instruction, the checker and the container together and ask whether a correct solution passes and an incorrect one fails. The published base rate for suites that survive that question is somewhere between 3% and 36%.
