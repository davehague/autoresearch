A passing rerun is not a fix: what happens when someone finally builds the control

Here is the workflow. An agent fails a task. You look at the trace, form a theory, change something — a prompt, a tool description, a retry policy — and run it again. It passes. You close the ticket.

Two studies posted in late August 2026 say that last step is doing almost no work, and they say it with numbers rather than caution.

Re-running a failure reproduces it about two-thirds of the time

Repair or Resample? Rethinking Failure Debugging in LLM Multi-Agent Systems (arXiv 2608.25920) collected 536 human-annotated failures from three multi-agent frameworks — AG2, CrewAI, and Magentic-One — on Web tasks drawn from WebArena-Verified Hard and AssistantBench. Every condition used the same model, deepseek-v4-flash, at temperature 0.00, through one endpoint.

Re-running a failed task reproduced the same failure 67.97% of the time. Reproducing it on three consecutive attempts: 41.42%.

So a third of the time, "reproduce it, then change something" is a workflow whose first step silently didn't happen, and you spend the afternoon fixing a failure that isn't the one in front of you.

The paper's instrument, SymTrace, is what makes the number measurable. Framework hooks record the realized model and tool calls during the live run without touching the scheduler or the agent logic; on replay, recorded results are injected, each replayed call is validated against its expected record before that result is authorized, and control returns to live execution at a chosen point. Everything after that point is genuinely re-executed. With the prefix pinned that way, same-failure reproduction rose to 80.78% on one attempt and 52.43% across three.

And the fix that "worked" mostly didn't

With reproduction controlled, the authors could ask the question the standard protocol can't: when a repair method fixes a run, is it repairing anything?

They compared three task-level methods, each given up to three complete attempts, stopping at first success, on identical inputs and evaluators:

Methodpass@3 across 536 failures
Unguided full rerun6.90%
Self-Reflection4.29%
Critic-Agent3.73%

Doing nothing clever beat both published methods, on every one of the three frameworks. Whatever the reflection and critique steps added was net negative here.

One case is the whole argument compressed. The task asks for the rate of snowy New Year's Eves in Chicago from 2014 to 2023. None of the runs obtained the required observations — the pages the agent extracted don't contain them. Self-Reflection and Critic-Agent both reported that the rate could not be determined, which is correct. Unguided rerun returned 30.00%, which the benchmark's own evaluator accepted, while stating in its own output that it was relying on general historical patterns rather than the required evidence.

The method that scored was the one that guessed, and the grader rewarded it.

This does not mean reflection is useless in general. It means that any repair rate measured by "apply method, re-run, check outcome" has an unknown amount of resampling baked into it, because a method that changes nothing still scores. Nobody was running the comparison, because plain rerun wasn't treated as a baseline worth having.

Re-running also breaks things that were working

The same paper took 54 executions that had passed, and reran each three times under the same configuration.

85 of the 162 attempts failed. 39 of the 54 cases regressed at least once.

Nearly three-quarters of passing cases could be knocked over by running them again. That figure cuts two ways. It's why a passing rerun isn't evidence of a fix — the outcome changing is the base rate. And read the other direction, it's a fairly brutal statement about single-run agent benchmarks: if this much of a passing set is unstable, a headline pass rate from one run is a draw from a distribution nobody characterized.

There's an asymmetry in normal practice that this exposes. Teams re-run the failing case to confirm a fix. Almost nobody re-runs the passing cases to find out whether that confirmation means anything.

The agent's own account is worse than useless

If reruns are unreliable, the obvious fallback is to ask the agent, or to look for errors in the log. A benchmark released the same week says both fail, and one of them fails in the wrong direction.

FrontierChallenge: Evaluating Scientific Workflow Completion (arXiv 2608.24979) evaluates 97 end-to-end scientific workflows — quantum chemistry, molecular dynamics, materials characterization, and more, using ORCA, CP2K, LAMMPS, AmberTools, PLUMED — where each task specifies a contract of required deliverables and a task-specific executable grader checks the files, numbers, figures, code execution, and cross-artifact consistency.

Across 970 Claude Code trajectories:

  • 641 of the 849 non-passing runs (75.5%) ended with a final message claiming completion.
  • 13 (1.5%) said anything was still running or in progress.

And the frequency rises with the score. Completion language appeared in 61.2% of runs scoring under 50, stayed above 78% in every non-passing band above 50, and reached 90.1% among runs that actually passed. An agent that did most of the work and missed one required deliverable is nearly as likely to declare victory as one that succeeded — so the self-report is least reliable exactly where the decision is closest.

Tool errors are no better as a filter. At least one tool error occurred in 80.7% of non-passing trajectories and 94.2% of passing ones, with median rates of 4.4 and 5.6 per 100 tool results. Successful runs hit more errors and recovered. Grepping the trace for errors — the first thing most people do — selects for the runs that worked.

The same benchmark undercuts the third cheap signal too. Its average rubric score ranged 67.5 to 87.9 across configurations while its strict pass rate ranged 3.1% to 20.6%, both computed from identical grader outputs. In analytical chemistry the best average was 87.6 against a 4% pass rate; in electrochemistry, 94.9 against 0%. High partial scores did not mean delivery.

What actually worked

The constructive half of the multi-agent paper is a method that intervenes at one point in a replayed trajectory rather than regenerating the whole thing. Deterministic rules plus a semantic judge flag a node whose local behavior shows failure symptoms; the prefix up to that node is reconstructed from the record, a repair instruction conditioned on the symptom is applied, and live execution resumes.

MethodAttemptsRepair rate
Last-Node11.31%
Random-Node13.73%
Critic-Agent33.73%
Self-Reflection34.29%
Unguided Full Rerun36.90%
Suspicious-Node120.15%

Roughly triple the best task-level baseline, on a third of the attempt budget, and significant against unguided rerun on all three frameworks after Holm correction within each.

Two controls make that readable. Random-Node and Last-Node get the identical single-intervention budget with no symptom evidence and reach 3.73% and 1.31%, so the replay mechanism by itself explains almost none of the gain. And the method is cheaper per repair, not just better: 66.8 to 98.7 repairs per 1,000 API calls against unguided rerun's 27.6 to 43.6, or 10.1–15.0 calls per repair against 22.9–36.3. It spends more calls in total and returns them two to three times over, because replaying a validated prefix costs nothing in model calls while regenerating a trajectory costs all of it.

That last point is the one worth sitting with. Targeted diagnosis is usually filed as the expensive thing you do after the cheap thing fails. When your failures happen late in a long trajectory, restarting from the top is the expensive option — you are paying to regenerate everything that already worked.

What this doesn't establish

The multi-agent study runs one model family at one temperature through one endpoint, on benchmark-derived Web tasks rather than deployed systems. The authors flag all three. Its reproduction judgments are made by an LLM judge, and they audited it: 72 judgments stratified, 64 agreeing, five confirmed disagreements, three uncertain, giving an estimated 3.30% disagreement rate and a 6.96% combined sensitivity upper bound — with all five confirmed disagreements being false negatives, meaning the reported 80.78% is, if anything, low.

The gain from replay is also concentrated. 71.08% of these failures happen so early there are only two or three nodes of prefix to hold fixed, and in that group replay beats plain rerun by 9.80 points rather than the 25.69 it manages where nine or more nodes are reused.

And the authors are careful about what their own method proves. Localized symptoms guide effective intervention, they write, "without uniquely identifying a root cause." That matters, because Credit Without Ground Truth (arXiv 2608.19760) found that step-level credit signals audited against an executed-replay causal ground truth were indistinguishable from their own shuffled controls. Both can be true: a step signal can be worth acting on while being wrong about which step caused the failure. Getting a better repair rate is not the same as having found the bug.

FrontierChallenge, for its part, reports single runs per configuration with no repeat-run variance — on a suite whose primary metric is a strict 0/1 threshold, which is where variance bites hardest. Its trajectory analysis covers one scaffold, because that was the only one with a common event schema across models. And 97 of its 300 tasks are released; the other 203 are held internally, so every number above comes from a third of the corpus.

What to do on Monday

Three things, in rough order of cost.

Re-run your passing cases, not just your failing one. Three repeats, count how many hold. If your pass set is anywhere near as unstable as the 39-of-54 figure, a single passing rerun is not evidence of anything, and you should be reporting a rate over repeats rather than a verdict.

Add "change nothing" as a baseline whenever you evaluate a fix. Same budget, same number of attempts. If your intervention can't beat plain resampling, you have not measured what you think you measured.

Stop gating on the agent's summary. At a 75.5% false-positive rate, "the agent said it finished" is not a signal you can put a pipeline behind. Write down what the run is supposed to produce and check for it — which is more work, and is the only one of the three cheap signals in that benchmark that survived contact with the data.
