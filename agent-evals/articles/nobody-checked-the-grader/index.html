The drift guard caught 0 of 56 reversals, and nobody had checked

A production system compared two versions of a text block by embedding-cosine similarity and passed anything above a cutoff. It was there to notice when a piece of text stopped meaning what it used to mean. Audited against 56 mutations built to break meaning, it caught zero of them. One of the edits it approved was "withhold the study drug" becoming "administer the study drug", at a similarity of 0.9608.

That is from Similarity Gates Approve Reversals: A Validity Audit of Embedding-Cosine Thresholds in Agent Systems (arXiv 2608.10216), which audits the whole class — deduplication filters, semantic caches, drift guards, answer graders — as measurement instruments rather than as features. Across 90 combinations of configuration, threshold and task, balanced accuracy never exceeded 0.700, with a median of 0.525. A coin is 0.500.

The diagnosis is one sentence long and applies to every gate in the class. These checks are deployed to ask "does this still mean the same thing," and the score answers "how much did the wording change." Those come apart hardest in exactly the cases the gate exists to catch, because reversing an instruction is often a one-word edit while agreeing with it usually means rephrasing a sentence.

The uncomfortable general point

Almost nobody validates the thing doing the grading, and when someone does, the results are consistently worse than anyone assumed.

Two months of published work make the pattern hard to dismiss:

A grading bug was worth 53 points. On CORE-Bench, one model scored 42% and then 95% after grading defects were fixed — described in Anthropic's Demystifying evals for AI agents. That is larger than any model-versus-model or scaffold-versus-scaffold difference reported in this literature. It is also invisible by construction: a broken grader produces a low score, and a low score on a hard new benchmark is what everyone expects to see.

The judges in circulation had not been checked either. Benchmarking LLM Judges for Mobile Agent Evaluation (arXiv 2608.11434) scored six judge methods against 931 human-annotated trajectories across 6 benchmarks, 4 agent models and 68 apps. Five of the six were the judges that published benchmarks were already using for their headline numbers. The paper also found that a deliberately simple baseline — sampled screenshots, no engineering — was competitive with and sometimes better than the purpose-built pipelines, and that the underlying model mattered more than any of the pipeline design.

Two judge models failed in opposite directions. The same study found one backbone systematically conservative and the other systematically permissive, tracking each model's precision-recall operating point. If you build a panel out of mixed judges and average them, opposed biases do not cancel into truth; they cancel into a rate that describes neither judge's behaviour, with the residual set by which models you happened to pick.

Set that next to the similarity-gate audit and the shape is consistent: the graders in production, whether they are language models or a cosine and a threshold, are mostly unaudited, and audits keep finding them at or near chance on the cases that matter.

What it costs to check, and it is not much

The useful part of this literature is that every available check is cheap. Five distinct ones now exist, and they catch different things.

Score something you already know is right. If your grader rejects a known-good output, you have found a grader bug in an afternoon. Nothing else on this list is cheaper, and the CORE-Bench swing is the argument for doing it first.

Hold out a quarter of your labels. Product Evals in Three Simple Steps gives the procedure: label 200+ samples binary with 50–100 failures among them, tune the judge on 75%, and report precision, recall and Cohen's κ on the held-out 25%. This catches a judge that disagrees with a person. It cannot catch a grader whose definition of correct is wrong, since a wrong definition is perfectly self-consistent across both splits.

Check the judge's biases without any labels at all. Evaluating the Effectiveness of LLM-Evaluators collects two defects measurable from the judge's own outputs: position bias — one model preferred the first-listed answer 50% of the time, another 70% — and verbosity bias, with both models preferring longer responses over 90% of the time. Swap the order and count disagreements; correlate the verdict with response length. At those magnitudes both surface on a few dozen items, before any annotation budget exists. The figures come from an older model generation, so treat the practice as more transferable than the numbers.

Let the system under test attack your ground truth. Vero: Can AI Agents Build Formally Verified Software Repositories? (arXiv 2608.13522) lets agents formally prove a specification unsatisfiable or a reference implementation wrong, surfacing errors in the benchmark during curation rather than blaming the agent. The weak form needs no proof assistant and costs nothing: when an agent fails an item and its trace argues the item is broken, read the argument. Most evaluation treats a failed item as a fact about the agent.

Rank your corpus for suspicious items without reading it. Predicting Task Difficulty Without Rollouts (arXiv 2608.05797) fits item difficulty from outcomes you already have, predicts it from the task text, and inspects where the two disagree most. A large positive residual on SWE-bench's django-14725 traced back to critical information missing from the task description. The prediction does not have to be good for this to work — a weak predictor still ranks items by how surprising their difficulty is, and only the tail matters. The paper reports two examples and no count across its 17 benchmarks, so nobody knows whether an hour of this finds one broken item or forty.

The fifth check is the one everyone will skip

The similarity-gate audit contains a second result that is easy to miss and probably matters more than the first.

The author checked whether evaluating the gate was affected by the same confounder as the gate itself. It was, decisively. On a corpus built the obvious way, the audit's decision AUROC was exactly 0.000 in 13 of 18 configuration-task cells, and at most 0.040 in all 18. On a balanced design — reversals paired against paraphrases at matched surface overlap — the same nine configurations scored 0.440 to 0.815.

An AUROC of zero is not a failure to answer. It is a confident, perfectly inverted answer. The instrument was the same in both cases; only the item set changed. And the paper reports that this bit its own headline claims twice before the design was fixed.

The generalisation is worth stating as a rule, because it is not in any of the construction guides: hold the variable your grader actually responds to constant across your two classes. For an embedding gate that variable is surface overlap. For an LLM judge it is response length or answer position. For an agent doing automated labelling it is input length — one study found label count correlating with input length at ρ = 0.81, falling to ρ = 0.15 once a human reviewed the labels, which is a free detector for a labeller that is transcribing rather than analysing.

If you skip this, your validation set will confirm whatever your grader already believes, and it will do so cleanly.

Two things that will not save you

Swapping a component. The obvious fix for a bad similarity gate is a better encoder or a smarter rule. The audit tried both: an overlap-conditioned gate reached 0.750 in-sample and 0.533 held out, an encoder swap landed at chance, and dropping in a natural-language-inference model did no better. The defect was the framing, not the part.

A random train/test split. The 0.750-to-0.533 collapse only appeared on separately authored held-out data. One person writing both halves of a split reproduces their own construction habits on both sides, so the split validates nothing. This is the strongest holdout in any of these papers and it costs a second author.

What is actually known, and what is not

Known, with numbers behind it: a grading defect can own more of a score than any model or scaffolding difference; the judges used for published headline numbers had not been validated against human labels until someone did it at population scale; a shipped deterministic gate class runs at chance on the cases it exists to catch; and position, verbosity and length biases are large and measurable for free.

Not known: how often any of this happens in ordinary practice. Every figure here comes from a paper whose purpose was to find the problem. The similarity-gate audit is one author with no replication, and its 0-of-56 result is one production system. Nobody has reported the base rate — what share of graders in the wild are broken, and by how much.

Also not known: whether anyone acts on this. All five checks are afternoons. There is no published report of a team running even one of them on its own eval, finding something, and saying what it was. Which means the single most useful contribution available right now is unglamorous: run one of these on your grader and write down what you found.
