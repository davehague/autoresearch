An LLM judge is not a thing you build. It's a thing you run.

Most advice about using a language model to grade another language model's output stops at the moment the judge starts working. Write the rubric, collect some labels, check that the judge agrees with your humans often enough, ship it. The published guidance is good on that part and nearly silent on what happens next.

A report from Netflix published in August 2026 is the first public account of a judge that has been in production long enough to have an operating history, and it is mostly about the "next" part. It comes with numbers.

What it cost to start

Netflix's judge grades short explanations of why a title was recommended — "because you watched X" — at a volume of hundreds of thousands of distinct explanations per week, served to millions of members. Before the judge existed, they built a benchmark: roughly 900 human-labelled explanations, split close to evenly between pass and fail, deliberately over-sampled so that about 54% were failures rather than the much smaller share you'd see in production. The items came from three places on purpose — hand-written by domain experts, model-generated to sit near the boundary, and sampled from real traffic.

Every failing item carried a written rationale from the human rater, not just a fail label.

Their first stated lesson is "invest in the benchmark before the judge," and 900 is a larger number than most published guidance implies. Hamel Husain's LLM Evals FAQ puts a working judge at roughly a hundred labels plus ongoing maintenance. Eugene Yan's Product Evals in Three Simple Steps prices 200 samples at about ±2.4 points of precision on a 3% defect rate, and notes that halving that error costs four times the samples.

Those aren't in conflict. A hundred labels gets you a judge you use to steer your own iteration. Nine hundred is what you need when the judge decides what reaches customers.

The failure mode every acceptance test misses

Here is the part worth stealing regardless of your scale.

Netflix tunes the judge's rubric — not the model — in a loop, and it builds its training focus set from two kinds of error. The first is obvious: the judge and the human disagreed about pass or fail. The second is not: the judge and the human agreed the item fails, and named different reasons.

Nearly every published judge-acceptance procedure grades only the verdict. Precision, recall and Cohen's kappa on a held-out set. True-positive and true-negative rates. Agreement against an annotator pool. All of them pass a judge that reaches the right answer through the wrong reading.

That judge is fine in one job and broken in another. If you only consume the pass/fail bit, a confabulated reason costs you nothing. If you feed the judge's explanation back to the generator as a revision instruction — which is exactly what Netflix does, in a generate-judge-revise loop with a three-retry budget — then the reason is the output, and a judge that is right by accident sends your system off to fix something that wasn't wrong.

Checking for this needs a second instrument: a model that compares the judge's written reason against the human's. Netflix validated that one before trusting it — 98.6% agreement with human validators across 300 test pairs — which is more discipline than most systems show when they add a model to supervise a model.

The cheap version of this costs you one extra column at labelling time. When a rater marks an item as failing, make them write one sentence saying why. You want that column for debugging the rubric anyway.

Knowing when it's gone wrong

Netflix samples about 300 explanations a week for human review, stratified across judge outcomes, and declares drift when the judge falls outside a band defined by how much the humans disagree with each other: two standard deviations below mean human performance on the same metric.

This is a better construction than a fixed threshold, and the reason is worth being explicit about. Human raters do not agree with each other very much. Eugene Yan's survey Evaluating the Effectiveness of LLM-Evaluators and Hamel Husain's judge guide both report human-human agreement in the kappa 0.2 to 0.3 range on subjective criteria. Holding a judge to 90% agreement on a task where two humans manage 70% is not a quality bar, it's a category error. A band anchored to human disagreement widens exactly where it should and tightens exactly where it should.

So: about 300 labels a week, indefinitely. Roughly three times your build-time label count per year. That's the first published price on keeping a judge aligned, and it is the number to take into the meeting where someone asks why the eval budget doesn't go to zero after launch.

One caveat the report is candid enough to make available. Since launch, the alarm has never fired. That's either a well-built judge on a stable task or a band wide enough to be decorative, and nothing in the report tells them apart. Two standard deviations of a noisy quantity estimated from 300 items a week is a generous interval. If you build this, run a deliberately degraded judge through your monitor once and confirm it trips. An alarm that has never gone off has never been tested.

Panels are cheaper than they look, and for the wrong reason

Separately, a paper called Reasoning Jury (arXiv 2608.12585) replaces a single judge with three to five, each returning a structured verdict, followed by either a moderator consolidating them or a multi-turn debate in which the jurors critique each other and revise their votes.

The headline is a cost result. On a step-level defect-detection benchmark, a three-model open-weight jury scored 80.8 balanced F1 against 83.9 for the best single frontier judge — while costing $12.35 versus roughly $77. It got there by burning 7.6 times the tokens across 10.9 times the API calls. The entire mechanism is the price sheet: the cheap model bills $0.62 per million output tokens where the expensive one bills $25.

That inverts the usual optimization. The question stops being how few judge calls you can get away with and becomes how many cheap ones buy back the capability gap. Here, about three, recovering most of a 16-point deficit but not all of it.

Two findings in the same paper deserve more attention than the cost number.

Vendor diversity is not the mechanism. A "jury" of three independent samples of one model gained 13.0 points over that model judging alone. A five-model jury spanning different vendors gained 15.4. So roughly 85% of what a panel buys is variance reduction from resampling, and only about 2.4 points comes from different model families genuinely failing in different ways. The multi-vendor panel is the expensive half to procure and to keep running — separate contracts, separate rate limits, separate re-validation every time any one of them ships an update — and it is carrying the smaller share. Before you buy one, resample the judge you already have and measure.

The moderator is a component nobody reports. Swapping the model that aggregates the jury's verdicts moved the final score by 6.1 to 6.4 points under consolidation, and 2.6 to 3.7 under debate. Six points is larger than most of the effects anyone is trying to measure with these judges. "We used a jury of five models" is an underspecified description of a method in exactly the way "we used an LLM judge" already was.

The asymmetry is also a design hint: debate is about half as sensitive to who moderates, because the jurors argue and revise before the moderator sees anything. You pay for that in latency — the paper rules the approach out for on-policy training on those grounds.

There is one more thing the jury results say that reads against the grain of most panel enthusiasm. When the frontier models formed the jury, the panel beat its best member by half a point. Panels don't add capability, they recover it, and the amount available to recover is roughly your gap to the best judge you could otherwise buy. If you already have the best judge, a panel is overhead.

The uncomfortable part

Netflix ran a five-week A/B test across tens of millions of members. Judge-aligned explanations shifted viewing toward novel content by +0.2% relative and increased successful browse-to-play sessions by +0.3% relative, both significant at p<0.05.

Four lifecycle phases, 900 annotated labels, a tuned rubric, a validated meta-judge, 300 labels a week forever, a few thousand dollars a week of inference — for two tenths of a percent.

The result that actually justifies the programme is the other one they report: no user-initiated takedowns or escalations related to explanation quality. The judge is insurance. It didn't make the explanations better in any way a product metric could detect at meaningful magnitude; it made them safe enough to ship at all.

That's a legitimate argument and it is a different argument from the one most evaluation work makes. "Better measurement makes the system better" competes on return against every other use of the same engineering time, and a 0.2% lift loses most of those comparisons. "Better measurement makes the system shippable" competes against the expected cost of the incident it prevents, which nobody wants to estimate out loud. If you're funding a judge, know which case you're actually making.

And note what the experiment cannot tell you. The control arm was no explanations, not unjudged explanations. So the test measured the value of having explanations at all. The judge's own contribution isn't isolated by it.

What to do about all this

If you're running a judge in production, four things follow, roughly in order of cost:

  1. Add a reason column to your labels. One sentence per failing item. It's near-free and it's the only way to catch a judge that's right for the wrong reason.
  2. Check whether your judge is admissible before you trust it at all. A separate result — Competence, Not Accuracy (arXiv 2608.18719) — shows that a judge grading without a reference answer is bounded by its own ability to solve the task, giving a computable floor: its competence must exceed 1/k for k possible answers. Measured on real runs, the same judge separated correct from incorrect at 0.489 on research mathematics — chance — and 0.735 on factual QA. Admissibility is a property of the judge and the task, not the judge. The screen costs one pass and no labels: have the judge answer your own items closed-book and count non-answers as wrong.
  3. Set your drift band from human disagreement, not from a round number — and then trip it on purpose once to prove it works.
  4. Before buying a multi-vendor panel, run three samples of the judge you have. One afternoon, and it will tell you whether you're about to pay for 2.4 points or 13.

The through-line across all of it: the expensive part of an LLM judge was never writing the prompt. It's the labelled benchmark underneath it and the weekly sample that keeps it honest — and both of those are recurring costs on a system that quietly changes underneath you.
