59:["$","h2","h2-14",{"children":"What to do with this"}]
5a:["$","p","p-63",{"children":"Three things follow, in descending order of confidence."}]
5b:["$","p","p-64",{"children":[["$","strong","strong-0",{"children":"Evaluate the model and the harness as a pair, not in sequence."}]," Picking a model from public standings and then dropping it into your scaffold is the exact procedure the rank-instability result invalidates. A harness comparison belongs inside your evaluation rather than downstream of it — on one realistic workload it was worth 15 points."]}]
5c:["$","p","p-65",{"children":[["$","strong","strong-0",{"children":"Do not generalize a scaffold win."}]," HarnessOpt-Bench's own caveat is the most portable finding across all four papers: improvements swing by task and by how good your starting point was. LangChain's tuned harness needed re-tuning for a different model, which adds the axis that costs the most — scaffolding gains do not survive a model upgrade, so budget harness work as recurring rather than capital."]}]
5d:["$","p","p-66",{"children":[["$","strong","strong-0",{"children":"Say which property you measured."}]," A harness comparison run on task accuracy and one run on containment gave opposite answers. Whatever you conclude about where your own variance sits is conditional on the axis you scored, and that condition is worth one sentence in whatever you write down."]}]
5e:["$","p","p-67",{"children":[["$","strong","strong-0",{"children":"Check the grader before you compare anything."}]," Fifty-three points on CORE-Bench came from scoring bugs. Nothing else on this list matters if the instrument is broken, and a broken instrument looks like a hard problem."]}]
5f:["$","p","p-68",{"children":[["$","strong","strong-0",{"children":"If you have persistent state or a long horizon, one run is one sample."}]," EduClaw-Bench's second finding — almost nothing held up over 30 days — says a point-in-time score has a shelf life, and it does not tell you what that shelf life is."]}]
60:["$","p","p-69",{"children":[["$","strong","strong-0",{"children":"Write down your inference-time settings, and your environment."}]," Reasoning effort, thinking budget, temperature — and the corpus snapshot, chunking policy, tool schema, and observation format. MDArena's numbers are too noisy to prove the first group matters and SimpleWikiSearch does not quantify the second, but all of them are cheap to record and all of them are currently invisible in almost every comparison anyone publishes, including the ones above. A comparison that does not state them is not reproducible, whatever else it is."]}]
61:["$","p","p-70",{"children":[["$","strong","strong-0",{"children":"Do not let a completion rate stand in for whether the run was acceptable."}]," Two thirds of AgentS4D's runs finished the task and tripped a safety signal. If your agent has side effects, that is two measurements, not one — and averaging them into a single score puts an agent that finishes everything badly level with one that refuses everything. Name the constraint, score violations as their own rate, and gate on it rather than folding it in."]}]
62:["$","p","p-71",{"children":["There is a useful piece of scaffolding for the diagnostic work. ",["$","em","em-0",{"children":"Model or Harness? An Interaction-Centric Taxonomy for Localizing Agent Failures"}]," (arXiv 2607.28802) assigns each of 41 observed failure modes to a component boundary and a side, so a failure routes to post-training, to harness engineering, or to environment redesign. It asserts the model/harness seam is real and worth labelling along. The benchmarks above are the first attempts to measure which side of that seam the variance sits on — and the honest current answer is that it depends on what you are asking the agent to do, that for the job most people have it is not mostly the model, that the seam has more sides than two, and that nobody has yet put a number on any of them."]}]
