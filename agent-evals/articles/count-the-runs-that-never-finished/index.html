Before you believe a comparison between two agents, count the runs that never finished

A team compares two ways of running the same model. Method A is the plain single pass. Method B samples four times and picks among the answers. Method B costs four times as much, and on one benchmark it scores three points worse. That looks like a clean negative result: the expensive thing didn't help.

It wasn't. The two arms hadn't been scored on the same questions.

The 3.6-point artefact

In Recursive Agentic Reasoning (arXiv 2608.23956), the authors compare three test-time reasoning strategies against a single-pass baseline: deepen one line of thought, decompose the problem, or sample several attempts and choose. On Qwen3.6-plus over Humanity's Last Exam, the sampling method came in at 11.20% against the baseline's 14.20%.

Then they counted the items each arm had failed to return at all. The sampling arm had lost 172 questions to timeouts. The baseline had lost 131. Every one of those extra 41 dropped items had been scored as a wrong answer.

Recomputed over only the questions that every arm managed to resolve, the sampling method was +0.66 — better, not worse. The published sign flipped on a scoring choice, and the size of the artefact was about 3.6 points.

The fix is one line: intersect the resolved-item sets across arms before computing accuracy. The authors call it paired scoring and argue it should be the default protocol for any comparison of this kind.

What makes this worth more than a curiosity is that the bias has a direction. The arm that spends more compute per question runs longer, hits more timeouts, and drops more items. Unpaired scoring converts that into lower accuracy. So the penalty falls hardest on exactly the intervention the study exists to evaluate — and it falls the same way in every study that doesn't pair. This is not noise that a confidence interval would absorb. It is a systematic tilt against the treatment.

Half the items produced no output at all

The same paper explains where those dropped items come from, and the number is larger than most people expect.

Running under a 16,000-token budget, the single-pass baseline returned empty, budget-exhausted content on 51.2% of Humanity's Last Exam items, 34.5% of BBEH, and 36.1% of Omni-MATH. The model burned its budget inside a hidden reasoning stream and emitted nothing.

Read that again next to the accuracy figure. On the hardest of those benchmarks, a majority of the baseline's failures were not wrong answers. They were silence. A published accuracy over those items averages "got it wrong" and "never spoke" into a single number, and the second is the bigger category — which means that cell is measuring how well the model's deliberation fits inside 16,000 tokens at least as much as it measures reasoning. Change the budget and the score moves with the model untouched.

It also explains most of the winner. The sampling method's advantage correlates with the baseline's empty-output rate at r = 0.72, and the authors say so plainly: part of its benefit is recovering from truncation rather than exploring better reasoning paths. Sample four times and at least one attempt fits in the budget.

That is a genuinely useful thing for a deployed system to do. It is a misleading thing to put on a leaderboard, because a team whose harness already handles truncation — this one added a retry that asks a budget-exhausted model to finalise, and still lost 172 items — would see a much smaller gain. The +5.98-point average across their 14 settings is an upper bound, not an estimate.

Two counters would have surfaced all of this, and both are already sitting in the logs of every evaluation ever run: items attempted and items resolved, per arm.

The same problem, one layer down: what counts as an answer

Dropped items are the extreme case of a more general question — what your grader is willing to accept as a response.

Beyond Executable Models (arXiv 2608.23653) builds a 232-task benchmark for agents doing physical system modelling in Modelica, a language where a model can compile and simulate perfectly while describing physics nobody asked for. The pass criterion is deliberately conjunctive: interface, model-specific constraints, model checking, successful simulation, and task-specific behavioural conditions. All five, or it fails.

The fifth gate is the one that pays. Among the 38 hardest generation tasks, one of the two harnesses under test produced 21 submissions that ran correctly and behaved wrongly; the other produced 4. A grader that stops at "it compiled and simulated" scores all 25 as passes and reports a five-fold difference between two systems as zero.

The paper is careful about what its own gate proves, in a sentence more benchmark authors should copy: a pass "means that the submitted artifact satisfies the frozen, scenario-covered engineering contract. It is not formal verification or a guarantee over every operating condition." Behavioural checks raise the floor a long way. They do not reach a ceiling.

Worth noting where the evidence here is thin: this is a benchmark built by the same author as one of the two harnesses being compared, with no repeated runs and therefore no variance on either arm. The 21-against-4 split is one unreplicated observation. The design argument — that "it executed" and "it was right" are different measurements — stands on its own.

And one layer up: a harness you searched for is a harness you fitted

There is a third version of the same mistake, and it has become urgent because tuning the scaffolding around a frozen model now works well enough that people are automating it.

StarHarness (arXiv 2608.24804) evolves the harness — prompt framing, tool interfaces, skills, subagent structure, agent-loop configuration — with model weights untouched, and moves three enterprise benchmarks a long way: ITBench SRE from 40.0% to 75.0%, EnterpriseOps-Gym from 23.3% to 43.7%, AutomationBench Finance from 57.1% to 83.2%, on four to twelve accepted changes each. The evolved harness then transferred to a different model family without being re-evolved, and estimated cost per task fell 17–53%.

Those are large numbers, and the reason to take them at all seriously is the bookkeeping. The system that proposes harness changes sees one set of tasks. The score that decides whether a change is accepted is computed on a second set the proposer never sees. A third set is held out and touched at the end. That is the standard train/validation/test discipline of supervised learning, applied to a scaffold — and it is the exception rather than the rule in this literature.

VideoHarness-RSI (arXiv 2608.24302) shows what the missing split costs. It searches over a much narrower object — just the program that selects which 40 frames of a long video reach a frozen vision-language model — using only two splits, promoting on development-set accuracy and reporting on a held-out set. The winning program scored 52.0% on development and 50.3% held out: a 1.7-point gap at 15 candidates compared, which the authors report widening when they gave the search more freedom. Small, honest, and exactly the shape you'd predict.

If you selected your scaffold by comparing scores, those scores can't also be your evidence that it works. Three numbers fix it, and the search already computed all three: development score, held-out score, and how many candidates you compared. Neither the earlier work in this area nor most of what's published alongside it prints them.

What to do on Monday

Four checks, none of which requires new instrumentation:

  1. Print items attempted and items resolved, per arm. Then compute your headline over the intersection. If the two arms dropped different numbers of items, your unpaired number is tilted against whichever arm is slower.
  2. Print the empty-output rate. A result that doesn't state what fraction of its items produced a parseable answer hasn't stated its denominator.
  3. Ask whether your grader can tell "it ran" from "it was right." If the only gate is execution, you are measuring something narrower than you think, and differences between systems on that axis are invisible to you.
  4. If you tuned your scaffold by looking at scores, report a held-out number and a candidate count. The gap between them is small when the search is small, and it grows.

The through-line is dull and hard to argue with: a comparison between two arms means nothing until both arms are represented identically — same items, same structure, same handling of the runs that fell over. The failures that break this rule are quiet by construction. A question that timed out looks exactly like a question the model got wrong, and nothing in the output tells you which one you scored.
