Skin sensitisation without the animal: how the 2-out-of-3 defined approach actually holds up

Is there a non-animal method for skin-sensitisation hazard you can actually rely on, and for what? For this one endpoint, yes — with specific caveats worth understanding. The OECD's 2-out-of-3 (2o3) defined approach, codified in Guideline 497, is a fixed, rule-based combination of non-animal assays that replaces the animal test (the mouse local lymph node assay) for calling a chemical a sensitiser or non-sensitiser — within a defined chemical applicability domain. It is the clearest "this NAM is ready" case in this vault, and it is the only one that is strong on all three axes this vault tracks: scientific validity (regulatory-adopted), regulatory acceptance (OECD-TG-adopted), and loop-readiness (closed-loop-ready — a machine could run it end to end). Those three are not the same thing, and keeping them apart is the whole point of reading a method honestly.

Refreshed 2026-08-25. Three things landed since the last refresh and one of them changes what this article is for. A multi-sector consortium with the US Food and Drug Administration at the table pointed out that the 2-out-of-3 rule was built and tested on single chemicals, which means a machine can run it correctly on a plant extract and return an answer nobody has established the meaning of. A machine-learning benchmark showed that the obvious way to decide which chemicals to send to this battery is blind in exactly the place it needs to see. And a group of European risk-assessment scientists, writing to argue that non-animal methods are being oversold, named this battery as the field's one worked example of doing it right — and then said the list is that short. New section at the end: "The one that works, and how alone it is."

The vault no longer holds that, and the sentence has been rewritten below rather than quietly deleted. What the "battery" actually is

The 2o3 is not a single clever assay — it is a rule applied to assays reading different steps of the biological chain that turns a chemical into an allergen (the skin-sensitisation Adverse Outcome Pathway). Take two concordant results out of three key events and you have your hazard call. The three original methods:

  • DPRA (OECD TG 442C) — the molecular initiating event: does the chemical covalently bind protein? Roughly 80% accuracy, 80% sensitivity, 77% specificity against reference data.
  • KeratinoSens (OECD TG 442D) — the keratinocyte-activation step, via an ARE-Nrf2 luciferase reporter. About 77% accuracy, 78% sensitivity, 76% specificity versus the animal test.
  • h-CLAT (OECD TG 442E) — the dendritic-cell-activation step, read as CD86/CD54 up-regulation. About 85% accuracy, 93% sensitivity, 66% specificity versus the animal test.

None of the three is a stand-alone animal replacement, and none is meant to be. That is the useful insight the component numbers give you: h-CLAT almost never misses a real sensitiser (93% sensitivity) but over-calls non-sensitisers (66% specificity); DPRA and KeratinoSens are the other way round — steadier on specificity. Against human data the combined approach lands at 72–97% balanced accuracy, 84–97% sensitivity, and 50–100% specificity.

A retraction, stated plainly. An earlier version of this article said the three were combined "precisely so the battery's blind spots don't line up." That claim is wrong in one specific and important way, and the vault found it out by asking its own standard question of this battery rather than by reading a new paper. The three assays' statistical profiles are genuinely complementary — the sensitivity/specificity trade-off above is real. But they share one structural failure. DPRA is pure chemistry with no cells and therefore no metabolism at all; KeratinoSens and h-CLAT run in immortalised cell lines whose skin-relevant metabolic competence is limited. So a pro-hapten — a chemical that is harmless until an enzyme in the skin turns it into an allergen — has three chances to be missed for one shared reason, and the 2-out-of-3 rule then reports two concordant negatives out of three, which is a pass. The blind spots do line up; they line up in the place the rule is least equipped to notice. This is flagged in the vault as a candidate awaiting a human verdict and a citation, not as a settled finding — the metabolic-competence claim about the two cell lines is drawn from the vault's own notes rather than from a source read for it.

What changed: the battery is now a rule with an admissible list, not a fixed triple

The version of Guideline 497 this article originally described was the 2021 edition. The current edition is 2 July 2026, and the most consequential change is structural rather than numerical: each key-event slot now admits alternative information sources. ADRA and kDPRA can stand in for DPRA at the protein-binding step; LuSens, EpiSensA, Epi²SensA and α-Sens® can stand in for KeratinoSens at the keratinocyte step; U-SENS™ and GARD®skin at the dendritic-cell step. Each admissible combination is a permutation of the 2o3 with its own performance table in the guideline's supporting annexes.

So what actually carries the adopted regulatory standing is a data-interpretation procedure plus a list of acceptable inputs — not three named assays. That is a more durable object than a fixed triple, and a more slippery one to cite: "the 2o3" is now a family.

One honest wrinkle inside that, and it is a vault inference rather than the guideline's claim. Three of the five methods now admissible at the keratinocyte step — KeratinoSens, LuSens and α-Sens® — share the same ARE-Nrf2 luciferase readout. Swapping between them buys supply-chain independence (a different vendor, a different cell bank, in α-Sens®'s case a formulation free of fetal bovine serum). It does not buy mechanistic independence: they measure the same thing the same way. Only EpiSensA and Epi²SensA, which read gene expression in three-dimensional epidermis, change what is being measured. If you are choosing a permutation for robustness, that distinction is the one that matters, and the guideline does not draw it for you. (The KE2 slot's five methods.)

And as of 8 August 2026 the same wrinkle is confirmed one slot along, with numbers attached. A study by the owners of U-SENS™ — the L'Oréal assay admissible at the dendritic-cell step — measured what happens when you actually make that substitution. Two things came out of it.

First, the permutations are not performance-equivalent, and the guideline publishes them as an admissible list rather than a ranked one. Swapping U-SENS™ in for h-CLAT gives 71% balanced accuracy against the mouse test (168 chemicals) and 77% against human data (66 chemicals) — at or below the bottom of the incumbent battery's 72–97% human-data range. Balanced accuracy averages the hit rate on true sensitisers with the hit rate on true non-sensitisers, so a coin flip scores 50%. A number in the low seventies is a long way above chance and a long way from settled. (What the substitution costs.)

Second, and worse for the "independent assays" story: the substitution is the same measurement. Both assays read the same immune-activation marker (CD86, the protein an immune cell displays when it has been switched on) on an immortalised human white-blood-cell line — THP-1 for h-CLAT, U937 for U-SENS™ — for the same step in the same pathway. Two permutations that differ only at this slot look like two different batteries and produce one vote. Neither line has meaningful skin metabolism, so swapping one for the other does nothing about the pro-hapten gap described above. The vault now records this as a correlated blind spot on both assays' maps (h-CLAT · U-SENS™).

The honest soft spots

Say the weakness plainly: specificity is the soft axis. That 50% floor in the human-data range means the 2o3 can call a non-sensitiser a sensitiser — a false alarm, not a missed hazard. For a screening decision that errs on the side of caution that trade-off is defensible; for a decision where over-classification has real cost, it is a number to look at directly rather than around.

And the guideline itself now qualifies how well that number is known. The 2026 edition warns that its reference dataset is imbalanced — more sensitisers than non-sensitisers — so "measures of balanced accuracy are more uncertain, particularly in the case of the human data comparison." The 72–97% range quoted above is a range whose own uncertainty the guideline flags. There is a second, smaller caveat this vault applies to itself: those component and battery figures were read from the 2021 edition and the per-permutation tables in the 2026 text have not been transcribed here. They are reported as the best available numbers, not as verified-current ones.

The second bound is scope: the approach is only validated inside its chemical applicability domain. Out-of-domain substances — unusual chemistries, pre- and pro-haptens that need metabolic activation — still fall back to other methods. And several component assays originated with method developers (Procter & Gamble, Givaudan); what earns them their weight here is that independent bodies (EURL ECVAM, with ESAC peer review) validated them, not the developers' own say-so.

Where the regulatory axis actually sits

The 2o3 is adopted — Guideline 497 exists, and each component method is an OECD Test Guideline. That is a genuine regulatory foothold, not a promise. Two pieces of corroboration have landed since this article was written, and they are worth separating from the OECD's own word:

  • The US FDA's drug centre says it independently. CDER's March 2026 draft guidance states it has accepted non-animal reconstructed-human-epidermis skin-sensitisation methods "to replace the use of animals (e.g., mice in the local lymph node assay)," citing Guideline 497 by name — a different regulator, in a different sector (pharmaceuticals rather than cosmetics or industrial chemicals).
  • The UK put it in the "mature replacement exists" basket in its 2025 national strategy, naming the whole stack with a date attached. Read the wording carefully, though: the target is "By the end of 2026 we will aim to apply only validated alternative methods" — an aim in a non-binding policy paper, widely reported as a mandate (targets are aims, not mandates). The same document also states the residual honestly: animal tests remain in use "only… if these alternative methods do not provide enough data for decisions on the risk of skin sensitisation to be made confidently." That sentence is the live boundary of this defined approach, written by a regulator.

"Adopted" also remains reversible — guidelines get revised, as the 2026 edition demonstrates — and it does not mean every downstream programme in every jurisdiction will take a 2o3 result for every purpose.

Three questions, three guidelines — and the guideline forbids chaining them

Potency is a separate question, and since this article was written the vault has mapped the rest of the family. Guideline 497 now answers three different regulatory questions with three different rules over the same underlying assays:

QuestionWhereWhat comes out
Is it a sensitiser?Part I — the 2o3, this articleSensitiser / non-sensitiser
How strong a sensitiser?Part II — the Integrated Testing StrategyA GHS potency category (1A / 1B / not classified)
At what dose?Part III — the quantitative point of departureA dose

The guideline forbids chaining them. A Part III dose cannot be used to make the Part I hazard call, and a Part II potency result is not a stronger version of a 2o3 answer. They are siblings, not a cascade — which means the 2o3's hazard claim is untouched by anything the other two do, in either direction.

Could a machine run it?

This is the question this vault exists to ask, and it was not in the first version of this article because the vault was not yet asking it. For the 2o3 the answer is yes, and it is one of the cleanest yeses here.

All three readouts are instrument-read — a chromatography peak (DPRA), luminescence (KeratinoSens), flow cytometry (h-CLAT). And the decision rule is not a matter of judgement: the guideline describes it as "a transparent, rule-based approach requiring no expert judgment." Machine-readable measurement plus a fixed, written-down interpretation rule is exactly what this vault grades as closed-loop-ready. There is no methodological blocker. What is missing is loop infrastructure — the plumbing to run three assays, collect three numbers and apply one rule without a person in the middle — not method science.

Four things stop that from reading as an endorsement, and one of them arrived on 8 August 2026 and is the sharpest of the set.

A fixed decision rule is not the same as a determinate one. The rule at the dendritic-cell step is a single number: the treated cells' immune-activation signal, divided by the untreated cells', above 150% means sensitiser. Fixed, published, no expert needed. The assay's own developers then measured how much that reading scatters on repeat runs and found the call is not determinate between 128% and 176% — a 48-point grey zone straddling the adopted threshold, holding 15–17% of chemicals, and reclassifying 35 of 191 chemicals in OECD's own reference database once applied. The adopted rule contains no representation of that zone. A toxicologist reading 151% can see it is a hair over the line and asks for a repeat; a comparison operator returns positive with exactly the same confidence at 151% as at 900%. That is the specific mechanism by which "requires no expert judgment" — literally true, and the foundation of this article's loop-ready rating — stops being reassuring the moment the reader is a machine. (The band the rule does not carry.)

A machine could make the call; that is not the same as a machine that should. The applicability domain is a told constraint, not a measured one: nothing in the assay chain detects that a chemical is a pro-hapten needing metabolic activation and therefore outside what the battery can see. Compare the newest non-animal eye test, which runs a physical pre-test and refuses chemicals it cannot handle. The 2o3 will answer confidently for a chemical it should have declined, and an automated system has no signal that anything went wrong.

The soft specificity gets worse, not better, under automation. A human running a 2o3 result that contradicts everything else known about a chemical will pause. A loop applying the rule will not, and will feed the false positive into whatever it decides next.

And the "three independent assays" framing has more than a seam in it — it has two. Three of the five methods admissible at the keratinocyte step read the same reporter. Both methods admissible at the dendritic-cell step read the same marker on the same class of cell. And all three key events share the metabolic gap described at the top of this article. A battery is only as independent as its measurements, and this one is meaningfully less independent than its structure suggests.

Why that combination is specifically an automation problem, and not merely a science one. A human reading two concordant negatives on a chemical they suspect is a pro-hapten treats the concordance as suspicious. A loop treats it as corroboration, because that is what the rule says it is — and then it iterates. It picks its next experiment on the strength of the call, and the error compounds instead of surfacing. The two batteries this vault grades as clearing end to end are the 2o3 and the phototoxicity battery, and both of them carry a shared-metabolism blind spot. The two things an autonomous lab could actually run today are the two whose internal agreement means least.

What a machine cannot ask: is this even one chemical?

Everything above assumes the thing in the well is one substance. On 20 August 2026 the vault ingested a review that points out this assumption is doing more work than anyone wrote down.

The Botanical Safety Consortium's Dermal Working Group — which exists under a formal agreement between the US Food and Drug Administration, the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences and a health-and-environment research institute — looked at what happens when you run this battery on a botanical: a plant extract, an essential oil, ground plant material. A botanical is not a chemical. It is a shifting mixture of hundreds of things whose composition changes with the cultivar, the plant part, the weather and the extraction method. Their example: bergamot oil pressed cold keeps the light-activated compounds that make it a phototoxicity risk; bergamot oil steam-distilled strips them out. Same name on the label, different hazard in the bottle.

Their finding, in their own words: "Because these approaches were primarily developed and validated using single chemicals, the extent to which concordant results across key events support hazard identification for complex botanical mixtures remains an important area of investigation."

Each of the three assays hits a stated wall on mixtures. The protein-binding test cannot even be made up to its prescribed concentration without knowing the substance's molecular weight — and plant tannins strip its test peptides non-specifically, which is exactly the signal the assay reads as a positive. The keratinocyte test is interfered with at the instrument, not the biology: plant-derived oestrogen mimics shut down the light-emitting enzyme the readout depends on, a fact stated in the test's own OECD guideline. The immune-cell test is warned in its own guideline that it can miss greasy, oil-soluble chemicals — which is precisely what an essential oil is. And the published record disagrees with itself in both directions: one study found botanical extracts switching on immune cells that five in-animal non-sensitisers should not have; another found propolis scoring negative in the immune-cell test while the mouse test called it a moderate allergen.

The vault deliberately did not downgrade the automation rating for this, and the reason matters. Nothing here introduces a human judgement step. The readouts are still instrument-read; the rule is still arithmetic. Demoting the automation axis because of a finding about what the rule was tested on would be exactly the axis-mixing this vault exists to avoid. What changed instead is the fine print about which chemicals the rule may be trusted on — and the consequence is worth stating as flatly as possible:

A machine can run this battery on a plant extract today, execute every step correctly, apply the rule correctly, and return a category whose meaning has never been established. The rule does not know what it was tested on.

That is the cleanest example in this whole vault of "a machine could run it" and "a machine should run it" coming apart.

A second way the votes line up, and this one has a citation. The metabolic gap described at the top of this article is still the vault's own inference, awaiting a source. This is a different one and it is sourced: for both cell-based steps — the keratinocyte test and the immune-cell test — the consortium names "the risk that cytotoxic components may mask responses induced by sensitizing constituents." Both assays are read against how many cells are still alive. So a mixture containing something merely poisonous at the concentration where a different component would have registered as an allergen returns two quiet negatives out of three — which the rule scores as a pass. (Cytotoxic masking.)

The gate in front of the gate

If you were going to automate this, you would not run every chemical. You would put a computer model in front to decide which ones are worth the plate. On 21 August 2026 the vault ingested a benchmark showing what that costs.

Researchers tested machine-learning predictors of skin sensitisation against 1,174 chemicals. The published tools ranged from 81% balanced accuracy down to 48.4% — and 50% is what you get by flipping a coin. (One tool reached its number by calling almost everything a sensitiser.) That contrast alone prices what the 2-out-of-3 battery has: an adopted, fixed procedure a machine can execute, in a field where the software alternatives span from useful to worthless.

The transferable warning is about the safety check, not the accuracy. These models carry an applicability-domain check — a guard rail that is supposed to refuse to answer for chemicals unlike anything the model was trained on. It passed 235 of 235 test chemicals, including every one the models got wrong. The reason is structural: the hard cases here are activity cliffs, pairs of chemicals that look nearly identical and behave completely differently. A guard rail built on chemical similarity cannot flag a chemical that is similar to its training data by construction. Any tiered strategy that puts a software prediction in front of this battery inherits that blindness, and inherits it silently. (The guard rail that passed everything.)

The one that works, and how alone it is

On 25 August 2026, eight European risk-assessment scientists published an editorial in Archives of Toxicology arguing that non-animal methods are being oversold. Their central complaint is that there are no agreed principles for how much a non-animal result counts when it has to be combined with everything else known about a chemical. Three questions, they say, come up constantly and have no published answer: when can non-animal data settle a disagreement between other lines of evidence, when is it enough on its own, and how do you compare uncertainty across data types that were never measured the same way? What fills that gap today, in their words: "expert judgement, introducing variability and reducing reproducibility."

Their single named exception is this battery. OECD Guideline 497 — the defined approaches for skin sensitisation — "exemplif[ies] this principle." And then the next sentence:

"However, similar success stories remain limited."

That is worth sitting with, because it reframes what this article has been describing. The 2-out-of-3 is not one good example among many. On the account of people whose job is to sign safety decisions, it is close to the example: one endpoint, out of everything a chemical has to be assessed for, where a written procedure turns test results into a classification without anyone deliberating. Everywhere else, a human decides — which means everywhere else, the automation question this vault asks does not even reach the assay layer, because the step above the assays is not automatable in principle yet.

And one more thing from the same editorial, which is uncomfortable and belongs here rather than buried. Those authors describe regulatory practice as ranking evidence by type: human data first, then animal data, then test-tube data, then chemistry and gene-expression methods, then computer predictions — an ordering they call "deeply embedded in legislation and guidance." Their summary of what that does: "NAMs are expected to replace animal testing, yet they are positioned in a way that prevents them from doing so."

For this battery specifically, that pressure is at its weakest — Guideline 497 exists precisely to let the non-animal result stand as the answer, and the US drug regulator has said in writing that it accepts these methods in place of the mouse test. But it is a reminder about what "loop-ready" buys you. Automating an assay makes it faster and cheaper and more reproducible. It does not move that assay up the evidence ordering, because the ordering is indexed on what kind of system produced the data, not on how carefully it was produced. A perfectly-run robot doing test-tube work is still doing test-tube work. (The evidence hierarchy · no published rule.)

Bottom line, and it has moved. For skin-sensitisation hazard identification, in-domain, the 2o3 defined approach is still about as trustworthy as a non-animal method gets today: strong on scientific validity (regulatorily adopted, and the science behind each component was validated by independent bodies rather than by the developers), strong on regulatory acceptance (OECD Guideline 497, independently corroborated by the US drug regulator), and technically automatable end to end. What has changed is the automation story, not the science. The known specificity limit is now joined by five things a machine cannot see for itself: an applicability domain it cannot check, a documented grey zone around a threshold it will read as a hard line, a set of "independent" components that share a failure mode, no way to ask whether the thing in the well is one substance or several hundred, and no software guard rail in front of it that can refuse the cases it gets wrong. None of those makes the battery worse science. All of them make "a machine could run it" a narrower statement than it sounds. This is a learning synthesis of the public evidence, not regulatory advice: it organises what the guidance and validation data say, it does not clear the method for any real testing programme, and it does not certify it as safe to automate. Read the method map for the dated through-line as the evidence keeps moving.
