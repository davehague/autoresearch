Skin sensitisation without the animal: how the 2-out-of-3 defined approach actually holds up

Is there a non-animal method for skin-sensitisation hazard you can actually rely on, and for what? For this one endpoint, yes — with specific caveats worth understanding. The OECD's 2-out-of-3 (2o3) defined approach, codified in Guideline 497, is a fixed, rule-based combination of non-animal assays that replaces the animal test (the mouse local lymph node assay) for calling a chemical a sensitiser or non-sensitiser — within a defined chemical applicability domain. It is the clearest "this NAM is ready" case in this vault, and it is the only one that is strong on all three axes this vault tracks: scientific validity (regulatory-adopted), regulatory acceptance (OECD-TG-adopted), and loop-readiness (closed-loop-ready — a machine could run it end to end). Those three are not the same thing, and keeping them apart is the whole point of reading a method honestly.

Refreshed 2026-07-24. Three things have moved since this article was first written: the guideline itself was reissued, the battery stopped being a fixed set of three assays, and this vault started asking a third question about every method — could a machine run it?

What the "battery" actually is

The 2o3 is not a single clever assay — it is a rule applied to assays reading different steps of the biological chain that turns a chemical into an allergen (the skin-sensitisation Adverse Outcome Pathway). Take two concordant results out of three key events and you have your hazard call. The three original methods:

  • DPRA (OECD TG 442C) — the molecular initiating event: does the chemical covalently bind protein? Roughly 80% accuracy, 80% sensitivity, 77% specificity against reference data.
  • KeratinoSens (OECD TG 442D) — the keratinocyte-activation step, via an ARE-Nrf2 luciferase reporter. About 77% accuracy, 78% sensitivity, 76% specificity versus the animal test.
  • h-CLAT (OECD TG 442E) — the dendritic-cell-activation step, read as CD86/CD54 up-regulation. About 85% accuracy, 93% sensitivity, 66% specificity versus the animal test.

None of the three is a stand-alone animal replacement, and none is meant to be. That is the useful insight the component numbers give you: h-CLAT almost never misses a real sensitiser (93% sensitivity) but over-calls non-sensitisers (66% specificity); DPRA and KeratinoSens are the other way round — steadier on specificity. The 2o3 combines them precisely so the battery's blind spots don't line up. Against human data the combined approach lands at 72–97% balanced accuracy, 84–97% sensitivity, and 50–100% specificity.

What changed: the battery is now a rule with an admissible list, not a fixed triple

The version of Guideline 497 this article originally described was the 2021 edition. The current edition is 2 July 2026, and the most consequential change is structural rather than numerical: each key-event slot now admits alternative information sources. ADRA and kDPRA can stand in for DPRA at the protein-binding step; LuSens, EpiSensA, Epi²SensA and α-Sens® can stand in for KeratinoSens at the keratinocyte step; U-SENS™ and GARD®skin at the dendritic-cell step. Each admissible combination is a permutation of the 2o3 with its own performance table in the guideline's supporting annexes.

So what actually carries the adopted regulatory standing is a data-interpretation procedure plus a list of acceptable inputs — not three named assays. That is a more durable object than a fixed triple, and a more slippery one to cite: "the 2o3" is now a family.

One honest wrinkle inside that, and it is a vault inference rather than the guideline's claim. Three of the five methods now admissible at the keratinocyte step — KeratinoSens, LuSens and α-Sens® — share the same ARE-Nrf2 luciferase readout. Swapping between them buys supply-chain independence (a different vendor, a different cell bank, in α-Sens®'s case a formulation free of fetal bovine serum). It does not buy mechanistic independence: they measure the same thing the same way. Only EpiSensA and Epi²SensA, which read gene expression in three-dimensional epidermis, change what is being measured. If you are choosing a permutation for robustness, that distinction is the one that matters, and the guideline does not draw it for you. (The KE2 slot's five methods.)

The honest soft spots

Say the weakness plainly: specificity is the soft axis. That 50% floor in the human-data range means the 2o3 can call a non-sensitiser a sensitiser — a false alarm, not a missed hazard. For a screening decision that errs on the side of caution that trade-off is defensible; for a decision where over-classification has real cost, it is a number to look at directly rather than around.

And the guideline itself now qualifies how well that number is known. The 2026 edition warns that its reference dataset is imbalanced — more sensitisers than non-sensitisers — so "measures of balanced accuracy are more uncertain, particularly in the case of the human data comparison." The 72–97% range quoted above is a range whose own uncertainty the guideline flags. There is a second, smaller caveat this vault applies to itself: those component and battery figures were read from the 2021 edition and the per-permutation tables in the 2026 text have not been transcribed here. They are reported as the best available numbers, not as verified-current ones.

The second bound is scope: the approach is only validated inside its chemical applicability domain. Out-of-domain substances — unusual chemistries, pre- and pro-haptens that need metabolic activation — still fall back to other methods. And several component assays originated with method developers (Procter & Gamble, Givaudan); what earns them their weight here is that independent bodies (EURL ECVAM, with ESAC peer review) validated them, not the developers' own say-so.

Where the regulatory axis actually sits

The 2o3 is adopted — Guideline 497 exists, and each component method is an OECD Test Guideline. That is a genuine regulatory foothold, not a promise. Two pieces of corroboration have landed since this article was written, and they are worth separating from the OECD's own word:

  • The US FDA's drug centre says it independently. CDER's March 2026 draft guidance states it has accepted non-animal reconstructed-human-epidermis skin-sensitisation methods "to replace the use of animals (e.g., mice in the local lymph node assay)," citing Guideline 497 by name — a different regulator, in a different sector (pharmaceuticals rather than cosmetics or industrial chemicals).
  • The UK put it in the "mature replacement exists" basket in its 2025 national strategy, naming the whole stack with a date attached. Read the wording carefully, though: the target is "By the end of 2026 we will aim to apply only validated alternative methods" — an aim in a non-binding policy paper, widely reported as a mandate (targets are aims, not mandates). The same document also states the residual honestly: animal tests remain in use "only… if these alternative methods do not provide enough data for decisions on the risk of skin sensitisation to be made confidently." That sentence is the live boundary of this defined approach, written by a regulator.

"Adopted" also remains reversible — guidelines get revised, as the 2026 edition demonstrates — and it does not mean every downstream programme in every jurisdiction will take a 2o3 result for every purpose.

Three questions, three guidelines — and the guideline forbids chaining them

Potency is a separate question, and since this article was written the vault has mapped the rest of the family. Guideline 497 now answers three different regulatory questions with three different rules over the same underlying assays:

QuestionWhereWhat comes out
Is it a sensitiser?Part I — the 2o3, this articleSensitiser / non-sensitiser
How strong a sensitiser?Part II — the Integrated Testing StrategyA GHS potency category (1A / 1B / not classified)
At what dose?Part III — the quantitative point of departureA dose

The guideline forbids chaining them. A Part III dose cannot be used to make the Part I hazard call, and a Part II potency result is not a stronger version of a 2o3 answer. They are siblings, not a cascade — which means the 2o3's hazard claim is untouched by anything the other two do, in either direction.

Could a machine run it?

This is the question this vault exists to ask, and it was not in the first version of this article because the vault was not yet asking it. For the 2o3 the answer is yes, and it is one of the cleanest yeses here.

All three readouts are instrument-read — a chromatography peak (DPRA), luminescence (KeratinoSens), flow cytometry (h-CLAT). And the decision rule is not a matter of judgement: the guideline describes it as "a transparent, rule-based approach requiring no expert judgment." Machine-readable measurement plus a fixed, written-down interpretation rule is exactly what this vault grades as closed-loop-ready. There is no methodological blocker. What is missing is loop infrastructure — the plumbing to run three assays, collect three numbers and apply one rule without a person in the middle — not method science.

Three things stop that from reading as an endorsement.

A machine could make the call; that is not the same as a machine that should. The applicability domain is a told constraint, not a measured one: nothing in the assay chain detects that a chemical is a pro-hapten needing metabolic activation and therefore outside what the battery can see. Compare the newest non-animal eye test, which runs a physical pre-test and refuses chemicals it cannot handle. The 2o3 will answer confidently for a chemical it should have declined, and an automated system has no signal that anything went wrong.

The soft specificity gets worse, not better, under automation. A human running a 2o3 result that contradicts everything else known about a chemical will pause. A loop applying the rule will not, and will feed the false positive into whatever it decides next.

And the "three independent assays" framing has a seam in it. Three of the five methods now admissible at the keratinocyte step read the same reporter. A battery is only as independent as its measurements, and this one is slightly less independent than its structure suggests.

Bottom line. For skin-sensitisation hazard identification, in-domain, the 2o3 defined approach is about as trustworthy as a non-animal method gets today — strong on scientific validity, strong on regulatory acceptance, and technically automatable end to end, with a known and stated specificity limit and an applicability domain a machine cannot check for itself. This is a learning synthesis of the public evidence, not regulatory advice: it organises what the guidance and validation data say, it does not clear the method for any real testing programme, and it does not certify it as safe to automate.
