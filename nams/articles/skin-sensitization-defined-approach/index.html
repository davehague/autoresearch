Skin sensitisation without the animal: how the 2-out-of-3 defined approach actually holds up

Is there a non-animal method for skin-sensitisation hazard you can actually rely on, and for what? For this one endpoint, yes — with a specific caveat worth understanding. The OECD's 2-out-of-3 (2o3) defined approach, codified in Guideline 497, is a fixed, rule-based combination of three non-animal assays that replaces the animal test (the LLNA) for calling a chemical a sensitiser or non-sensitiser — within a defined chemical applicability domain. It is the clearest "this NAM is ready" case in this vault, adopted on both the axes that matter: scientific validity (regulatory-adopted) and regulatory acceptance (OECD-TG-adopted). Those two are not the same thing, and keeping them apart is the whole point of reading a method honestly.

What the "battery" actually is

The 2o3 is not a single clever assay — it is three assays, each reading a different step of the biological chain that turns a chemical into an allergen (the skin-sensitisation Adverse Outcome Pathway). Take two concordant results out of three and you have your hazard call:

  • DPRA (OECD TG 442C) — the molecular initiating event: does the chemical covalently bind protein? Roughly 80% accuracy, 80% sensitivity, 77% specificity against reference data.
  • KeratinoSens (OECD TG 442D) — the keratinocyte-activation step, via an ARE-Nrf2 luciferase reporter. About 77% accuracy, 78% sensitivity, 76% specificity versus the LLNA.
  • h-CLAT (OECD TG 442E) — the dendritic-cell-activation step, read as CD86/CD54 up-regulation. About 85% accuracy, 93% sensitivity, 66% specificity versus the LLNA.

None of the three is a stand-alone animal replacement, and none is meant to be. That is the useful insight the component numbers give you: h-CLAT almost never misses a real sensitiser (93% sensitivity) but over-calls non-sensitisers (66% specificity); DPRA and KeratinoSens are the other way round — steadier on specificity. The 2o3 combines them precisely so the battery's blind spots don't line up. Against human data the combined approach lands at 72–97% balanced accuracy, 84–97% sensitivity, and 50–100% specificity.

The honest soft spot

Say the weakness plainly: specificity is the soft axis. That 50% floor in the human-data range means the 2o3 can call a non-sensitiser a sensitiser — a false alarm, not a missed hazard. For a screening decision that errs on the side of caution that trade-off is defensible; for a decision where over-classification has real cost, it is a number to look at directly rather than around. The second bound is scope: the approach is only validated inside its chemical applicability domain. Out-of-domain substances — unusual chemistries, pre/pro-haptens that need metabolic activation — still fall back to other methods. And two of the three component assays originated with method developers (P&G, Givaudan); what earns them their weight here is that independent bodies (EURL ECVAM, with ESAC peer review) validated them, not the developers' own say-so.

Where the regulatory axis actually sits

This is the part it is easy to overstate, so: the 2o3 is adopted — OECD Guideline 497 exists, and the three component methods are each an OECD Test Guideline. That is a genuine regulatory foothold, not a promise. It is also reversible: guidelines get revised, and the key-event slots are still moving (OECD ran 2025 consultations on new keratinocyte-activation methods). "Adopted" means a Test Guideline exists for the hazard call in-domain — it does not mean every downstream program, in every jurisdiction, will take a 2o3 result for every purpose. Potency is a separate question entirely: the sub-categorisation (how strong a sensitiser) runs through a different defined approach in the same guideline, not the 2o3.

Bottom line. For skin-sensitisation hazard identification, in-domain, the 2o3 defined approach is about as trustworthy as a non-animal method gets today — strong on both the validity and the acceptance axis, with a known and stated specificity limit. This is a learning synthesis of the public evidence, not regulatory advice: it organises what the guidance and validation data say, and it does not clear the method for any real testing program.
