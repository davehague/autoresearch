Predicting toxicity from a chemical's structure: where (Q)SAR and read-across can actually stand alone

Can a computer predict whether a chemical is toxic well enough to skip the test — and would a regulator accept it? The honest answer is "almost never on its own, with exactly one important exception." For most endpoints, in-silico methods are a supporting input you fold into a larger weight-of-evidence case, not a replacement for a test. But in one bounded, high-value domain — screening pharmaceutical impurities for mutagenicity — a purely computational assessment is an accepted stand-alone substitute for an actual assay. That gap between "helpful context" and "accepted replacement" is the whole story of the vault's first computational method, and it's the sharpest example here of the two axes this vault tracks — is the science valid versus is it regulatorily accepted — pulling apart.

What these methods actually are

Two techniques sit under this map. (Q)SAR — a quantitative structure–activity relationship model — predicts a toxicological endpoint from a molecule's structure and computed descriptors, learned from a training set of chemicals with known outcomes. Read-across is the more hand-built cousin: infer a data-poor chemical's toxicity from structurally and mechanistically similar, data-rich analogues (grouping chemicals into a "category" and reading the missing value across from its neighbours). Both are purely computational — no cells, no animals, no wet assay at the moment of prediction. The free OECD QSAR Toolbox is the reference platform for building categories and running read-across; commercial systems (Lhasa's Derek and Sarah Nexus, MultiCASE, Leadscope) cover the (Q)SAR side.

Because there's no wet step to reproduce, the discipline lives entirely in the method's construction. The OECD principles for (Q)SAR validation are the guardrails: a defined endpoint, an unambiguous algorithm, a defined applicability domain, appropriate predictivity, and — where possible — a mechanistic interpretation. The applicability domain is the load-bearing one. A model is only trustworthy for chemicals that look like what it was trained on; a prediction for a structure outside that domain is not evidence, it's extrapolation. That single caveat is why, for most endpoints, these methods read within a weight-of-evidence rather than alone.

The broad science axis: real, but a supporting tool

Graded as a general method class, in-silico prediction is honestly the vault's lowest evidence tier — in-silico-mechanistic: structure-based reasoning without wet validation of the specific prediction. That is not a criticism, it's a description. Regulatory frameworks reflect it exactly. Under REACH (Annex XI), a registrant may use (Q)SAR or read-across in place of a study, but only under conditions — an adequate and reliable model, results documented, the substance inside the applicability domain — and typically as part of a weight-of-evidence argument rather than a solo data point. So for the broad class this vault marks regulatory_status: proposed / accepted-within-an-integrated-assessment. Useful, increasingly standard for prioritisation and data-gap filling, but not a stand-alone replacement for a test across the board.

The one place it stands alone: ICH M7

Here is the exception worth the whole article. ICH M7 — the pharmaceutical guideline for assessing and controlling DNA-reactive (mutagenic) impurities — accepts, when no experimental data exist, an in-silico assessment of bacterial mutagenicity in place of running the test. The requirement is specific: two complementary (Q)SAR methodologies, one expert rule-based (structural alerts) and one statistical, plus expert review to resolve ambiguous or conflicting calls. If both come back negative and the expert review concurs, the impurity can be treated as non-mutagenic without running the Ames test (the bacterial reverse-mutation assay, OECD TG 471).

That is a genuine, stand-alone, regulatory-accepted use of computation replacing an adopted wet assay in a defined domain — so in this narrow slice the vault grades it up to evidence_tier: regulatory-adopted and an adopted regulatory status, deliberately higher than the broad class. The guideline's own logic is telling: it judges the combined two-method-plus-expert-review accuracy as comparable to the reproducibility of the Ames test itself. The reason it works here and not everywhere is that this is the most tractable possible target — a single, well-understood, heavily-databased endpoint (bacterial point mutation) with decades of training data behind both model types.

One load-bearing honesty flag: the two-methodology approach is operationalised by commercial platforms, and vendor-reported performance figures carry an industry funding interest — so this vault tags those claims funding_flag: industry and leans on the guideline framework, which is public and vendor-neutral, rather than on any one product's marketing.

How it sits in the rest of the vault

In-silico is the vault's second cross-cutting method, alongside PBK/QIVIVE. Where PBK is the toxicokinetic connective tissue — turning an in-vitro concentration into a human dose — (Q)SAR and read-across are the structure-based connective tissue that feeds every endpoint upstream. In practice it's the customary tier-1 screen ahead of the wet genotoxicity battery — structural alerts prioritise what gets tested in the mammalian gene-mutation and micronucleus assays — and it supplies the structural-alert half of the carcinogenicity picture that the cell transformation assays complete for non-genotoxic carcinogens.

Bottom line. In-silico (Q)SAR and read-across are the real, standardised, computational floor of the NAM toolkit — governed by the OECD validation principles and increasingly accepted as supporting evidence within an integrated assessment (in-silico-mechanistic / proposed, applicability-domain-bounded). For most endpoints, treat a prediction as one input to a weight-of-evidence case, never as a cleared substitute for a test — and never trust one outside its applicability domain. The exception is real and specific: under ICH M7, two complementary (Q)SAR methods plus expert review are an accepted stand-alone replacement for the Ames test for pharmaceutical-impurity mutagenicity — the cleanest case in this vault of computation replacing a wet assay. This is a learning synthesis of the public evidence, not regulatory advice. Read the method map for the through-line as endpoint-specific (Q)SAR validation keeps maturing — the thing worth watching is whether any second endpoint ever earns an ICH-M7-style stand-alone acceptance.
