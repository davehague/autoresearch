Predicting toxicity from a chemical's structure: where (Q)SAR and read-across can actually stand alone

Can a computer predict whether a chemical is toxic well enough to skip the test — and would a regulator accept it? The honest answer is "almost never on its own, in a growing number of carefully bounded exceptions." For most endpoints, in-silico methods are a supporting input you fold into a larger weight-of-evidence case, not a replacement for a test. But in several bounded, high-value domains — screening pharmaceutical impurities for mutagenicity, scoring one leg of an adopted skin-sensitisation procedure, and now a route into medicines submissions that never mentions test guidelines at all — a purely computational assessment carries real regulatory weight.

That gap between "helpful context" and "accepted replacement" is the whole story of the vault's first computational method, and it is the sharpest example here of the axes this vault tracks — is the science valid, is it regulatorily accepted, and could a machine run it — pulling apart from one another.

Where this piece stands after seven weeks of new evidence: the science axis has not moved, the acceptance axis has moved three times, and eight results have arrived that change how you should read a computational result at all — which score a model paper reports, what happens when you combine several of these tools by vote, what a published rule for refusing to answer looks like, how a training-set check gets written up as a verification, what it means when the model itself sits behind a login, that the standard applicability-domain check passes the chemicals the model gets wrong, that the login barrier now has a free open-source counter-example which fills its own inputs with guesses, and that the plain-English explanation printed beside a prediction carries no accuracy figure at all.

What these methods actually are

Two techniques sit under this map. (Q)SAR — a quantitative structure–activity relationship model — predicts a toxicological endpoint from a molecule's structure and computed descriptors, learned from a training set of chemicals with known outcomes. Read-across is the more hand-built cousin: infer a data-poor chemical's toxicity from structurally and mechanistically similar, data-rich analogues (grouping chemicals into a "category" and reading the missing value across from its neighbours). Both are purely computational — no cells, no animals, no wet assay at the moment of prediction. The free OECD QSAR Toolbox is the reference platform for building categories and running read-across; commercial systems (Lhasa's Derek and Sarah Nexus, MultiCASE, Leadscope) cover the (Q)SAR side.

Because there's no wet step to reproduce, the discipline lives entirely in the method's construction. The OECD principles for (Q)SAR validation are the guardrails: a defined endpoint, an unambiguous algorithm, a defined applicability domain, appropriate predictivity, and — where possible — a mechanistic interpretation. The applicability domain is the load-bearing one. A model is only trustworthy for chemicals that look like what it was trained on; a prediction for a structure outside that domain is not evidence, it's extrapolation. That single caveat is why, for most endpoints, these methods read within a weight-of-evidence rather than alone.

The broad science axis: real, but a supporting tool

Graded as a general method class, in-silico prediction is honestly the vault's lowest evidence tier — in-silico-mechanistic: structure-based reasoning without wet validation of the specific prediction. That is not a criticism, it's a description. Regulatory frameworks reflect it exactly. Under REACH — the European Union's registration and evaluation regulation for industrial chemicals — a registrant may use (Q)SAR or read-across in place of a study (Annex XI), but only under conditions: an adequate and reliable model, results documented, the substance inside the applicability domain, and typically as part of a weight-of-evidence argument rather than a solo data point.

Three regulatory standings at once, and now a fourth door

The interesting thing about this method class is that it does not have a regulatory status. It has several, in different bounded domains, and they were acquired one at a time.

1. The broad class: supporting evidence inside an integrated assessment. regulatory_status: proposed. Useful, increasingly standard for prioritisation and data-gap filling, not a stand-alone replacement across the board.

2. A stand-alone replacement for one wet assay. ICH M7 — the international pharmaceutical guideline for controlling DNA-reactive (mutagenic) impurities — accepts, when no experimental data exist, an in-silico assessment of bacterial mutagenicity in place of running the test. The requirement is specific: two complementary (Q)SAR methodologies, one expert rule-based (structural alerts) and one statistical, plus expert review to resolve conflicting calls. If both come back negative and the expert review concurs, the impurity is treated as non-mutagenic without running the Ames test — the bacterial reverse-mutation assay, OECD Test Guideline 471, which grows bacteria that cannot make an essential nutrient and counts how many regain the ability, a proxy for DNA damage.

In this narrow slice the vault grades the method up to evidence_tier: regulatory-adopted, deliberately higher than the broad class. The guideline's own logic is telling: it judges the combined two-method-plus-expert-review accuracy as comparable to the reproducibility of the Ames test itself — i.e. the bar it clears is not "perfect" but "no worse than the wet assay's own run-to-run variation." That is the right way to state a replacement claim, and it works here because bacterial point mutation is the most tractable possible target: one well-understood endpoint with decades of training data behind both model types.

3. A scored component inside an adopted wet-assay procedure. Found in July 2026 and new to this vault: under OECD Guideline 497 — the adopted defined approaches for skin sensitisation, i.e. whether a chemical can make someone allergic to it — Derek Nexus and the OECD QSAR Toolbox are not merely tools used alongside the procedure. Each supplies a scored 0-or-1 information source inside it, one of the three legs of its 0–7 point total. A computational prediction is a graded input to an adopted regulatory decision rule.

4. A door that never mentions test guidelines — new, 29 July 2026. ICH M15 is the international guideline on model-informed drug development: using computer models of a drug and of the body to answer questions that would otherwise need an experiment. Through it, model-derived evidence can already support first-in-human dose justification, bridging pharmacokinetics between formulations, drug–drug interaction assessment, and extrapolation to children and pregnant women. Alongside it, the EU Pharma Package (approved 2026) writes "in vitro, ex vivo, in silico, or in chemico" into the legal definition of a non-clinical study.

Why that matters for how you read this whole vault: the acceptance ladder used here — does an OECD Test Guideline exist? — was built to grade assays. A model can score regulatory_status: none on that ladder and still have a live, well-defined acceptance route. Nothing about this method's grade changes; the way to read the grade does. The evidence is a four-author perspective paper with no data, two of whose authors are national medicines-regulator staff arguing for machinery they help run — so treat it as a map of the instruments, not as proof that any submission has succeeded on model evidence. None is shown here.

The pattern across all four: computation gets adopted in bounded domains, one at a time, and the boundaries are drawn tightly on purpose. The thing worth watching remains whether a second toxicological endpoint ever earns an ICH-M7-style stand-alone acceptance.

Could a machine run it? The axis this article originally skipped

This map is graded loop_readiness: computational-connective, which is scored off the automation ladder rather than on it. The reason: this is not a physical assay. There is no plate, no robot, no readout. It is the model layer that a closed loop would update — the thing on the other side of the experiment from the instrument.

So the question "could a machine run it?" is malformed here. A machine already runs it; that is all it is. The useful questions are the two underneath:

  • Would an automated system know when not to trust it? Only if the applicability domain is enforced mechanically rather than stated in prose. A prediction outside the training set's chemical space is not a weak result, it is not a result.
  • Does its acceptance rise or fall as the human steps back? It falls. Under ICH M7 the stand-alone route requires expert review as a named component — the human is written into the accepted procedure. And under ICH M15, an AI model brought into a submission needs "rigorous assessment of model risk, comprehensive documentation of training data and validation performance," scaled up "particularly for high-impact decisions." That is the same proportionality this vault recorded from the US Food and Drug Administration in July 2026: the more alone the model is in a decision, the heavier the evidence it must carry.

The practical design advice that comes with it is worth repeating because it is architectural rather than bureaucratic: pair the learned model with a mechanistic one — a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model, say — "to preserve biological interpretability." A learned component constrained by a causal one, not a single learned function.

Two results that should make you more careful, not less

A deep-learning attempt on the hardest endpoint, and the arithmetic underneath it. In July 2026 a group trained graph neural networks on 23 US EPA ToxCast bioassays to predict developmental and reproductive toxicity, and validated against an in-vivo reference set of 90 chemicals. Reported: F1 0.68, recall 0.57, precision 0.93, specificity 0.81, accuracy 0.61. In plain terms — when it says a chemical is a reproductive toxicant it is right about 93% of the time, but it misses 43% of the chemicals that really are one. For a first-tier screen, whose whole job is a trustworthy negative, that is the wrong direction. And the reference set was 75 positives to 16 negatives, roughly 83% positive, so a model that simply answered "toxic" every time would score about 0.91 on the same F1 measure — better than the model's 0.68. The paper computes no such baseline and its abstract describes the result as "balanced predictive performance." That baseline arithmetic is this vault's inference from the authors' own published numbers, flagged as an inference. Multi-task learning, the paper's headline feature, gave no meaningful gain, which the authors report honestly.

A named way these models can go wrong. A commentary in July 2026 argues that models trained on high-throughput screening hit-calls, without the screening programme's own counter-screen flags, "can learn how the assay behaved rather than how the chemical harms" — and that this is specifically a structure-based-model problem, "because the confounders are structurally systematic." Autofluorescence, reporter-enzyme inhibition and general cytotoxicity are simpler functions of molecular structure than real biology is, so a (Q)SAR learns them better than it learns the signal. It is a commentary with no data and it names no model as wrong; it is a hypothesis worth designing against.

The number that reframes the comparison

Here is the result from 29 July 2026 that most changes how this method should be positioned.

The US federal Tox21 programme — the robotic screening effort that runs ~10,000 chemicals against a fixed panel of cell-based assays — expanded that panel from 36 biological targets to 49, and asked whether the extra biology improved prediction of two toxicities that matter commercially: liver injury and cardiotoxicity from drugs. They trained models three ways: on assay data alone, on chemical structure alone, and on both.

What the model was givenLiver injuryCardiotoxicity
structure plus 49 assays0.740.82
structure alone0.730.80

Those are AUC-ROC scores, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect. A model that never touched a cell scored within one to two points of the model that consumed 262 robotic assay readouts — a gap inside the reported error bars. And the assay data on its own lost to structure alone, on both endpoints.

The authors' explanation is unusually generous to the computational side, and it is worth quoting because it comes from a screening programme: a structure model "relies exclusively on chemical structure information, making it less susceptible to such variability" — the variability being "changes in experimental conditions, technical noise, and batch effects."

Resist the obvious conclusion. This is not evidence that computation beats biology. It is one modelling study, on two endpoints, inside one chemical library, with reference labels compiled by an automated join over adverse-event databases rather than measured. And it cuts both ways: this is the same corpus the commentary above says teaches structure-based models the assay rather than the hazard. A structure model that matches the assay panel it was trained beside is consistent with the model being good and with the model having learned the panel. Nothing here separates those two readings.

What it does establish is a floor for honest comparison. Any wet method proposing to predict drug-induced liver injury now has a published cheap comparator to beat, and it is 0.73 — not zero.

Two results from August 2026 about how to read a computational result

Both landed on 5 August 2026, from unrelated groups, and neither is about whether these models work. They are about how their results get reported and combined — which is the part a person deciding whether to trust one actually has to navigate.

The score that improved is not the score a decision uses

A group added semantic context to a structure-based model: alongside the molecule itself, the model was given a knowledge graph — a network wiring each chemical to the genes, proteins and pathways that published studies associate with it. The idea is sound and the execution is careful: two architectures, five random runs each, benchmarked against a plain random forest on 37 assays from the Tox21 robotic screening panel.

The headline improved, clearly and consistently.

ModelAUROC
Semantic graph network0.894
Joint molecular + semantic0.890
Gradient boosting (baseline)0.855
Fine-tuned structure-only network0.849
Random forest (baseline)0.845

AUROC answers: pick one truly toxic chemical and one truly safe one at random — how often does the model score the toxic one higher? So this is roughly 85 times in 100 rising to 89 times in 100, significant in every pairwise comparison, and the semantic models took the top spot in 33 of the 37 assays.

Then look at the metrics that describe acting on the answer. Precision–recall (AUPRC) and the Matthews correlation coefficient both ask the question a screening programme lives on: of the chemicals you flag, how many are really hazardous, and how many real ones did you miss? On those, the semantic network scored 0.443 against the random forest's 0.479, and 0.382 against 0.431 — significantly worse. The joint variant recovered to roughly a tie. It never won.

The authors say so themselves: "semantic models improve AUROC but do not always improve AUPRC or MCC."

Why the two come apart here, and why it will keep happening. Twelve of these 37 assays have fewer than 5% active compounds. When the thing you are hunting is that rare, AUROC is dominated by how well the model orders the enormous inactive majority — which is not the job. The decision metrics are unforgiving of exactly the errors that matter.

And the mechanism was measured rather than guessed, which is the part worth carrying away from this whole piece. Across the 37 assays, the chemicals that came back active had lower median connectivity in the knowledge graph than the inactive ones — in 36 of 37 assays, and in 11 of the 12 most lopsided ones. A knowledge graph is a record of what has been studied, not of what is true. A compound is sparsely connected because nobody looked at it, and nobody looked at it is uncorrelated with it is safe. So the extra context enriches the well-studied safe majority and thins out exactly where the hazards are. Unsurprisingly, expanding the graph further outward — adding pathway entities — gave no gain at all.

Two caveats, both stated by the authors: the absolute gains are "overall, modest", and the senior author built the knowledge graph being evaluated. Read the candour as the mitigation it is, not as a clearance.

Six tools voting together are not six opinions

A screening workflow for cosmetic ingredients ran nine organic UV filters — sunscreen ingredients — through two layers. The computational layer was six free structure-prediction tools (including the OECD QSAR Toolbox) combined by majority consensus. The assay layer was existing robotic screening data from the US EPA's ToxCast programme, available for four of the nine.

The consensus flagged hormone-receptor activity in one chemical. The assay data flagged it in three of the four it covered — including two the consensus had cleared.

The structural point is not that the models were wrong. It is that six structure-based tools trained on overlapping public datasets, encoding overlapping structural alerts, are not six independent votes. A hazard that is not visible in a molecule's structure — or is visible only after the body metabolises the chemical into something else — is invisible to all six for one shared reason. Majority voting then converts a shared blind spot into apparent agreement, which is worse than a single tool being wrong, because agreement reads as confidence.

This matters directly for one of the adopted uses described above: under the skin-sensitisation defined approaches, an in-silico prediction from either Derek Nexus or the OECD QSAR Toolbox scores one leg of the potency total. The guideline treats them as interchangeable. Interchangeable and independent are different claims, and nobody has tested the second.

The honest counter-reading, which has to travel with it. ToxCast's receptor-antagonist readouts are confounded by ordinary cytotoxicity — a chemical that kills the cell also switches off the reporter and looks like a blocker. Whether those two calls survive a counter-screen is not stated, and the paper's full text is behind a subscription. So the disagreement between the layers is documented; which layer is right is not. And the bias is worth saying plainly: three of the seven authors work in product safety at a cosmetics manufacturer, and the paper concludes that four of the nine ingredients have acceptable environmental margins.

Three more results, from the second half of August 2026

These landed after the last revision of this piece and they pull in different directions — one is the best thing this article has to report about how a computational method should behave, one is the worst, and the third is a barrier that has nothing to do with the science.

A model that publishes the arithmetic for saying "I don't know"

This is the most useful single design in the article, and it is worth stating why before the numbers.

Everywhere else in this vault, the property that separates a method a machine could run end to end from one it cannot is whether somebody published a fixed decision rule — an arithmetic procedure converting the measurement into a call. Roughly two dozen methods here produce a perfectly clean number and stop, because nobody has written the rule down.

A model published this month for blood-brain-barrier permeability — whether a chemical can get from the bloodstream into the brain at all, which most chemicals cannot, because the brain's blood vessels are lined with tightly sealed cells and pumps that throw intruders back out — publishes one, and it has a third branch (Chushak et al. 2026, NAM Journal).

Seven separate models vote: three descriptor families crossed with two classical algorithms, plus a graph neural network that learns its own representation of the molecule. The spread of their votes is the confidence measure. Unanimous is 0.00, one dissenter 0.35, two dissenters 0.45, a four-to-three split 0.49. The published rule is: below 0.46, report the answer; at or above it, return "Inconclusive" rather than a classification.

What that buys and costs, in plain numbers:

Catches chemicals that do crossCorrectly clears the ones that don't
Whole test set86%76%
Restricted to what the rule accepts89%79%

— at the price of declining to answer for 12% of the test set (18% in its real application). Headline performance on the honest split, where molecules sharing a chemical skeleton are forced out of the training set so the test chemistry is genuinely unfamiliar: balanced accuracy 0.81, AUC 0.89 — right about four times in five across the two classes weighted equally.

The applied result is the sort of triage this method class is for. Pointed at 6,031 chemicals that a real robotic screen had already called active against a brain receptor, it predicts 27% of them would never have reached that receptor in a living person. Those are in-vitro hits with nowhere to go, and the model finds them for the cost of a computation.

And the caution has to travel with it, because it is this article's own recurring theme. Seven models trained on one dataset with overlapping descriptor sets are not seven independent opinions. The refusal branch fires on disagreement — so a gap shared by all seven produces a confident answer rather than an Inconclusive one. That is exactly the shape of the six-UV-filter consensus failure described above, with the tools now inside a single method's confidence machinery instead of spread across a workflow. Nobody has measured how far short of independent the seven fall, and the paper does not raise the question.

Evidence tier: in-silico-mechanistic — a computational study, no new measurement. Regulatory status: none. Funding declared as no competing interests, and worth naming anyway: the work is funded by a US Air Force research programme whose project title is "Validating the use of in vitro models for Neurotoxicity." The labels it trains toward come from a laboratory permeability test that the authors themselves describe as variable and — crucially — lacking the active pumps that are the barrier's biological point.

The same paper, four paragraphs later, verifies itself on its own training data

"The screened dataset contained 52 compounds from the modeling set … the model classified all of them correctly. Verified accuracy on known compounds illustrates the high reliability of the consensus model's predictions."

Those 52 compounds are training data. Recovering them shows the model fitted; it says nothing about anything new. It does not touch the scaffold-split numbers above, which are the paper's real result and were produced properly.

It is here because of what surrounds it. Two paragraphs earlier the same authors cite a 2026 analysis finding that near-identical compounds in blood-brain-barrier datasets inflate reported AUC by an average of 13.3%, and de-duplicate their own dataset — 7,807 compounds down to 3,968 — specifically because of it. The discipline and the lapse are adjacent, and the lapse is in the section where the frame has shifted from how good is this model to here is the model being used.

For a reader deciding whether to trust a computational method, the practical lesson is the one this whole article keeps arriving at from different directions: the number a paper puts next to a confident adjective is frequently not the number its protocol earned. Two sentences in one paper, one defensible and one not, with no formatting difference between them.

An access gate, on a model

A cardiac-safety model published on 14 August predicts blockade of three heart ion channels at once and is served only from a company's login-gated web endpoint. This map now carries an access_gate for it, and the reason is not commercial fairness — it is that a model behind a login cannot be version-pinned, re-run against a fixed set of reference chemicals, or audited for which part of its applicability domain a given prediction came from. Every other access gate in this vault blocks running a method: an unobtainable cell line, a single-supplier kit, a licensed software seat. This one blocks checking it, which is a different and worse problem for anything that has to justify a decision to a regulator.

Three results from late August 2026: the guard rail, the free button, and the sentence nobody scored

The applicability-domain check passed every chemical, including the ones the model got wrong

Everything above rests on one safety device. A structure-based model is only trusted inside its applicability domain — the region of chemistry it has seen enough of to be believed — and the standard way to enforce that is a similarity check: if the new chemical looks like the training chemicals, answer; if it does not, decline. That check is the reason a regulator will look at a prediction at all, and it is the mechanism that would let an automated system know when to stop.

A clean benchmark from the Johns Hopkins Center for Alternatives to Animal Testing tested it. 1,174 chemicals labelled for skin allergy, two algorithm families crossed with five ways of describing a molecule, 235 chemicals held out, plus a head-to-head against four published tools. The best model reached 81% balanced accuracy on the held-out set — it catches 85% of real allergens and wrongly flags 23% of safe chemicals — falling to 74% under cross-validation. Respectable, unremarkable, and better than every published comparator tested on the same chemicals.

Then they ran the domain check on the held-out set, and 235 out of 235 chemicals passed it. Including the ones the models got wrong.

The reason is structural rather than a flaw in this implementation. The errors concentrate on activity cliffs — pairs of chemicals that look nearly identical and behave completely differently. A check that measures structural similarity is, by construction, incapable of seeing a case whose defining property is being structurally similar to something with the opposite answer. The "I don't know" signal an automated loop would depend on is silent exactly where it matters.

One more result from the same paper transfers past this dataset. k-nearest neighbours is read-across written as an algorithm — borrow the answer from the structurally closest chemicals that have data — and read-across is a permitted route under European chemicals law. It lost everywhere it was tested (63–73% accuracy), and it failed in the dangerous direction: on compounds carrying two or more reactive structural alerts, the ones a human assessor would look at hardest, 100% of its errors were sensitisers called safe. Feeding it more chemistry made it worse, not better.

The honest scope. One study, one group, one endpoint, 235 test chemicals. And 95% of the labels come from animal studies, with a further 2% assigned from a regulatory classification rather than an experiment — so every "error" here is a disagreement with a mouse, not with a person. What moved is the vault's confidence in the check, not in the models.

The access gate loosened: a free, open-source button that builds these models for you

The article's previous revision recorded a new kind of barrier — a cardiac model served only from behind a company login. That gate is not about cost. It is about the ability to check: a model you cannot version-pin, re-run against a fixed reference set, or interrogate for which part of its domain a prediction came from cannot be audited, whatever its accuracy.

ToxiVerse, from Tulane University, is the opposite arrangement in principle: free, no-code, open-source, with the code public and a live service anyone can use. Three parts — a module that builds chemical descriptors from public bioactivity data, a curated database of roughly 50,000 chemicals across toxicity endpoints, and a module that auto-generates a (Q)SAR model from a dataset you upload. Its stated audience is "researchers who lack programming expertise."

The gate loosens and does not lift. Whether open source delivers auditability in practice depends on release tagging and bulk data download, and neither is stated. One open tool does not undo a class-level barrier.

And it arrives with a new blind spot, one level below the one this article has been tracking. A (Q)SAR is a function from descriptors to a prediction. In this platform some of the descriptors are themselves model predictions, filled in by machine learning where the underlying bioactivity data is missing. So the model and its own inputs fail on the same chemicals for the same reason — sparsity — and a single cross-validation number over the whole pipeline reports that as model quality. Those are precisely the data-poor chemicals a structure-based prediction exists to serve.

Put the two results in this section together and the shape is uncomfortable: a platform that generates models for non-programmers will generate, at scale, exactly the applicability-domain hole the previous subsection showed cannot see its own worst errors. For a tool whose product is predictions, the publication reports no accuracy, no cross-validation, no benchmark, no comparison to an existing tool, and no applicability-domain policy.

The number is scored and the sentence beside it is not

A second blood–brain-barrier model landed three days after the one this article already covered, with a better headline: 93.72% area under the curve (roughly 94 out of 100 orderings right, when asked to rank a crosser above a non-crosser) and 89.19% recall — catching about 89 of every 100 chemicals that really do reach the brain and missing about 11, which for a filter standing in front of neurotoxicity testing are the ones screened out and never tested. Precision and specificity are not reported, so how many safe chemicals it wrongly promotes is unknown, and the wet-lab confirmation is eleven chemicals, which cannot support any error-rate estimate at all. To its credit it reports a scaffold split — testing on structural families the model never saw — which is the honest choice and makes the number a materially stronger claim than the same figure under an easy split.

The finding is not the number. It is the packaging. The model's predictions are "supplemented with natural-language explanations generated from a large language model." Every accuracy figure in the paper belongs to the predictor. There is no faithfulness check on the explanation, no expert agreement rate, and no statement of whether it derives from the model's actual internals or is written afterwards from the prediction.

And the explanation is the part a human reads and acts on. Nobody looks at a gating weight. An unscored sentence attached to a scored number is becoming the default packaging for computational non-animal methods, and it is the layer with the most influence on a decision and the least evidence behind it.

One genuinely encouraging detail, worth carrying. The model's dominant learned feature is collision cross-section — effectively how large a molecule is as it tumbles through a gas — which is not a learned abstraction but a quantity you can measure on an instrument, and which alone explains 72% of the variance in permeability. A model whose main signal has an external handle can be checked against the world. That is the precondition for the model layer being auditable inside a loop, and most models here do not have it.

How it sits in the rest of the vault

In-silico is one of the vault's cross-cutting method classes, alongside PBK/QIVIVE — the modelling that turns a concentration measured in a dish into a dose in a person. Where that is the toxicokinetic connective tissue, (Q)SAR and read-across are the structure-based connective tissue feeding every endpoint upstream. In practice it is the customary tier-one screen ahead of the wet genotoxicity battery — structural alerts prioritise what gets tested in the mammalian gene-mutation and micronucleus assays — and it supplies the structural-alert half of the carcinogenicity picture that the cell transformation assays complete for non-genotoxic carcinogens.

Bottom line. In-silico (Q)SAR and read-across are the standardised computational floor of the non-animal toolkit: broad class evidence_tier: in-silico-mechanistic and regulatory_status: proposed, applicability-domain-bounded, and loop_readiness: computational-connective — not an assay a machine could run, but the model layer a machine would update. For most endpoints, treat a prediction as one input to a weight-of-evidence case, never as a cleared substitute for a test, and never trust one outside its applicability domain. The exceptions are real, specific, and multiplying: a stand-alone Ames-test replacement under ICH M7 with expert review written into it; a scored leg inside the adopted skin-sensitisation defined approaches; and, as of this month, a medicines-submission route under ICH M15 that never uses the phrase "test guideline." The new cautions are three, and they compound: these models are demonstrably good at learning the screen as well as the hazard; the score that goes up in a model paper is frequently not the score a screening decision runs on; and combining several structure-based tools by majority vote does not buy independent opinions. The new fact in their favour is that on two commercially important endpoints, structure alone matched a 49-assay robotic panel.
