The dose bridge

Is this method ready? For what? And could a machine run it? In order: yes for some questions and demonstrably not for others; it is already carrying real regulatory weight, though never as a stand-alone method with a test guideline of its own; and it is not an assay at all — it is a computer model, so it is the only thing in this vault a machine already runs end to end. That last fact is why the honest reading of it is harder than it looks.

The problem it solves, in one paragraph

Every non-animal toxicity test in this vault produces the same kind of answer: a concentration in a dish. Cells stopped growing at 12 micromolar. The reporter gene lit up at 4. That number is real and it is useless on its own, because nobody is dosed in micromolar. People are exposed in milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day, through skin, lungs or food, after which a body absorbs some of it, distributes it, chemically alters it, and pisses most of it out.

So there is a translation problem sitting between every in-vitro result and every decision anyone wants to make with it, and the translation is what this article is about.

PBK modelling — physiologically based kinetic modelling — is the machinery. It represents the body as a set of connected compartments (liver, fat, blood, gut, kidney) with real volumes and blood flows, and simulates a chemical moving through them: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, collectively ADME. QIVIVE — quantitative in-vitro to in-vivo extrapolation — is what you use it for here: run the model backwards from the concentration that did something in a dish, and it tells you what external daily dose would produce that concentration in a person.

Take the dish number, run it through the model, get a human-equivalent dose. That is the bridge.

Where it stands on the three axes, said plainly

This vault grades every method on three things that move independently: is the science sound, has a regulator accepted it, and could a machine run it. On this one, the three answers are unusually far apart, and keeping them apart is most of the point.

Scientific validity: formally-validated. There is a mature, internationally agreed framework for how you build one of these models, characterise it, and demonstrate it is fit for a stated purpose — OECD Guidance Document 331, the 2021 document that specifies how a physiologically based kinetic model must be characterised, validated and reported. It explicitly supports models parameterised entirely from in-vitro and computational data, which is the version that matters if you are trying to get animals out of the pipeline.

Regulatory acceptance: under-validation. And this is a real distinction, not a bureaucratic one. GD 331 is a guidance document, not a Test Guideline. A Test Guideline says this method, run this way, produces an answer regulators accept. A guidance document says here is how to build and defend your model — and we will decide whether to believe yours, one submission at a time. Momentum is forward: the 2021 framework was followed by agreed QIVIVE principles in 2025. But there is no blanket adoption, and there is no method here you can simply run and cite.

Loop-readiness: computational-connective. This is the vault's off-the-ladder category, and it is the honest one. The usual ladder asks whether an instrument can read the result and whether a published rule can interpret it. Neither question applies, because there is no instrument and no experiment. This is software. It runs unattended by construction, on any machine, at whatever speed you like.

And that is precisely why "loop-ready" would be the wrong word for it, in a way worth dwelling on. A closed loop is: a robot runs an assay, a model updates, an AI picks the next experiment. This is the model that updates. It is not a candidate for automation; it is the thing automation would be built around. Calling it automatable is like calling a database automatable — true, and not the question anyone was asking.

How far the bridge actually carries — the number that matters most

The single most useful thing this vault holds about this method is that its accuracy is set by the endpoint, and the spread is enormous.

In 2026 a team at the US validation body took 35 priority chemicals, pulled their activity concentrations out of the largest robotic screening dataset in existence, ran them backwards through a standard model, and compared the answers against published human health guidance values (Chang, Allen, Kleinstreuer & Mumtaz, Toxicological Sciences 209(3):kfag017, DOI 10.1093/toxsci/kfag017). Read the results by organ system:

What was being predictedHow often the model landed closeTranslated
Liver effects9 of 10 within 15-foldWorks. Not precise — a 15-fold window is wide — but the model is in the right neighbourhood almost every time.
Nervous system effects8 of 16 within 10-foldA coin flip.
Developing fetus1 of 6 within 10-foldIt does not work. The misses were 83 to 250 times off.

Against the published guidance values the full spread of disagreement runs to 5,745-fold for neurological effects, 2,556-fold for liver, and 17,042-fold for developmental. Seventeen thousand fold. That is not a model that is somewhat wrong; on that endpoint it is a model that is not answering the question.

Two things follow, and the second one is the one people miss.

First, the paper's own abstract says points of departure "for a majority of chemicals can be predicted." That is defensible for liver, arguable for nerve, and false for development — and this vault grades on the results, not the abstract. Thirty-five chemicals, six of them developmental, is a signal and not a verdict, and it should be read as one. But the direction is unambiguous.

Second, the developmental failure lands exactly where this vault is already weakest. The reproductive and fertility endpoint has no validated non-animal method at all. The developmental neurotoxicity battery is contested. Zebrafish teratogenicity is emerging. Now the translation layer fails there too — and it fails independently, at the extrapolation step rather than at the assay step. Two separate weaknesses stacked on the same endpoint is worse than either, because fixing one does not fix the other.

And how often it runs at all — a different question from whether it is right

Accuracy asks when it runs, is the answer good. Coverage asks how often does it produce an answer. Those are different failures and the vault only got the second number recently.

Running this machinery end to end on ToxCast bioactivity data — the output of the biggest robotic screening programme there is — produced a usable chronic oral dose estimate for 9 of 41 US EPA-assessed substances (Chiu, Roe & Rusyn, Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology 169:106104, DOI 10.1016/j.yrtph.2026.106104). About one chemical in five. It was the lowest coverage of the four routes that paper compared.

An honest caveat, because the number invites over-reading: the published abstract does not break down the 32 failures. A chemical that was simply inactive across the whole test panel, a chemical that would not dissolve properly, and a chemical with no measured clearance value to feed the model all land in that 32, and only the last is a defect of the bridge itself.

Feed that back into the loop question and it gets sharper. A closed-loop testing system does not fail loudly here — it fails by returning nothing. Four chemicals in five come back with no number, and "no number" is not an error state a naive optimiser knows what to do with.

What a regulator will let it do, and what it will not — the sharpest line in the file

In 2026 the European Food Safety Authority published something remarkable: a procedure for waiving a legally required animal study. For agrochemical approval, the dog study can be skipped, and the flowchart's terminal step is a comparison of internal exposure between species, supported by a physiologically based kinetic model. If the dog's internal exposure tells you nothing the other species do not, the study need not happen.

That is a model deciding an experiment does not need to be run — the most consequential regulatory job this layer has ever been given, and a genuine reduction in animal use, delivered by computation.

The same opinion draws the boundary in the same breath. PBK modelling is "a tool and not for the derivation of HBGVs" — health-based guidance values, the numbers that say how much of a substance a person may safely be exposed to — and "the application of standard regulatory uncertainty factors remains unchanged."

A model may say which experiment to skip. It may not say what dose is safe.

If you are trying to work out how much of the regulatory stack a computational method can occupy, that sentence is the answer as of 2026, written by a regulator, and it stops well short of the decision. It is the same proportionality logic the medicines regulators apply to AI models, arriving from a completely different direction: the more alone the model is in a decision, the heavier the evidence it must carry.

The UK government said the quiet part in its 2025 strategy for replacing animals in science, which puts dedicated pharmacokinetic studies in the "alternatives exist but are not validated enough" basket and states flatly: "There are currently no alternative methods which fully predict in vivo pharmacokinetics." Its target for dogs and monkeys in those studies is a 35% reduction by 2030 — not replacement. A government with every incentive to claim progress, declining to.

What it costs to remove the last measured input

Here is the most encouraging number in the file, and it needs its caveat attached with a bolt.

A 2026 study predicted drug-induced liver injury from three dish-based potency measurements divided by the peak concentration a drug reaches in blood, and ran the whole comparison twice — once using a concentration measured in humans, once using one simulated end to end from nothing but the chemical's predicted properties (Geci et al., Archives of Toxicology 100(5):2029-2046, Geci et al. 2026, DOI 10.1007/s00204-026-04305-2). Swapping the measurement for the simulation cost about five points of discrimination (a standard ranking-accuracy score fell from 96% to 91%) and changed 13.94% of individual calls.

That is the cleanest published price for removing the last organism-derived input from a hazard pipeline, and it is a small price. One call in seven changes; the ranking barely degrades.

What it is not is validation of this layer. The test set is 241 marketed pharmaceuticals — molecules with better-characterised absorption and metabolism than almost any other chemical class on earth, and about as favourable to a kinetic model as a test set can be. The authors add that the potency values cluster in narrow ranges, which limits transfer to novel candidates. Read it beside the 207-chemical clearance dataset in the same map, where roughly two-thirds of a consumer-chemical set cleared slowly enough that the liver is not what controls internal concentration, and one pattern holds across everything here: this bridge performs where the compounds are well behaved.

The uncomfortable structural point, and it is about what the loop would learn

Every accuracy figure in this article shares one property. The success criterion is agreement with an animal result.

Nine of ten liver predictions "within 15-fold" — of what? Of a point of departure derived from an animal study, or a guidance value derived from one. One of six developmental predictions "correct" — measured against the rodent.

This is not a criticism of the work; concordance with the animal is the only large labelled dataset the field possesses, and everybody uses it because there is nothing else. But it has a specific consequence for automation that is worth stating carefully. A self-improving loop optimises whatever it is scored on. Score it on agreement with the rodent, and it will learn to reproduce the rodent — including the rodent's species-specific errors, which are the entire reason for building non-animal methods in the first place.

The same validation body that produced the benchmark study above has separately argued for replacing ring-trial-style validation with mechanistic anchoring, and one author appears on both. The field knows. The vault records the tension as a flagged inference rather than a finding, because nobody has measured how much rodent-specific error a concordance-optimised model actually inherits.

So: is it ready?

For dose-anchoring liver-relevant in-vitro results, and for arguing that a specific animal study is redundant — yes, with a regulator's signature already on the second one. For the developing fetus, on the evidence in this vault, no, and the failure is large enough that a number produced there should be treated as absent rather than uncertain.

As a stand-alone method: it is not one, and it does not claim to be. It is guidance, accepted per submission, never a Test Guideline.

And for a closed loop, the thing to hold onto is not that it is automatable. It plainly is — it is software. The thing to hold onto is that it is the layer that decides what every other number in the loop means, that it produces no answer at all for roughly four chemicals in five, that its error varies by a factor of seventeen thousand depending on which organ you asked about, and that a loop consuming its output downstream inherits all of that and can see none of it. The transcriptomic potency route in this vault already has that problem: its headline concordance figure is a joint result of a measurement and this model layer, and the published abstract does not decompose the two.

This is learning synthesis from public literature and public guidance — not regulatory advice, and not a statement that any method here is safe to automate. Sources and the full dated through-line are on the method map: pbk-qivive-toxicokinetics.
