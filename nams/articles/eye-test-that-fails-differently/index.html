Two ways to be wrong about an eye

Here is a rule worth more than any single test in this vault: if you are going to combine two safety tests and trust that they agree, the two tests had better be able to fail differently. On 2 July 2026, a regulator quietly shipped a battery that gets this right — and it is worth understanding why, because almost everything else in toxicology gets it wrong.

Start with the problem the test replaces. For a century, the way you found out whether a chemical would blind someone was the Draize test: drip the substance into a live rabbit's eye and watch what happens. It is exactly as unpleasant as it sounds, and — this is the part that matters — it is not even reliable. Run the Draize test twice on the same chemical and it disagrees with itself about 12% of the time. The gold standard is noisy.

So the field has been building non-animal replacements, and the eye is now one of the better-covered endpoints. This month it got a new one: OECD Test Guideline 496 — an internationally agreed protocol, newly adopted, for identifying eye hazards without an animal. What makes it interesting is not the assay. It is what happens when you stand it next to the eye test the vault already had.

What the new test actually does

TG 496 is almost aggressively simple. There are no cells and no animal in it at all — it is what the field calls in chemico, meaning a test-tube chemistry reaction. A freeze-dried mixture of proteins is rehydrated so it self-assembles into an ordered matrix that mimics the structure of the cornea — the clear front window of the eye. You add your chemical. If the chemical is going to hurt an eye, it disrupts that protein matrix, the matrix goes cloudy, and a plate reader measures exactly how cloudy as an optical density — a single number. A fixed cut-off turns the number into a call. Instrument in, number out, rule applied. There is no human judgment anywhere in it.

Two commercial kits are approved under the guideline (Ocular Irritection, or OI®, and OptiSafe). Both do one job well: ruling a chemical out — declaring it safe enough not to need an eye-hazard warning label. Only OI® also tries to rule chemicals in as causing serious eye damage, and here the guideline is refreshingly honest about a weakness. As a positive detector, OI® catches only about half of the chemicals that truly cause serious eye damage (54% sensitivity). Half. That is why the guideline never lets it stand alone and treats it strictly as a first-pass screen. The rule-out job it does well: when OptiSafe clears a chemical as safe, it was right for every genuinely-safe chemical in the validation set, at the cost of over-cautiously flagging about a fifth of safe chemicals for more testing — the cheap direction to be wrong in.

Where does it land on this vault's three axes?

  • Does the science hold up? Yes — it is regulatory-adopted, the top of the scientific-validity ladder, built on validation data and (for OptiSafe) an independent peer review.
  • Has a regulator accepted it? Yes — there is an OECD Test Guideline, which is what OECD-TG-adopted means. Both boxes ticked.
  • Could a machine run it end to end? This is the vault's own third question, and here the answer is a clean yes: closed-loop-ready. An optical-density reading against a fixed threshold is about as automatable as a measurement gets — the same clean shape as a pH meter. No cells to keep alive, no images to score, no judgment call. It is one of the purest loop-ready methods the vault holds.

That last point comes with one asterisk the vault always attaches, and it matters for anyone building an automated system: loop-ready does not mean trust it on anything. Because there is no living tissue in the tube, the test cannot metabolise a chemical. A substance that is harmless as-is but turns dangerous once the body processes it will sail straight through. An automated pipeline has to know that boundary and check it before believing a clean result. (The vault records this as a domain_gate — a documented input contract, the tox equivalent of "this function is only valid for positive integers.")

The reason this test matters more than it looks

Now put TG 496 next to the eye test the vault mapped in July: TG 492, which grows a small disc of real human corneal cells and measures whether the chemical kills the cells.

Both answer the same question — will this hurt an eye? — but they answer it by completely different physical mechanisms. One measures whether a chemical denatures protein in a tube. The other measures whether it kills living cells. A chemical that scrambles protein and a chemical that poisons cells need not be the same chemical.

That sounds like a footnote. It is actually the whole point, and to see why you have to know what usually goes wrong.

The trap: two tests, one blind spot

This vault has a recurring, uncomfortable finding it calls the correlated blind spot. It shows up everywhere. Two fish tests used together to predict environmental harm — and neither has a nervous system, so both miss the same nerve-poisoning chemicals for the identical reason. Two phototoxicity tests stacked in sequence — and neither can metabolise, so a chemical whose dangerous form only appears after the body breaks it down passes both. Three hormone tests built on the same engineered genetic backbone, so their "agreement" is partly an artefact of shared plumbing.

In every case the failure is the same: you run two tests, they agree, and you feel more confident — but they agreed because they share a weakness, so their agreement is one opinion wearing a disguise, counted twice.

And this is far more dangerous for an automated system than for a person. A human scientist reading a suspicious result might get a feeling that something is off. A closed automated loop just sees "two tests agree, decision rule satisfied, confidence high" and does the logical thing: it explores more chemicals like that one — fast, and confidently, straight into the blind spot. Speed turns a known, tolerable bias into a compounding, invisible one. That is the vault's single sharpest warning about handing toxicology to a machine.

The vault also holds the fix, and it came from the largest toxicology robot on earth. The Tox21 platform faces exactly this problem — nearly every test on its panel is a living cell rigged to glow, and all of them misread a dying cell as an active one. Tox21's answer is not to add more glowing-cell tests hoping the errors cancel. It adds a separate measurement of whether the cell is simply dying, in the same well — a measurement that fails differently. The principle the vault extracted:

The remedy for a correlated blind spot is a measurement that fails differently, not a bigger battery.

The quiet thing the regulator got right

Here is why the new eye test is worth an article. That principle — the one the vault derived from a robot and preaches at every correlated blind spot — is already built into the adopted eye battery.

The eye defined approach (TG 467, the framework that combines the component tests into a final classification) now draws on two components that fail differently by construction: TG 492 measures cell death, TG 496 measures protein denaturation. They are not two views of the same underlying signal. They are two genuinely different physical routes to the same answer — which means when they agree, that agreement carries real independent weight, closer to two votes than to one. This is not a fix someone is proposing in a paper. It is sitting inside an international guideline that regulators accept today.

That is rare enough to name plainly, because most of the vault's batteries are the bad case — components that quietly share a weakness. The eye column is the good case: a battery whose parts were chosen so their blind spots don't line up. If you are designing an automated testing strategy and you want one worked example of doing it right, this is it.

The honesty clause, because the vault does not do clean stories: the independence is real but not total. Neither eye component has metabolism, so both are blind to the metabolite-mediated hazard mentioned earlier. They are independent on the mechanisms they each read, and jointly blind on the one neither reads. "Fails differently" bought two independent votes on protein-versus-cell-death; it did not buy a vote on what the body does to the chemical first. A good automated system treats their agreement as strong for direct-acting chemicals and weak for the ones metabolism might transform — which is exactly the kind of context a decision rule has to encode, and exactly the kind of thing that gets lost when "the battery agreed" becomes a single green checkmark.

What to take away

Three things, in plain terms.

First, the axes really do move independently. TG 496 is scientifically adopted, regulatorily accepted, and fully machine-runnable — all three at once. That is a genuinely good state, and it is worth seeing one, because most of this vault is methods that score high on one axis and zero on another.

Second, a battery is only as trustworthy as the independence of its parts. Adding tests does not add confidence unless the new test can be wrong in a new way. Two tests that share a blind spot are one test with a louder voice. The newest eye test earns its place precisely because it fails differently from the one already there.

Third — and this is the point that outlives the eye — an automated lab inherits its battery's blind spots and then accelerates through them. The correlated-blind-spot problem is a nuisance for a human and a compounding failure for a closed loop. The eye battery shows the discipline that prevents it: choose measurements that fail differently, and write down where even those run out. The remedy was never a bigger battery. It was a better-designed one.
