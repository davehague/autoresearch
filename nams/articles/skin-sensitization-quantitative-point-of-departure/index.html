A dose, not a verdict: the first non-animal method that tells you how much

Can a non-animal method tell you not just whether a chemical causes skin allergy, but at what dose — and would a regulator accept that number? As of 2 July 2026 the answer is yes to both, with a caveat large enough that stating the yes without it would be misleading. OECD Guideline No. 497, Part III adopted two defined approaches that derive a quantitative point of departure — a dermal dose — from in-chemico and in-vitro assays that already exist. They are adopted on both axes this vault tracks: the science is regulatory-adopted, and the regulatory status is OECD-TG-adopted. The caveat is that the dose they return lands within ten-fold of the reference value about 85% of the time, and within two-fold only about a third of the time. Whether that is impressive or alarming depends entirely on what you were going to do with it.

This matters beyond one endpoint. Every other method in this vault answers a classification question: is this a sensitiser, is it GHS Category 1A, is it corrosive. These two answer how much. It is the first time a NAM here outputs a number on a dose axis rather than a label — a new kind of claim, not a deeper version of an old one.

What the two methods actually do

Neither one runs a new experiment. Both are statistical layers that extract more information from assays the skin-sensitisation battery already produces.

SARA-ICE is a Bayesian model. It takes results from the peptide-reactivity, keratinocyte-activation and dendritic-cell-activation assays — plus, where they exist, historical animal (LLNA) and human patch-test data — and returns an ED01: the dermal dose at which one percent of an exposed, susceptible population would be sensitised. Crucially it returns that as a distribution, not a point. You can see how confident the model is. It runs in a free application hosted by NICEATM.

The regression-based DA is plainer and, in one respect, more conservative by construction. It fits multiple linear regressions on three inputs — kDPRA, KeratinoSens and h-CLAT — trained separately against animal (LLNA EC3) potency values and against human weight-of-evidence values, then takes whichever of the two predictions is more conservative. It includes a correction for volatility based on vapour pressure. It runs in a spreadsheet you can read.

Both require at least two information sources across at least two key events of the underlying adverse outcome pathway. Both may consume existing animal data as an input; neither permits generating new animal data to feed them.

One structural detail is worth pulling out, because it quietly contradicts the tidy story people tell about this battery. The regression DA requires kDPRA — the kinetic variant of the peptide-reactivity assay, which measures a rate constant. The ordinary DPRA reports a percentage of peptide depleted, and a regression needs a rate, not a percentage. So the dose question demands an assay the hazard and potency questions do not.

Read the performance numbers slowly

Here is where honesty costs something. Both defined approaches place roughly 85% of their dose estimates within 10-fold of the reference value. Ten-fold is not a rounding error; it is the difference between a concentration you would put in a leave-on cosmetic and one you would not. Only about one third of estimates land within 2-fold.

Two things make that band more tolerable than it first sounds. First, the estimates are not systematically skewed: against human reference data SARA-ICE shows a mean and median fold-difference of about 1.1, and the regression DA a geometric-mean fold-difference of about 0.99. They are wide, not biased. Second, an uncertain dose you can see the uncertainty on — SARA-ICE's posterior distribution — is a more useful object for a risk assessor than a falsely precise point estimate.

And one thing makes it worse, which the guideline reports rather than buries. The regression DA under-predicts potency by more than ten-fold for between 5.5% and 8.9% of chemicals. Under-predicting potency means the derived point of departure is too high — the under-protective direction. A symmetric summary like "85% within 10-fold" conceals that the errors are not symmetric in consequence. If you take one number away from this article, take that one.

What "adopted" does and does not buy you

The two axes come apart in an unusual way here. On scientific validity, these are regulatory-adopted methods evaluated by the OECD expert group on defined approaches against a curated 196-chemical reference set carrying both animal and human data. On regulatory acceptance, they sit inside an adopted OECD guideline covered by the Mutual Acceptance of Data agreement. Both axes are as green as this vault records.

But adoption is scoped, and the scope is a wall rather than a fence. The guideline states that the Part III approaches "cannot be used in Part I or Part II." Part I is the "2-out-of-3" defined approach, which answers is this a sensitiser. Part II is the Integrated Testing Strategy, which answers is it GHS 1A or 1B. Part III answers at what dose. One battery of assays, three data-interpretation procedures, three regulatory questions — and no chaining between them. You may not derive a point of departure and then reason backwards to a hazard classification, or take a potency sub-category as licence to skip the PoD derivation.

That prohibition is the most conceptually interesting thing in the guideline. It means the object that carries the regulatory standing is not the assays. It is the data-interpretation procedure. Swap the procedure, feed it the same chemistry, and you get a different kind of answer with a different validity envelope — and the envelopes do not compose.

Who built these, and why that is on the record

funding_flag: [industry, method-developer]. SARA descends from work at Unilever's Safety and Environmental Assurance Centre (Reynolds and colleagues, 2019), later developed with NICEATM and NIEHS. The regression DA comes from Givaudan (Natsch and Gerberick, 2022). Both were then independently evaluated by the OECD expert group before adoption.

This is not a scandal, and it is not nothing. The companies that originated these models are fragrance and consumer-goods manufacturers — which is to say, the organisations that use points of departure to decide how much of an ingredient goes into a product. Developer origin combined with independent regulatory evaluation is stronger than a developer validating its own method, and weaker than a method that came from somewhere with no stake in the answer. Both halves belong in the record. Notice, too, which direction the regression DA's known error runs: under-predicting potency, permitting a higher dose. That is not evidence of anything. It is simply the reason you keep the funding flag attached to the number.

So: is it ready, and for what?

For deriving a skin-sensitisation point of departure to feed a quantitative risk assessment, within the chemical applicability domain, using at least two key-event assays: these are adopted, internationally recognised methods, and the science underneath them has been independently evaluated. That is a genuinely strong position, and it is the first time a non-animal method in this vault has reached it for a dose.

For anything that depends on the dose being precise to better than about an order of magnitude: read the distribution, not the point estimate, and treat the regression DA's under-prediction tail as a real risk rather than a statistical footnote. For hazard or potency classification: do not use these at all — the guideline forbids it, and the other two Parts exist for that purpose.

What this vault can say is that the evidence is adopted, the performance is wide but unbiased, the failure mode is asymmetric and under-protective, and the provenance is industry-originated and regulator-evaluated. What it cannot say — and does not — is whether any of that clears a particular chemical for a particular product in a particular jurisdiction. This is a learning ledger. It organises the public evidence; it certifies nothing.
