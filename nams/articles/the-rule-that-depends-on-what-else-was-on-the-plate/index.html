The rule that depends on what else was on the plate

A new non-animal test cuts the false-alarm rate of one of the field's mandatory safety tests roughly in half. It reads out as numbers, and it comes with a written-down rule for turning those numbers into a verdict — the exact combination this vault uses to say "a machine could run this." It is the first genotoxicity method to arrive already at the top of that ladder. And it is also the first to show that the word we have been leaning on — deterministic — was doing more work than it could bear.

Start with the problem, not the method

Genotoxicity is the question of whether a chemical damages DNA. It matters because DNA damage is the first step on the road to cancer and to inherited defects, so essentially every chemical that touches people — drugs, cosmetics ingredients, industrial compounds, pesticides — has to be screened for it before it can be sold.

The screen everyone uses has a known and expensive flaw. The in-vitro micronucleus test — adopted internationally as OECD Test Guideline 487, an agreed lab protocol for spotting chromosome damage in cultured cells — works by dosing cells, letting them divide, and then having a trained person look down a microscope and count little stray blobs of DNA that got left outside the nucleus. Those blobs are called micronuclei, and they are the physical evidence that a chromosome was broken or misplaced.

It is very good at not missing real hazards. It is bad at the opposite error. The new paper puts its specificity — the fraction of genuinely harmless chemicals it correctly clears — somewhere between 30% and 65%, depending on which dataset you use.

Translate that. Between a third and two-thirds of the safe chemicals you put through this required test come back flagged as dangerous. Each of those false alarms is not a shrug. Historically it buys a follow-up study, and the follow-up study has usually been in animals. The field has been paying for that error rate for decades, in money, in time, and in animals killed to disprove an alarm that was wrong in the first place.

What the new test does

TGx-DDI — the name stands for toxicogenomic DNA damage-inducing — attacks that specific problem. Instead of looking for the damage, it listens to the cells talk about it.

When a cell's DNA is damaged, it switches on a coordinated emergency programme: a specific set of genes turns up, another set turns down. TGx-DDI measures the activity of 64 genes and asks whether the resulting pattern looks like the signature of a cell responding to DNA damage. It runs in TK6 cells — a human white-blood-cell line that is also the workhorse of the micronucleus test — and the readout is digital counting of individual RNA molecules on a commercial instrument. No microscope, no human eye, no judgement call at the bench.

The evidence behind it is unusually good for this vault. In a ring trial — the field's gold standard, where several independent laboratories run the same blinded samples to prove the method works on somebody else's bench with somebody else's hands — four labs (Georgetown University, Procter & Gamble, Sanofi, and a contract research organisation) each ran 14 compounds whose identity they did not know, six known DNA-damaging and eight known harmless, plus three controls (Wang & Crute et al. 2025, Toxicological Sciences 208(2):233–243, PMID: 41031489).

The results, in plain terms:

  • It caught every one of the six DNA-damaging compounds. Zero misses.
  • Of the eight harmless ones, it wrongly flagged about one in seven — a specificity of 86%.
  • Overall, 91 calls in 100 were correct.
  • The labs agreed with each other well to perfectly; two of the four agreed on every single call.

The sensitivity is nice and is not the news. Every genotoxicity screen in existence is tuned to never miss a mutagen, because that is the error nobody will tolerate. The news is the 86%. Against the micronucleus test's 30–65%, it roughly halves the false-alarm rate on the problem the micronucleus test is worst at.

That earns TGx-DDI the vault's formally-validated grade for scientific validity — a genuine multi-lab blinded trial with published agreement statistics, which few sources here clear.

Now the honest part

Fourteen compounds is a small panel, and the statistics say so. "100% sensitivity" is the observation "zero misses out of six." The 95% confidence interval runs from 82% to 100% — meaning the true sensitivity could plausibly be 82% and this trial was too small to tell the difference. The specificity interval runs 67% to 96%, which is wide enough to overlap the top of the micronucleus test's range. The comparison is directionally solid and quantitatively soft, and anyone quoting "86% versus 30%" as a settled fact is quoting it harder than the data supports.

Nobody has adopted it. On the regulatory axis it sits at proposed. A qualification package has been submitted to the US Food and Drug Administration's Biomarker Qualification Program — the formal route by which FDA blesses a measurement for reuse across drug programmes — and nothing has been accepted. If it is accepted it would be the first transcriptomic (gene-activity-based) biomarker FDA's drug centre has ever qualified, which is a genuinely large precedent. It has not happened.

The people who invented it helped validate it. The group that developed TGx-DDI is on the author list and is the party seeking FDA qualification. Two of the four participating labs are commercial pharmaceutical and consumer-goods companies; a third is a commercial contract lab. The funding is public (an FDA grant), but this is not the same thing as an independent validation body running the trial on somebody else's method. It is a strong ring trial. It is not a disinterested one, and the vault records it as funding_flag: industry.

And it is not a replacement for the micronucleus test. The authors do not claim it is. Their recommendation is to use it alongside the existing battery, and the reason is worth understanding, because it turns into the sharpest thing in this article.

Where it is blind, on purpose

TGx-DDI does not detect DNA damage. It detects the cell's reaction to DNA damage — specifically, a reaction coordinated by p53, the protein cells use as a damage alarm. Everything about the test's limitations follows from that one design choice, and the nice thing is that they are all predictable in advance rather than discovered by surprise:

  • The cells must have working p53. Many lab cell lines don't. The authors name the four verified so far and say anything else must be checked first.
  • It cannot see one entire class of genotoxin. Some chemicals don't break DNA at all — they sabotage the machinery that pulls chromosomes apart during cell division, so a whole chromosome ends up in the wrong daughter cell. These are called aneugens, they are genuinely dangerous, and TGx-DDI is structurally incapable of detecting them. That is not a performance shortfall; it is the design.
  • Some harmless chemicals will trip it anyway — anything that pokes the p53 alarm system without damaging DNA. The paper names kinase inhibitors and protein-synthesis inhibitors specifically. Again: the test working exactly as designed, on the wrong stimulus.

Aneugens are exactly what the micronucleus test was built to catch. So on that axis the two tests are genuinely complementary — one covers the other's structural blind spot. That is what a battery of tests is supposed to look like.

The blind spot they share

And now the thing this vault exists to notice.

Both tests run in the same TK6 cells. Neither TK6 culture has any meaningful ability to chemically transform the compounds you put on it — which is a problem, because a lot of chemicals are harmless as you dose them and only become dangerous after the body's liver enzymes convert them into something else. Toxicologists call these pro-genotoxicants, and the standard workaround is to add rat liver S9: a processed fraction of homogenised rat liver, tipped into the well as a stand-in for a functioning liver.

Both tests use it. The same one.

So take a chemical that only becomes DNA-damaging through a metabolic route rat liver doesn't perform — a human-specific enzyme, say. TGx-DDI calls it clean. The micronucleus test calls it clean. They agree, and a weight-of-evidence assessment reads that agreement as two independent confirmations.

It is one confirmation, reported twice. The two tests failed for the same reason: neither has the liver chemistry required, because both are borrowing the same rat's.

This vault calls that a correlated blind spot, and it now appears in three of the six pairs it tracks — always for the same underlying cause. Every in-vitro test solves the metabolism problem the same way, so every in-vitro test inherits the same gap.

What all this means if a machine is running it

The vault grades every method on a third axis nobody else tracks: could a machine run this, unattended, and make the call? The bar has two parts. Is the readout something an instrument produces rather than a person judges? And does a fixed, written-down procedure exist for turning that readout into a verdict?

TGx-DDI clears both, on paper, cleanly. The readout is digital molecule counting. And the rule is published: run three classifiers over the 64-gene profile, and call the chemical positive if any one of them fires. Deliberately conservative, mechanical, no expert judgement. That is precisely what several otherwise excellent adopted methods in this vault lack — the estrogen-receptor binding guideline, for instance, is fully quantitative and still cannot be automated end-to-end, because the guideline itself says no agreed way to interpret the numbers exists.

So TGx-DDI lands at closed-loop-ready — the vault's top automation grade, and the first genotoxicity method to get there.

Here is the asterisk, and it is new.

Of the three classifiers, one is supervised: it compares the sample's gene profile against a pre-trained reference and answers on the sample's own data. The other two — a principal component analysis and a hierarchical clustering — are unsupervised. They work by finding structure within the set of samples being analysed. Where a sample projects, and which cluster it lands in, depend on what else was analysed alongside it.

In a normal laboratory that is a non-issue, and the ring trial proves it: technicians run the protocol's standard reference compounds every time, the batch composition is effectively constant, and the labs agreed with each other beautifully. The rule is reproducible.

A closed loop breaks that assumption by design. The entire point of an autonomous lab is that the AI picks the next set of compounds based on what it just learned. Which means the loop is continuously changing the very thing two of its three classifier legs are conditioned on — deliberately, adaptively, and in a direction correlated with the previous result. A borderline compound tested in an early exploratory batch and the same compound tested later among its close structural relatives are not guaranteed to get the same verdict.

So this vault has had to split a word it was treating as one thing:

  • Fixed — the procedure is written down and applied mechanically. TGx-DDI: yes.
  • Sample-independent — the verdict on compound X depends only on compound X's data and pre-trained parameters. TGx-DDI: partly, no.

The automation grade was defined on the first property. This is the first method in the vault to show they come apart.

It does not demote the method. The rule is fixed, the reproducibility is measured and good, and the failure mode is an argument rather than an observed error. What it demands is a specific, cheap engineering requirement: a loop running this assay must pin its reference set and run it in every single batch, treating the reference panel as part of the protocol rather than as overhead. Which is worth stating loudly, because a reference set that produces the same answer every time is exactly the sort of thing an optimising autonomous system would identify as redundant and remove.

The three verdicts, kept separate

Is the science good? Yes, by this vault's standards unusually so — a four-lab blinded ring trial with published agreement statistics is the strongest evidence type here, and TGx-DDI clears it. Tempered by a 14-compound panel, wide confidence intervals, and developers who participated in validating their own method.

Is it accepted? No. proposed. A package is with FDA and has not been ruled on. Nobody may currently substitute this for a required genotoxicity test. If you need a regulatory answer today, the micronucleus test and its 30–65% specificity remain what you must run.

Could a machine run it? Yes — and this is the part that is not an endorsement. A machine could read the plate and apply the rule. Whether it should, on a chemical nobody has checked against the two-part applicability gate (does this compound have a plausible p53-independent mechanism? do these cells still have working p53?), is a completely different question with a completely different answer. And a loop that pairs TGx-DDI with the micronucleus test, sees them agree, and marks the chemical resolved will be right most of the time and systematically, invisibly wrong on pro-genotoxicants — steering its own next experiments away from the region where the battery is blind, because from inside the loop that region looks like solved space.

That last failure is not hypothetical and it is not exotic. It is what happens when you take a battery designed for a human reading it slowly, and run it fast without writing down what it cannot see.
