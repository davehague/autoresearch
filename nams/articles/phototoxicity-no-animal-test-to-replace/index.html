The toxicity test with no animal test to replace

If you want to know whether a chemical will burn someone's skin in sunlight, you can answer that today with adopted, non-animal methods — and you never had the option of asking a rabbit. Phototoxicity is the first endpoint in this vault where the non-animal methods are not challengers to an animal incumbent. There is no incumbent. The OECD's own guidance says so plainly, and that single fact makes this small corner of regulatory toxicology worth more attention than its size suggests: it is the closest thing we have to a controlled experiment in what regulators build when they are not trying to reproduce an animal study.

The short answer to "can I rely on this?" is: yes, for photoirritation, within a defined strategy — and not at all for the other two ways light can make a chemical dangerous. That second half is the part most summaries leave out, and it is the more important half.

What the methods are, and where they stand

Two adopted OECD Test Guidelines carry the endpoint, and both are regulatory-adopted on the validity axis and OECD-TG-adopted on the acceptance axis — the strongest pair of gradings this vault issues.

The screen is the in vitro 3T3 NRU phototoxicity test (OECD TG 432). It is almost aggressively simple: mouse fibroblasts get the chemical on two plates, one is irradiated with UVA, the other sits in the dark, and the test asks how much more cytotoxic the chemical becomes in light. A Photo-Irritation-Factor above 5 (or a Mean Photo Effect above 0.15) means phototoxic. Worth stating clearly, because it is the kind of thing that gets rounded up in secondary sources: the guideline reports no sensitivity or specificity figure at all. It says only that the test "was shown to be predictive of acute phototoxic effects." We record that absence as an absence.

The confirmation is the reconstructed human epidermis phototoxicity test (OECD TG 498), whose current edition was adopted on 2 July 2026. Here the chemical is applied to engineered human skin — layered, differentiated, with a real stratum corneum — and phototoxicity means a 30% or greater drop in viability under simulated sunlight versus the dark. The barrier is the entire point. A bare fibroblast monolayer measures whether a chemical can be photoactivated against a cell; human epidermis measures whether enough of it gets through skin for that to matter. The two assays read nearly identical biology and are not remotely substitutes for each other, which is a distinction worth holding onto.

And there is a nice piece of engineering hiding in TG 498: it is, by the guideline's own description, two older methods welded together. It "combines the basic principles" of TG 439 — the reconstructed-epidermis tissue and the MTT viability readout used for skin irritation — with the standardised irradiation procedure of TG 432. New endpoint, no new technology. The reconstructed-human-epidermis platform now carries four adopted OECD methods (skin irritation, skin corrosion, phototoxicity, and, in its cornea-like form, eye), which are distinguished from one another mostly by exposure protocol. That is efficient, and it is also a concentration of risk nobody seems to be tracking: a systematic flaw in these tissue models would move four adopted guidelines at once.

The honest problem with the numbers

Here is where the two axes come apart, and in an unfamiliar direction.

TG 498's Validated Reference Method is MatTek's EpiDerm™ PT, and its formal performance figures — sensitivity 86.7%, specificity 93.3% — come from a pre-validation study run in 1999 on ten chemicals. Not ten thousand. Ten. A later database of more than sixty chemicals in the same model performed better than that, and the guideline volunteers, without being asked, that those studies "whilst using the same SOP, were not conducted as formal validation studies." The 2026 edition adds a second tissue, KeraSkin™, with figures that look spectacular (100% sensitivity, 98.6% specificity) — but those are "me-too" figures, earned by demonstrating equivalence to an already-fixed prediction model under the guideline's Performance Standards. That is a real, independently peer-reviewed check that a second tissue behaves like the first. It is not a fresh validation of the underlying model, and the two numbers should not be quoted in the same breath.

So: this is an adopted method resting on a thin formal validation base, and it is the first one this vault has met. Usually the gap between the two axes runs the other way — good science waiting years for acceptance, as with the twelve years between the CYP-induction assay's validation and its adoption as TG 445A. Here, acceptance has outrun the depth of validation.

Before that sounds like an indictment, look at why. You cannot assemble a large reference set of animal-derived truth for an endpoint that has no validated animal test. TG 498's own proficiency table records that its in-vivo reference classifications "were derived from the validation studies of the 3T3 NRU PT test (OECD TG 432) and were mostly based on clinical human data as no validated in vivo method is available for this endpoint." The benchmark is scarce human clinical observation, which nobody generates on demand. The thinness is a property of the endpoint, not evidence of a lax guideline — and it is a genuinely hard problem rather than an oversight. It still means that "adopted on both axes" and "extensively validated" are different claims here, and anyone leaning on TG 498 should know which one they are relying on.

What regulators build when there is no animal test to copy

The methods are tied together by an OECD Guidance Document (GD 397, 2024) into an Integrated Approach to Testing and Assessment. Note the status carefully: every method it composes is an adopted Test Guideline, but the composition itself is guidance, not a guideline — formally-validated on the science, proposed on acceptance, applied case by case. That is the same split the vault records for PBK/QIVIVE modelling under GD 331. The innovation is the composition, and composition is exactly the thing regulators are slowest to bind.

The strategy runs cheapest gate first: a physico-chemical filter (TG 101 — a chemical whose molar extinction coefficient is below 1,000 is very unlikely to be photoreactive at all), then an in-chemico reactivity screen (TG 495, the ROS assay, which ICH S10 names as an optional first screen for pharmaceuticals), then the sensitive cell screen (TG 432), then the human-tissue confirmation (TG 498).

Look at what that is not. It is not a battery of assays each mimicking a piece of an animal study. It is not a defined approach engineered to reproduce a Draize call or an LLNA call. The first gate is not even biological. Every other integrated strategy in this vault — the skin-sensitisation 2-out-of-3, the eye defined approaches, the developmental-neurotoxicity battery — was designed in the shadow of an animal test it had to match. This one had nothing to match, so it was built the way you would build it from scratch: eliminate what cannot possibly be photoreactive, screen what might be, confirm what matters in human tissue. It is hard to look at that and not suspect the shadow has been costing the other endpoints something.

The part that gets left out

Light can hurt you through a chemical in three ways, and the adopted methods cover one.

  • Phototoxicity (photoirritation) — the acute, non-immune sunburn-like response. Covered, as above.
  • Photoallergy — an immune-mediated reaction, where light structurally alters the chemical so that it acts as a hapten. No OECD Test Guideline exists.
  • Photogenotoxicity — genetic damage, either by light exciting DNA directly or by exciting the chemical. No OECD Test Guideline exists.

That is not this vault's inference. GD 397 states that "there are no validated test methods (i.e., OECD Test Guideline) to evaluate photosensitive or photogenotoxic potential of chemicals to this date," and then — rather than building an IATA over unvalidated assays, which it notes "would be difficult to use in regulatory contexts" — scopes itself around the hole. It is an unusually clean admission, and it deserves to be repeated rather than buried.

What makes the two blanks genuinely irritating is how close the field already is to both. Photoallergy is, in mechanistic outline, the skin-sensitisation pathway with a photochemical step bolted onto the front — and skin sensitisation is the most mature non-animal battery in existence, with three adopted key-event assays and three adopted defined approaches sitting on top of them. Photogenotoxicity is the standard genotoxicity battery with the lights on — and that battery, too, is adopted, mature, and four assays deep. In both cases the assays exist, the biology is adjacent, and the validation has simply never been done with an irradiation arm.

It is tempting to conclude that the existing batteries must therefore mostly cover these hazards. They do not, and the temptation is exactly the failure mode this vault is built to resist. An assay's applicability domain is what it was validated for, not what it plausibly might handle. Adjacency is not applicability, and a regulator who accepted the substitution would be accepting an untested one. The blanks stay blank.

Where this leaves you

For photoirritation of a topically applied chemical, the position is genuinely good: two adopted OECD Test Guidelines, an OECD-authored integrated strategy, and — unusually — a benchmark of human clinical data rather than an animal proxy. Use them within the tiered strategy rather than as standalone verdicts, and understand that the reference method's formal validation is thinner than its adopted status implies.

For photoallergy or photogenotoxicity, there is no validated non-animal method, and there is no adopted animal method being replaced either. That is not a gap someone forgot to close. It is a live, stated hole in the field, and if you need those endpoints, no test guideline is going to help you today.

And for anyone trying to understand why non-animal methods advance as slowly as they do, phototoxicity is the control group worth staring at. Freed from having to reproduce an animal study, regulators produced a strategy that is cheaper, faster, human-benchmarked, and structurally sensible — and they still left two-thirds of the hazard space empty. Removing the animal test was never the hard part.
