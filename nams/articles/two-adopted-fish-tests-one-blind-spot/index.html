Two adopted fish tests, one blind spot

Is this method ready, what for, and could a machine run it? Ready, unambiguously — this is one of the small number of non-animal methods in this vault that is adopted on every axis that matters: the science is settled, the guideline is in force, and the readout is close enough to machine-readable that it is one published decision rule away from running itself end to end. But two regulators are about to pair it with another adopted test that fails on exactly the same chemicals, for exactly the same reason, and call that pairing independent evidence. That is the story worth telling here.

What the test is

The Fish Embryo Acute Toxicity (FET) test takes a newly-fertilised zebrafish (Danio rerio) egg, exposes it to a chemical for 96 hours, and watches for four things: does the egg coagulate (go opaque and die), does it fail to form somites (the body segments that become muscle and spine), does the heart fail to beat, does the tail fail to detach from the yolk sac. Any one of those four is a death call. It is OECD Test Guideline 236, adopted in 2013 and updated in 2025, validated across multiple laboratories between 2012 and 2014.

The reason this counts as a non-animal method at all is a specific regulatory line, not a biological one: embryos up to 120 hours post-fertilisation are not classified as a protected life stage under the frameworks that matter here. Cross that line — hatch the fish, let it start feeding, raise it to adulthood — and you are back to animal testing. FET exists entirely inside that window, standing in for the older adult acute fish toxicity test (OECD TG 203).

What you need for the rest of this article: a "domain gate" is this vault's term for an applicability-domain carve-out — a condition under which a test's otherwise-trustworthy decision rule should not be trusted. A "correlated blind spot" is when two supposedly independent tests fail on the same chemicals for the same underlying structural reason, so that running both does not actually double your evidence.

Where it stands on all three axes

Is the science sound? Yes, at the top of the scale. evidence_tier: regulatory-adopted — an OECD inter-laboratory validation backs it, and it has been reproduced for well over a decade.

Has anyone accepted it? Yes, also at the top. regulatory_status: OECD-TG-adopted. Status: consensus.

Could a machine run it end to end? Almost. loop_readiness: automatable-open-loop. This is a genuinely different answer from most of the eye-scored tests this vault tracks — the blocker here is not a human judgement call, it is paperwork. The four endpoints are close to binary (an egg either coagulates or it doesn't; a heart either beats or it doesn't) and are read by a camera, not a trained eye. What is missing is a single published, fixed, step-by-step procedure — a data-interpretation procedure (DIP) — turning those four observations into one pass/fail call, the way other adopted methods in this vault already have one written down. Whole-organism does not mean unloopable. This is the clearest counter-example in the vault to the intuitive (wrong) idea that a living embryo is automatically harder to automate than a dish of cells: the embryo sits in a standard well plate, and the thing standing between this test and closed-loop-ready is a committee writing down a rule that almost certainly already exists informally in every lab that runs it.

Honest limit, stated inside the adopted scope. FET correlates strongly with the adult fish test it replaces for baseline/narcotic chemicals — generic membrane-disrupting toxicity. It systematically under-predicts for three classes: neurotoxic chemicals, biotransformation-dependent chemicals (ones that need a metabolic step to become toxic), and chemicals the embryo simply doesn't take up well. That is not a flaw in the validation — it is the validation's own stated domain, and it is the seed of everything that follows.

The blind spot, and why regulators are about to make it worse rather than better

This vault flags a correlated blind spot whenever two tests that look independent fail on the same chemicals for the same structural reason. FET has one, and the partner is OECD Test Guideline 249, the RTgill-W1 fish-gill-cell-line assay — also adopted, also proposed as a stand-in for the same adult fish test.

Both guidelines say, in their own text, that they miss the same two things: chemicals that act through brain or nervous-system receptors ("less sensitive" in both, per TG 249's own wording), and chemicals that only become toxic after metabolic activation (TG 249's own worked example: allyl alcohol, which the cells cannot convert into the actually-toxic acrolein). The reason is structural and shared: a fish embryo this young has no functioning nervous system yet, and neither it nor an isolated gill cell line has a mammal's or adult fish's full biotransformation machinery. Two tests, one underlying gap. A battery that runs both and finds them agreeing is not getting two confirmations — it is getting the same blind spot twice.

This stopped being a vault footnote and became a live policy problem in 2025. The UK government's "Replacing Animals in Science" strategy, and a parallel EU REACH proposal, both name the same pairing — FET plus RTgill-W1 plus a computational (QSAR) check — as the proposed weight-of-evidence replacement for the in-vivo adult fish test, on a UK deadline of end-2028. Neither document acknowledges that the two wet-lab legs of that combination share a known, structural failure mode. The chemicals the combination is least likely to flag correctly — neurotoxicants, chemicals needing metabolic activation — are exactly the chemicals where getting it wrong matters most.

Why this is a worse problem for a machine than for a person. A toxicologist looking at two negative results on a chemical with a known structural red flag for bioactivation has somewhere to go: distrust the concordance, order a different test. An automated screening loop does not have that instinct built in. Two negatives that agree is exactly what a decision rule is built to read as confirmation — and a system that uses that confirmation to decide what to test next will keep walking confidently past the chemicals its whole battery cannot see. Speed turns a known, documented bias into a compounding one, and nothing in either government's proposal names a fix. The OECD's own in-progress fish-toxicity integrated testing strategy, due in 2027, is where a check for this would have to live — worth reading closely when it lands.

A second gate, found from outside the field

In 2026, an independent regulatory-literature review — not written by anyone building or defending either test — went looking across the whole zebrafish OECD Test Guideline portfolio for unresolved gaps, and named two. One was the blind spot above, independently confirmed from a completely different angle. The other was new to this map: the chorion, the protective shell enclosing the embryo for the entire 96-hour exposure window, may act as a barrier that large or poorly-permeable molecules simply cannot cross in time. A chemical that cannot get through the shell in appreciable quantity reads as "not toxic," regardless of what it would do to a fish without a shell in the way — or to the organism this test is meant to be predicting for, which by the time it matters in the environment is not wearing one. No one has yet published a quantitative threshold for which chemicals this affects, so this is logged as an open, named gap rather than a scored one.

The same review restated something this test's own blocker already says in different words: the four apical endpoints are deliberately conservative and were never meant to capture the "large majority" of sublethal, behavioral, or mechanistic information a 96-hour-old zebrafish embryo actually carries. That is not a new problem — it is this test doing exactly what it was designed to do, narrowly. Worth stating plainly rather than letting "automatable" imply "comprehensive."

The step before the test, measured for the first time — and it drops one embryo in seven

Everything above concerns what this test can see once an embryo is correctly in its well. In 2026, a study asked a question nobody in this vault had asked yet: how good is the step before that, where a device sorts and loads the eggs?

A commercial AI-based embryo sorter was benchmarked against trained human technicians across 117,956 embryos and found to systematically undercount by 13.41% — not randomly wrong, consistently short — with roughly three times the run-to-run variability of a person doing the same job, and its own internal quality classifications statistically unrelated to which embryos actually survived, hatched, or showed deformities. On an objectively checkable fluorescence-detection task, it missed 50.8% of true positives.

This does not move this test's own grading, and that is deliberate, not an oversight: loop_readiness grades a method's readout and its decision rule, and specimen handling is upstream of both — a miscounted batch of eggs changes the denominator the test's own statistics are calculated against, without appearing anywhere in the test's own data. A systematic 13% undercount, varying with how crowded the sample is, quietly puts error into a median-lethal-concentration calculation that nothing downstream can see or correct for. The finding was serious enough to open its own dedicated map in this vault for specimen-handling automation, separate from the toxicology test itself — and worth remembering every time "the test is automatable" gets read as "the whole pipeline around the test is trustworthy."

The number that puts "zebrafish is a NAM" in its place

A 2026 bibliometric analysis did something nobody had done for this organism before: it systematically counted what the entire zebrafish-toxicology literature — 20,291 articles published 2014–2024, screened down from over 93,000 — actually studies. The headline number is blunt. 83.95% of that literature used whole-organism, in-vivo zebrafish. Only 16.05% used any in-vitro, zebrafish-derived system at all. And within the in-vivo studies, the overwhelming majority stop at the embryo or larval stage — only 691 of the 20,291 articles followed a fish from embryo all the way through to adulthood.

This does not change this test's grading, and it should sharpen how you read the whole species. FET's non-animal status is earned legitimately — it lives entirely inside a specific, narrow, regulator-defined embryo window. But "zebrafish" as a research organism is overwhelmingly still a live-animal model once you look past that one window: most zebrafish toxicology studies use juvenile or adult fish, or follow embryos well past the point where the non-protected-life-stage line applies. The honest claim is "this specific 96-hour embryo protocol is a NAM," not "zebrafish research is animal-free." Worth keeping in mind for every other zebrafish-based method this vault tracks that reads neurotoxicity, developmental toxicity, or any other endpoint on the same organism — several of them, by the same count, sit inside the same narrow, well-studied early-life window, and the field's chronic/adult-exposure evidence is correspondingly thin.

What to take away

On the science: as solid as this vault's evidence ladder gets — OECD-validated, internationally adopted, reproduced for over a decade. The documented under-prediction domain (neurotoxic, metabolism-dependent, low-uptake chemicals) is a stated limit, not a hidden flaw.

On acceptance: fully adopted, stable, and — unusually for this vault — about to be leaned on harder by two separate government strategies (UK, EU) as part of a proposed replacement battery for the animal test it already partially replaces.

On automation: closer to the finish line than almost anything else this vault tracks. automatable-open-loop, blocked by a missing published decision rule rather than a human judgement call — a rule that would plausibly be straightforward to write, because the readout is already close to binary and camera-read. That is exactly why the uncorrected blind spot and the undocumented chorion gate matter more here, not less: a test this close to running itself end to end is a test whose errors, once the rule is written, propagate at machine speed with no person in the loop to notice that two adopted assays just told it the same wrong thing twice.

And the caution this vault repeats every time: none of the above says this test, or the battery regulators are proposing around it, should be run by a machine today. It carries a documented correlated blind spot with its nearest regulatory partner, an undocumented applicability gate at the chorion, and — one level upstream of the test itself — a demonstrated automation failure in simply getting the right number of embryos into the right wells. "Nearly loop-ready" was never the same sentence as "safe to loop."

This is learning synthesis from public literature and public guidance, not regulatory advice. It does not certify any method for a real testing programme, and it does not certify any method as safe to automate.
