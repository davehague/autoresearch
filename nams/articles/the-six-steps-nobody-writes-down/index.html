The six steps nobody writes down: why a machine-readable test still isn't a machine-runnable one

There is a comfortable way to describe a modern safety test, and this vault has been using it.

You put a chemical on some human cells in a plastic dish. A machine measures what happened — how much light the dish absorbs, how bright it glows, how much electricity passes through it. A number comes out. If a rule somewhere says "call it a hazard if that number crosses this line," then in principle a robot could run the whole thing: pipette the chemical, read the plate, apply the rule, move on. No human required.

That description is how this vault decides whether a method could ever run inside an autonomous laboratory. It sorts every method into tiers, and the top tier — the machine could read it and make the call — currently holds 24 of them.

Tonight the description turns out to have a gap in the middle, and it is not a small one.

A paper published in ALTEX, a journal for non-animal testing methods, says the following: between the number the instrument emits and the answer the rule consumes, there are six or more separate processing steps, and "for some [methods], a dozen or more processing steps can be required." Each step involves a choice. Most choices are not written down anywhere a second laboratory could find them. And, in the authors' words:

"The same raw data, processed in different ways, may result in different final outcomes."

Thirty-two people signed that sentence. They include staff of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (the body that writes the internationally-agreed test guidelines this whole field runs on), the European Food Safety Authority, the US Food and Drug Administration, the US National Toxicology Program's alternative-methods centre, Germany's federal risk-assessment institute, and toxicologists from BASF, Bayer, Unilever, Beiersdorf and Boehringer Ingelheim. This is not a critic sniping from outside. It is the field describing its own plumbing.

What the six steps actually are

Here is the chain, in the paper's own layering, with the choice hiding in each step.

#What you haveWhat has to be decided to get to the next one
0a machine, with settingsfilter widths, camera gain, electrode sensitivity, incubator conditions
1machine output — voltages, light intensity, raw pixels—
2raw data — a cell count, a fluorescence valuewhich readings are errors and get thrown out
3averaged, relative data — percent of the controlwhat counts as a replicate; mean, median or geometric mean; normalise against which control
4a dose-response curvewhich curve shape is allowed to be fitted, and how many points are needed to trust it
5a single number — the dose where something starts happeningwhat size of effect counts as "something happening"
6the answer — hazard, or notthe published decision rule

The vault's top loop-readiness tier grades step 6. Only step 6. Everything from 0 to 5 is what this vault has been calling "the readout," as though a plate reader emitted a safety threshold. It emits step 1.

For an engineer the analogy is immediate and slightly uncomfortable. We have been describing a deployed system by its final if statement, and treating the several thousand lines of preprocessing above it as though they were the hardware.

Does this break anything? Mostly no — and the reason matters

No method in this vault was downgraded tonight, and the honest reasons are worth stating, because the temptation with a finding like this is to over-apply it.

First, for most of the 24 top-tier methods the chain really is short. Take the lab-grown human skin test for irritation — OECD Test Guideline 439, in which a chemical is applied to a small disc of reconstructed human skin and you measure whether the cells survived. The whole chain is: read absorbance, express it as a percentage of the untreated control, compare to 50%. That's a normalisation and a threshold, and both are printed in the guideline. There is no curve fitting, no benchmark dose, no multi-endpoint arbitration.

Second, that is largely what an internationally-agreed test guideline is for. These documents exist precisely to nail down the steps that would otherwise vary. Adopted methods are in genuinely better shape here than research assays are, and the paper's warning is aimed hardest at the complicated end: methods with curve fitting, dose thresholds, and several readouts that have to be reconciled.

What changes is what the tier means. Machine could make the call certifies the decision rule. It has never certified the pipeline that feeds the rule, and this vault had drifted into reading it that way.

The sentence that should worry anyone planning to automate this

Buried in the conclusions is a line with sharp operational teeth:

"changing the pipelines of already established [methods] can have serious consequences. In the worst case, the [method] may need to be re-validated, as the alteration of data processing may have changed the output… If the output changes (because of the use of an alternative data processing pipeline), then the apparent performance could change."

Read that as an engineer and it says: the analysis code is part of the method.

Every performance number in this field — catches 85% of real allergens, 96% of the dangerous chemicals correctly flagged, 98 times out of 100 it ranks the risky drug above the safe one — is a property of the method plus its pipeline, measured against a reference set of chemicals whose true answers are known from human or animal data. The number is not a fact about the biology. It is a fact about the whole computation.

So swapping in a better curve-fitting routine does not give you the same test with better software. It gives you a different test whose published accuracy no longer describes it, running under the same guideline name, citing a validation study that measured something else.

Now consider what an autonomous laboratory does. It optimises. And the analysis is the single cheapest place in the whole system to find improvement — no reagents, no plates, no waiting. It is also the one place where improvement is indistinguishable from invalidation, because the correctness signal is not in the loop. It is in a validation report the loop never reads.

Three ways that goes wrong, none of which trips an alarm:

  • A better outlier rule. Discard more noisy wells; reproducibility improves; the reported dose shifts. Nothing in the data says this is wrong.
  • A better curve fit. Allow curve shapes the guideline assumed away, and the answer changes for exactly the chemicals where the shape mattered most.
  • A cheaper measurement. Drop the separate "are the cells even alive?" channel to save a step — and silently remove the check that stops a dying culture being reported as a biologically active one.

That third one is not hypothetical. It is the architecture behind Tox21, the US federal robotic screening programme that has run 10,000 chemicals a week since 2013: every cell-based readout there is paired with an independent viability measurement in the same well, precisely so a dying cell is never mistaken for a responding one. This vault filed that on 13 July as a clever counter-design against a specific kind of shared blind spot. It is that — and it is also a pipeline feature, and the ALTEX paper warns explicitly that re-analysis by an alternative pipeline "may not consider such rules in the same way."

The second paper, and why two is much better than one

Four months earlier, in Archives of Toxicology, a different group reached the same place from a different direction.

That paper was led by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre — specifically the unit that houses the European Union's reference laboratory for alternatives to animal testing, the body that decides whether a non-animal method is fit to be used in regulation. Its subject was narrower: methods that work by taking pictures of cells. Its findings land on exactly the same layer, and are blunter about why the layer is dark.

  • On step 0, the instrument: "Manufacturers are reluctant to disclose details on the instrument settings and controls" — with the stated consequence that "only few imaging-based… methods would be used for regulatory toxicology."
  • On working practice: "many researchers relying on the acquired images themselves to set the acquisition parameters for each experiment." Exposure and brightness tuned per run, by eye, against the data being collected, and not recorded.
  • On the analysis software: it carries "the proprietary nature of the used algorithms with little insight of their precise makeup."
  • Even the plastic: "Current standards inadequately address plate bottom flatness." Microscopes have trouble focusing on dishes that are not flat, and nobody standardised flatness.

This vault has a rail it applies constantly: when two tests agree, ask whether they could have failed the same way. Two results that share a hidden cause are one vote, not two. Usually the rail is used to demote a comfortable agreement.

Here it does the opposite, and that's worth saying out loud. These two papers share two authors out of fifty-four. Different journals. Different convening institutions — a German university's toxicology centre in one case, the European Commission's research arm in the other. Different technical territory: general data pipelines versus microscope images. They converge anyway. By the same reasoning the vault uses to discount false agreement, this one counts.

The bill nobody has itemised: a trained model is a second method

The imaging paper adds something the vault had not been tracking, and it changes how one of this vault's running stories should be told.

Four of the methods here are stuck in a tier called human judgement is load-bearing — meaning a trained person sits at a microscope and counts things. Counting small dark spots inside cells. Grading clumps of transformed cells. Scoring malformations in fish embryos. Over the past ten days this vault has found a published machine-vision system for every one of them, and has been treating that as the obvious escape route: build the classifier, validate it, get it written into the guideline, and the method graduates.

The European Commission's paper attaches two conditions to that route.

The first is technical. A deep-learning image scorer has its own applicability domain — its own set of conditions outside which its answers are meaningless. But that domain is not defined by chemistry. It is defined by the pictures it was trained on: that microscope, that staining batch, that cell density, that operator's exposure setting. The named failure mode is domain shift — "discrepancies in statistics between training and test data."

This is worse than it sounds, and here is why: it is invisible to every check the test already runs. Those checks test the biology — did the positive control respond, did the negative control stay quiet. All of them can pass on images that lie well outside what the model has ever seen. The dish is valid; the model is extrapolating; nothing reports it.

And this vault already holds a measured example it had filed under the wrong heading. The machine-vision scorer for the cell transformation assay (a test for cancer-causing potential) scores 0.95 on the chemical it was trained on and 0.87 on chemicals it hasn't seen. The vault recorded that as "single-lab, weaker evidence." It is more specific and more useful than that: it is the model's own applicability domain being crossed, with the cost visible only because somebody happened to measure it. Nobody has measured it for the other three.

The second condition is not technical at all, and it is the one with no expiry date:

"AI algorithms excel at automated tasks, human expertise remains irreplaceable for confirming and interpreting the outcome and toxicologists will maintain responsibility for final interpretation and decision-making."

Read that precisely, because it is easy to over-read. It does not say the counting can't be automated — the same paper documents a test whose scoring is done end-to-end by two neural networks, in production, today. It says that responsibility for the interpretation stays with a person. The measurement can move. The accountability may not.

That is a claim about who answers for the result, not about what a computer can do, and improving the technology does not dissolve it. It also rhymes with something this vault found on the other side of the Atlantic two weeks ago: the US Food and Drug Administration prices an AI model's evidentiary burden partly by how alone it is in the decision, and states that adding a human cross-check "would reduce" that burden. Two regulators, two continents, both structurally rewarding a human in the loop.

So where does that leave the three axes?

This vault grades every method on three things that move independently, and refusing to infer one from another is the whole discipline of the exercise.

Scientific validity. For this finding: in-silico-mechanistic — the vault's floor. Both papers are reasoning documents with no experiments of their own. The claim that pipelines change answers is carried entirely by two studies they cite (five statistical procedures that changed the outcome of a developmental-neurotoxicity test; five mathematical models that gave different answers on one mixture dataset). Neither is reproduced here. The worked examples are promised in "a follow-up publication" that does not exist yet, and that is the part a reader would need to check the claim.

Regulatory acceptance. none. Nothing here is adopted anywhere. The one real hook is a footnote: the revision of the guidance document that defines what validation means is said to be considering data-processing documentation as a readiness criterion. In progress, not law.

Loop-readiness. This isn't an assay, so it sits off the ladder as part of the model layer. But the finding about everything else's loop-readiness is the point of the article, and it is a qualification rather than a demotion: the top tier certifies the decision rule, not the six steps under it.

One thing worth flagging about the evidence, since this vault is meant to be honest about who paid. Both papers argue for a disclosure burden — publish your pipeline, publish your model versions, publish your instrument settings. And both were co-written by the people that burden would land on: instrument manufacturers, image-analysis software vendors, and large chemical and consumer-goods companies. That is the opposite of the usual direction of interest, and it is recorded as unusual rather than as reassuring.

The uncomfortable summary

The remedy is embarrassingly familiar to anyone who ships software. Version the pipeline. Pin it. Treat a pipeline change as a change to the method, requiring re-qualification. Keep a set of known inputs and check that they still produce the known outputs — the European Commission paper asks for exactly this, in its own vocabulary: verify "with a fixed set of input images and by proving that the results obtained are consistent." That is a regression test with golden files, requested by a regulator.

All of it is cheap. All of it is also precisely the kind of overhead an optimising system deletes first, because it consumes resources and produces nothing measurable.

That is now the third time in five days this vault has arrived at the same shape of conclusion from a completely different starting point. Pin the random seed, or a decision rule that uses random sampling gives you a different answer on the same plate. Pin the reference set, or a classifier that compares each sample to its neighbours changes its mind when you change the neighbours. Pin the pipeline, or the method you validated is not the method you are running.

Three findings, three sources, one instruction: the loop's correctness lives in the boring parts, and the boring parts are what optimisation removes.

And here is the thing that should stay with an engineer reading this. We tend to assume the hard part of automating a laboratory is the laboratory — the robot arms, the liquid handling, the physical mess of biology. The evidence in this vault keeps pointing the other way. The robots have worked since 2013. What is missing is a written-down, versioned, publicly obtainable specification of what to do with the numbers.

Not a better model. A spec.
