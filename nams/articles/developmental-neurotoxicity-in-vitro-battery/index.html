Testing for developmental neurotoxicity without the animal study: how far the in-vitro battery actually is

Is there a non-animal way to test whether a chemical harms a developing nervous system — and is it ready to lean on? The honest answer is "there's a real, internationally-agreed battery, and it is good science — but it is not an adopted Test Guideline, and it hasn't yet been benchmarked against the animal results it's meant to stand in for." That gap between scientifically validated and regulatorily accepted is the whole story of the developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) in-vitro battery, and it's a cleaner example of the two-axis distinction than almost anything else in this vault.

Why a battery, not an assay

Developmental neurotoxicity is not one thing. A developing brain has to get many separate processes right — progenitor cells have to proliferate the right amount and not die off, migrate to the right places, differentiate into neurons and glia, grow and mature neurites, wire up synapses, and form working networks. No single dish-based test captures all of that, so the field did the only sensible thing and built a battery: 17 in-vitro assays spanning 9 key neurodevelopmental processes (13 of the assays are human-cell-based, 4 are rat-based), read together inside an integrated approach to testing and assessment (IATA) rather than scored by a simple vote. This is the same "combine assays that each read one step of the biology" architecture as the skin-sensitisation 2-out-of-3 defined approach — but larger, and integrated by expert judgement rather than a fixed rule. The details of the coverage are in the method map and its concept note.

Where the science axis sits: genuinely validated

On scientific validity, this is not a single lab's proof-of-concept. The battery was standardised by a multi-party effort — EFSA, the US EPA, the US NIEHS/NTP and academic groups — and in April 2023 the OECD's Working Party of National Coordinators (WNT) approved it, with the OECD publishing Initial Recommendations on Evaluation of Data from the DNT In-Vitro Testing Battery (OECD, 2023). Multiple labs reproduce it, and it has a defined domain in terms of which neurodevelopmental processes it covers. In this vault's grading that earns evidence_tier: formally-validated — the tier just below a fully adopted method. So: promising is an understatement; this is a mature, agreed-upon battery.

Where the regulatory axis sits — and the caveat that keeps it honest

Here is the part it would be easy to overstate. The DNT battery is recognised OECD guidance, not an adopted Test Guideline. A guidance document telling you how to evaluate battery data is a real regulatory foothold, but it is not the same as a Test Guideline that a program can require and accept for a stand-alone DNT call. On the acceptance axis this vault marks it under-validation, not OECD-TG-adopted — deliberately kept distinct from the strong science score.

And there is a specific, load-bearing scientific limitation that the honest version has to state: there is no published proof-of-principle comparing points of departure derived from the DNT battery to in-vivo DNT reference values. In other words, the battery reliably flags the neurodevelopmental processes a chemical disturbs, but the field has not yet demonstrated that the doses it implies line up with what the animal studies would have said. That is exactly the kind of gap that separates "we can measure this" from "we can replace the existing test with this."

The missing piece that's now arriving: dose

A battery reports effects at in-vitro concentrations; a regulator needs a human-relevant external dose. The bridge is toxicokinetic modelling — PBK modelling and quantitative in-vitro-to-in-vivo extrapolation (QIVIVE), tracked in this vault as its own method map. It is not a side note here: when the OECD adopted its Principles of QIVIVE (Working Party on Hazard Assessment, June 2025), it worked them through the DNT battery as the example endpoint. So the two are moving together — the battery supplies the bioactivity, PBK/QIVIVE supplies the dose — and watching whether that pairing finally produces the missing proof-of-principle against in-vivo reference values is the single most useful thing to track next.

Bottom line. For developmental neurotoxicity, the in-vitro battery is real, internationally standardised, and scientifically well along — formally-validated on the science axis. But it is guidance, not an adopted Test Guideline (under-validation on the acceptance axis), and it has not yet been shown to reproduce in-vivo DNT dose benchmarks. It is a strong, consolidating approach you should take seriously and should not yet lean on for a stand-alone regulatory DNT decision.
