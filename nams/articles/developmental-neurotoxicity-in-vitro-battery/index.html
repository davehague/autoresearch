Is there a non-animal way to test whether a chemical harms a developing nervous system — and is it ready to lean on? The honest answer, updated: there is a real, internationally-agreed battery of laboratory tests that hundreds of chemicals have now been through; it has never been formally validated as a battery; and nothing it produces can conclude anything by itself. That last clause is not a technicality. It is the entire story.

Why a battery, not a single test

Developmental neurotoxicity is not one thing. A developing brain has to get many separate processes right — progenitor cells must multiply the right amount and not die off, travel to the right places, turn into the right cell types, grow and mature their connections, and wire into working networks. No single dish-based test captures all of that, so the field built a battery: 17 in-vitro assays spanning 8 key neurodevelopmental processes (7 of the assays use human cells, the rest rat), read together inside an integrated approach to testing and assessment (IATA) rather than scored by a simple vote.

(Small housekeeping note that says something about how young this field is: this article said "9 processes" until tonight. The OECD's own 2026 account tabulates 8. The vault has corrected the number and recorded that it cannot tell from the outside whether a process was merged, dropped, or simply counted differently.)

The architecture is the same "combine assays that each read one step of the biology" pattern as the skin-sensitisation 2-out-of-3 defined approach — but larger, and integrated by expert judgement rather than a fixed rule. Hold onto that difference; it turns out to be worth more than every other difference between them combined.

The correction: standardised is not validated

Here is the sentence that moved this method's status, from From concept to regulation: OECD's role in the development of the DNT in vitro battery (2026), written jointly by the OECD Secretariat, the US validation centre NICEATM, and EFSA:

"no formal validation procedure has yet been completed for the DNT-IVB as an integrated battery."

This vault graded the battery formally-validated for two weeks, reasoning that a battery standardised across EFSA, the US EPA, NIEHS and two European academic institutes, and endorsed by the OECD, must have cleared an inter-laboratory validation. That was an inference, and it was wrong.

The two things are genuinely different, and the difference is worth spelling out because it recurs everywhere in this field. Standardisation means many labs have agreed on how to run the tests — same protocol, same acceptance criteria, same analysis pipeline. That is a real achievement and it happened. Formal validation asks a different question: does the assembled battery, run blind, give the right answer? It has an apparatus — coded reference chemicals, pre-set acceptance criteria, an independent management team, multiple laboratories that do not know what they are testing. That apparatus exists and gets used; it was applied to a human thyroid assay earlier this year. It has not been applied to this battery.

Read who is saying it. The first author works at the OECD, in the division that runs the Test Guideline programme. The second is at the US validation centre. The third is at EFSA. The paper calls the battery "a flagship OECD project." These are the three organisations with the strongest reason to present it as further along than it is, and they put the gap in their own limitations list. When that happens, believe it.

What it is used for, and what it never concludes

None of this means the battery is idle or bad. It is running constantly:

  • EFSA: more than 200 compounds
  • US NIEHS: about 200 more
  • US EPA: roughly 250, including about 160 PFAS ("forever chemicals")

That is around 650 chemicals, with harmonised analysis and centralised databases. And here is the disposition of every single one, in the OECD's words:

"These applications did not treat DNT-IVB outcomes as stand-alone replacements for guideline studies, but rather as complementary evidence streams informing regulatory judgement."

Six hundred and fifty chemicals tested, and not one regulatory decision made by the battery alone. Every result went to an expert who weighed it alongside other evidence. The tests inform; a person decides.

The part that should interest anyone who builds automated systems

This is the finding that made tonight's run worth doing, and it is buried in the methods section of a paper about something else entirely.

The battery's flagship human assay is already scored by machine, end to end. Images are captured automatically on a high-content imaging platform. The cells in them are identified by two convolutional neural networks — trained, the authors note, on historical human-scored data. Neurites are measured by an algorithm. The dose–response curves are fitted by a script. Six of the assay's seven readouts run without a human touching them. (The seventh, one of the migration measurements, is still done by hand in ImageJ.)

So: the liquid handling is automated, the microscopy is automated, the image interpretation is a neural network, the statistics are scripted — and the method still cannot make a regulatory call.

Because none of that was ever the obstacle. The obstacle is that after all the automation produces its numbers, nobody has written down the rule that turns those numbers into a sentence like "this chemical is a developmental neurotoxicant." The OECD names this gap precisely, distinguishing an IATA — which "necessarily involves some degree of expert judgment" — from a defined approach, whose predictions are "rule-based." And it names the fix as an aspiration:

"The aspiration is to increase standardization and pave the way towards the development of Defined Approaches for DNT."

An aspiration. No date, no draft, no candidate rule. For calibration: the liver-enzyme-induction method this vault tracks was validated in 2014 and became an official Test Guideline in 2026. Twelve years, for a method that had already cleared the harder scientific bar.

Compare the skin-sensitisation battery, which is the whole lesson in one comparison. It uses three assays. This one uses seventeen, with more automation, more chemicals run, and better imaging. The skin-sensitisation battery can be run start-to-finish by a machine; this one cannot. The entire difference is that a committee wrote the skin-sensitisation rule down. More assays, more data and more automation do not add up to a decision rule. One paragraph of agreed arithmetic does.

Two further limits, both new tonight

It has holes it can name, and they are the awkward kind. A 2026 study probed the flagship assay with 18 signalling pathways known to matter in human neurodevelopmental disease. Sixteen produced a measurable response. Two — STAT3 and TrkB — produced nothing at all, putting them outside what the assay can see. One of them governs the development of astrocytes, a brain cell type the battery does not assess at all.

Worse for anyone hoping to automate this: one of the seven readouts, neuronal migration, responded to none of the eighteen pathways, and in the same group's earlier screen it flagged one chemical out of 120.

Why this is harder than it sounds: most limits on a test are chemical and therefore checkable — a machine can measure a chemical's acidity or greasiness and refuse to test it if it falls outside the validated range. This limit is biological. To know whether a chemical falls in the assay's blind spot, you would need to know which biological pathway it acts through — which is what you ran the test to find out. A negative result from this battery cannot distinguish "this chemical is safe for brain development" from "this chemical works through something we cannot see." A human expert inside an IATA can hold that ambiguity. An automated screen cannot, unless someone writes the caveat into the rule.

And the non-animal tests are still partly made of animals. A separate 2026 audit read the shopping lists for the seven human-cell assays. All seven have removed fetal bovine serum — the notorious ingredient everyone knows about. All seven still contain other animal-derived material: a gel scaffold extracted from mouse tumours (about 16 mice per 100 mL), albumin from cattle blood, antibodies raised in mice and goats. The authors' concern is not only ethical — these materials vary from batch to batch in ways nobody documents, and the relevant OECD good-practice guidance covers serum but not the scaffolds or the albumin.

For an automated system that is a specific hazard. A closed loop assumes that when the readout changes, the chemistry changed. A human who has worked in a cell-culture lab for ten years eventually learns to suspect the new lot of gel. A learning algorithm has no concept of "reagent batch" at all — it will treat the shift as real information and learn from it.

The number nobody had

(New, 2026-07-30.) For most of this article's life the battery had no published performance figure at all in this vault — a striking absence for something 650 chemicals have been through. One arrived in July, from an unexpected direction: the authors of a competing method, a zebrafish behavioural screen, cited it while positioning their own assay above the battery in a testing sequence.

86% sensitivity, 60% specificity.

Translated: the battery catches roughly six of every seven chemicals that really are developmental neurotoxicants, which is respectable. And it wrongly flags two of every five chemicals that are actually safe.

Sit with the second number, because it is the one that matters for automation. A 40% false-alarm rate is survivable when a human expert reads every result inside a weight-of-evidence assessment and can say "that one's an artefact." It is close to unusable as the reward signal for a machine that picks its own next experiment — an optimiser trained against it would chase alarms two times in five and have no way to know.

Two honest caveats on the number itself: it was reported by people with an interest in the comparison, not measured here, and it is a single figure for a battery whose 17 assays surely differ. Recorded as suggestive.

And it makes a July argument concrete. Three toxicologists at the chemical company BASF published a commentary this month arguing that "expanding test batteries without predefined integration rules inflates false-positive accumulation" — that a battery read under an "any positive is a concern" convention gets more alarmist as it grows, because every added test is another chance to fire spuriously. That is an argument with no data behind it. This battery is 17 assays with no integration rule and a 60% specificity, which is what that argument predicts. Not proof; a consistent instance.

What it took to actually use it

(New, 2026-07-30.) This article has said since July that 650 chemicals went through the battery and not one regulatory decision was made by it alone. That was a summary statistic with nothing behind it. A case study published on 29 July shows the shape of one of those 650, and it is more informative than the statistic.

An older animal study of acibenzolar-S-methyl — an agricultural compound that switches on a plant's own immune defences rather than killing pests — produced equivocal brain morphometry findings. In plain terms: somebody measured brain structures in the offspring of dosed rats and got numbers that were ambiguous. Not clean enough to call an effect, not clean enough to dismiss. Ambiguity like that is expensive; it can force another animal study or a permanently conservative safety margin.

The battery was run to decide whether to believe that rat result. Not to replace the rat study — to adjudicate it.

The compound and its metabolite were run through all 17 assays, with test concentrations chosen by a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model — a computer simulation of how a dose moves through a body and what concentration it actually reaches in tissue — so the dish would mimic the animal. Results: the parent compound did nothing, anywhere. The metabolite moved one assay out of seventeen, weakly, at a concentration the body model says is never reached at the doses that produced the ambiguous measurements. Conclusion: the rat finding does not indicate a real hazard.

Two things follow, and the second is the important one

First, the honest reading of the direction. A non-animal battery whose realised job is to interpret animal studies is making existing animal data go further. That is a reduction argument, not a replacement one — a distinction this vault keeps having to draw, and one worth keeping visible.

Second — and this is the finding — look at what actually decided the answer.

  1. Seventeen mostly-automated assays returned numbers.
  2. Sixteen were quiet. One was not.
  3. A second computer model was consulted, and said that concentration is unreachable in a living animal.
  4. A person decided the gap was big enough.

No assay's decision rule appears anywhere in that chain. For a month this article has said the battery's blocker is that "nobody has written down the rule." Here is what that abstraction looks like in practice: the last step consumes a second model and then an unquantified human judgement about sufficiency, and no published procedure states how large the margin between an in-vitro active concentration and a modelled in-vivo concentration has to be before you may dismiss the hit.

So automating this would require more than writing a rule. The loop would have to carry a toxicokinetic model for each specific chemical inside its own cycle — and those models are built per compound, as research, not looked up.

And here is the uncomfortable part, which is new. Suppose someone did write the margin rule down. The step becomes automatable — and the automated version would be dismissing positive results on the strength of a model whose own uncertainty is not in the rule. Quickly, consistently, with every error pointing the same way. This is the first blocker in this vault whose removal is not unambiguously an improvement.

One caveat that has to be said plainly: the company that registers this chemical co-authored the paper clearing it, and the company that sells the battery co-authored the paper endorsing it. US EPA scientists are on the same paper, which is a real counterweight and is not the same thing as independent review. And a negative result is the weakest possible demonstration of any test — nothing is what this battery finds when it works, and also what it finds when it is blind, and the section above lists exactly where it is blind.

The automation finished, and nothing changed

(New, 2026-08-10.) This article has argued since July that the battery's obstacle was never the robots. On 1 August a paper closed the last gap in that argument, and the result is the cleanest demonstration of the point the vault has.

Fourteen authors led by the US National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, its validation centre and the US EPA ran 95 chemicals — drugs, flame retardants, fungicides, herbicides, industrial chemicals including PFAS, insecticides, and negative controls — through the battery's eight neurodevelopmental processes. Then they did something the battery had not had before: they built a fully computational integration pipeline. Curve fitting to a benchmark concentration, a multi-objective optimisation step, and a weighted scoring scheme that combines all seventeen assay outputs into one number per chemical.

Put that next to what this article already reported — automated liquid handling, automated microscopy, two neural networks doing the cell scoring, scripted statistics — and the chain from plate to number now runs end to end with nobody touching it.

And the thing it produces is a ranked list. Fungicides, drugs and insecticides came out highest. That is genuinely useful — it tells a programme what to look at first. It is not a hazard classification, and no published threshold converts one of these scores into the sentence "this chemical is a developmental neurotoxicant." Nor could anyone responsibly choose a threshold today, because there is no reference-chemical performance figure for the combined score to calibrate against.

So the blocker is unchanged, and it is now specifiable in a single sentence: the pipeline is complete and its output is an ordering, in a regulatory system that needs a category. For an autonomous laboratory that distinction is the whole game. An ordering is something an optimiser can happily chase forever without ever producing an answer anybody is allowed to act on.

(One awkward detail worth surfacing, because it cuts against the battery's own framing: the paper's most sensitive leg for potency was a zebrafish neurobehaviour assay, which the authors themselves label "an in vivo assay" — a living vertebrate embryo. The most sensitive part of the non-animal battery is, on this paper's own labelling, an animal.)

A ranking that depends on the company it keeps

(New, 2026-08-10.) The same paper reports something that should worry anyone planning to put this battery in a loop, and it is easy to read past.

Some hits are selective — the chemical disturbs a neurodevelopmental process at a concentration below the one that simply kills the cells. Others are not selective; the chemical is just poisonous, and the neurodevelopmental readout moves because everything moves. Only the first kind is evidence of specific harm to a developing nervous system.

The authors found that "selectivity varies significantly by assay and chemical class, with neurite outgrowth assays and fungicides exhibiting the highest percentage of selective hits."

Follow that through. A chemical's score is computed relative to the others in the set, and the fraction of selective hits differs by chemical class. So a chemical's rank depends on what it was ranked against, and the pipeline carries no correction for it.

For a human running a fixed screening campaign this is a manageable caveat. For a machine that chooses its own next experiments it is a trap with a particular shape: the system would concentrate its follow-up on the classes the battery happens to see best, get a high hit rate there, and read that high hit rate as evidence it was looking in the right place. It would be measuring its own bias and scoring it as discovery — the same structural failure this vault flags whenever two assays in a battery fail on the same chemicals for the same reason, arriving here through arithmetic rather than through biology.

Two things landed that change nothing, and both are worth a line

(New, 2026-08-10.) The battery got a named seat in the EU's flagship chemical-risk programme — it is the first-tier developmental-neurotoxicity screen for eight replacement chemicals for bisphenol A, in a project whose chemical list was co-designed with the European Food Safety Authority and the European Chemicals Agency. That paper contains no results at all; the authors say experimental findings are expected in early 2027. A programme choosing a battery is not a validation of it, and the integration step there is once again a judgement call — substances "may subsequently be prioritized" under a weight-of-evidence framework.

And the OECD has named a hole inside the battery. A fifty-plus-author European consortium paper records that the blood–brain barrier — the filter deciding which chemicals reach a developing brain at all — has been "identified by the OECD as a gap for the DNT in vitro test battery," and that endocrine disruption in the developmental-neurotoxicity context "remains a significant regulatory gap." Work on stem-cell-derived barrier models is planned. No results yet.

That paper carries a conflict this article has now recorded twice from two different sources, and it should be said plainly rather than in a footnote: two of its authors hold equity in DNTOX GmbH, the company that sells this battery as a commercial service, on a document naming the battery's gaps and proposing the programme to fill them. The journal states the company's funders had no role in writing it. The same two names appear on the 95-chemical paper above, alongside an author at the company selling the zebrafish assay that paper reports as most sensitive. Public agencies are co-authors on both, which is a real counterweight and is not the same thing as independent review.

The other gap, still open

A battery reports effects at concentrations in a dish; a regulator needs a human-relevant dose. The bridge is toxicokinetic modelling — PBK modelling and quantitative in-vitro-to-in-vivo extrapolation (QIVIVE), tracked here as its own method map. When the OECD adopted its principles for that bridge in 2025, it used this battery as the worked example. But EFSA's expert panel confirmed in 2025 that no published study yet compares doses derived from this battery against the animal study results they would replace. That gap was open when this article was first written and it is still open.

Bottom line

Corrected, and stated on all three of the axes this vault tracks separately:

  • Scientific validity: peer-reviewed-method — downgraded from formally-validated on 2026-07-20. Real, extensively used science, standardised across many labs, with no completed formal validation of the assembled battery, per the OECD's own account.
  • Regulatory acceptance: under-validation — unchanged. OECD-approved descriptive documentation and Initial Recommendations for evaluating the data. Not a Test Guideline. A genuine foothold; not a licence to make the call.
  • Loop-readiness: automatable-open-loop — unchanged, well-sourced rather than assumed, and as sharp as this vault can currently make it. As of 2026-08-10 the chain from plate to number runs end to end with no human in it: automated liquid handling, automated imaging, neural-network cell scoring, scripted statistics, and now a fully computational integration pipeline that combines all seventeen assays into one score. The specific blocker is that the completed pipeline emits a ranking and the regulatory system needs a category — no published threshold converts a score into a call, and no reference-chemical performance figure exists to calibrate one against. The OECD lists the fix as an aspiration with no date. Three further blockers sit behind it: the ranking is not independent of the chemical set it was computed over, so an automated system choosing its own next experiments would amplify its own bias; the applicability domain is defined by biology, which a machine cannot check for itself; and, from a July analysis of the data pipeline, outcome-changing choices at the curve-fitting and statistics layers sit upstream of the missing rule. And the one instance the vault holds of the battery actually deciding something consumed a second computational model plus an unquantified human judgement about how large a concentration margin has to be.

One more thing worth knowing about who uses these methods at all. Japan's medicines regulator published an audit this month of five years of its own approval files for products carrying an active cosmetic claim — the easiest case, where non-animal skin and eye tests have had official guidelines for years. Non-animal methods appeared in 22 of 115 tests: about one in five. Uptake was concentrated almost entirely where an adopted guideline exists and stopped there. That is a different endpoint and a different jurisdiction from this article's subject, so it proves nothing about this battery. But it points at the same variable: where somebody wrote the rule down, the method gets used; where nobody did, the animal study still happens.

The one thing to carry away. This is the clearest case this vault holds that automating a measurement and automating a judgement are different projects. Seventeen assays, six hundred and fifty chemicals, neural networks doing the scoring — and the thing standing between all of it and an autonomous laboratory is a paragraph of agreed arithmetic that nobody has written yet. The 2026-07-30 refresh adds the sting: when that paragraph does get written, it will be encoding a judgement that four expert groups would probably make differently, and the machine will apply it faster than anyone can notice.

This is a learning synthesis of the public evidence, not regulatory advice. Read the method map for the dated through-line as this keeps moving — and it moved a long way tonight, downward.
