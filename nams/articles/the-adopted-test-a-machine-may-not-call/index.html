The adopted test a machine may not call

Is this method ready, what for, and could a machine run it? Ready, yes — this is one of the most firmly established non-animal safety tests there is, required by regulators worldwide, and it has been for fifteen years. Ready for what needs a caveat you should hear early: it wrongly flags somewhere between a third and two-thirds of harmless chemicals, and every one of those false alarms historically bought somebody an animal study to sort it out. And could a machine run it? A machine can already do the hard-looking part. It is not allowed to do the part that looks easy — and working out why is the most useful thing this test has to teach about automating toxicology.

What the test is

When a cell divides badly, something can get left behind. A broken piece of a chromosome, or occasionally an entire chromosome that failed to make the trip, ends up stranded outside the cell's main nucleus in its own little membrane. That stranded blob is a micronucleus, and it is one of the clearest visible signs that a chemical has damaged a cell's genetic material.

The in-vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test — "in vitro" meaning in a dish rather than in an animal — grows cultured cells, exposes them to the chemical, and counts how many end up with micronuclei. It is OECD Test Guideline 487, which is the internationally agreed protocol for exactly this: the OECD writes the recipes that regulators in dozens of countries accept as valid ways to test a chemical. TG 487 was adopted in 2010 and revised in July 2023.

Its particular strength is that it catches two different kinds of genetic damage in one experiment. Clastogens break chromosomes; the pieces get left behind. Aneugens interfere with the machinery that pulls chromosomes apart, so a whole chromosome gets left behind. Most tests see one or the other. This one sees both, because both end up as the same visible blob.

It is a core member of the standard genotoxicity battery, alongside the Ames test (OECD Test Guideline 471 — bacteria engineered so that a mutation lets them grow, so counting colonies counts mutations) and the mammalian gene-mutation assays (OECD Test Guidelines 476 and 490). Together those cover the two main ways a chemical can damage DNA: small mutations within a gene, and damage at the scale of whole chromosomes.

Where it stands on all three axes

This vault grades a method on three things that move independently, and this test is a good argument for why they should never be collapsed into one.

Is the science sound? Yes, at the top of the scale — evidence_tier: regulatory-adopted. It has been through European validation, endorsed by ECVAM (the EU's reference laboratory for alternatives to animal testing) and its scientific committee, and reproduced everywhere.

Has anyone accepted it? Yes, at the top of that scale too — regulatory_status: OECD-TG-adopted. Status consensus.

Could a machine run it end to end? No. loop_readiness: human-in-the-loop. And this is the vault's sharpest case of the three axes disagreeing, because on the first two measures nothing in this vault ranks higher.

The number that should bother you first

Before the automation question, the accuracy question, because it drives everything else.

Specificity is the fraction of genuinely harmless chemicals a test correctly clears. This test's specificity is reported at somewhere between 30% and 65% depending on the dataset. In plain terms: give it a hundred safe chemicals and it will raise the alarm on between thirty-five and seventy of them.

That is not a scandal — it is a designed trade. The test is deliberately tuned to miss as few real hazards as possible, and the price of that is false alarms. The battery's design assumes that a positive gets followed up rather than believed. But it is the reason so much of the recent work around this assay is about fixing the false positives, and it is why an alternative that halves them matters.

One such alternative is in this vault: TGx-DDI, a genomic test that reads which of 64 genes the cells switch on rather than looking at them, reported at 86% specificity in a four-laboratory blinded trial (PMID: 41031489) — roughly halving the false-alarm rate. It is not adopted anywhere, and it is blind to aneugens by design, which is precisely this test's strength. They are complementary, and they are also not independent — more on that below.

Now the automation question, and it has two halves

Here is the part that makes this assay worth an article.

Half one — reading the plate. For fifteen years, the answer to "why can't a machine run the micronucleus test?" was: because a trained person has to look down a microscope and recognise micronuclei by their shape. Morphological judgement. Not the kind of thing you automate.

That answer is now demonstrably wrong, twice over, by two different routes.

The first route is machine vision. Wills et al. 2021 (Archives of Toxicology, PMID: 34036417) combined imaging flow cytometry — cells photographed one at a time as they flow past a camera — with a deep-learning image classifier. Trained on data from two laboratories, it scored an unseen third laboratory without reconfiguration, and it reproduced the benchmark dose — the dose figure that actually drives the safety decision. That is a stronger demonstration than any other machine-vision escape hatch in this vault: inter-laboratory, and matching the number that matters rather than just the classification.

The second route is more mundane and possibly more important. In 2026 an industrial laboratory ran the assay across 28 chemicals and scored it flow cytometrically — using the Litron In Vitro MicroFlow method, over 5,000 nuclei per sample — with no images and no classifier at all (PMID: 40655310). No machine vision required, because a different instrument simply measures the thing directly.

Both routes have a catch worth stating. The deep-learning scorer runs only on the Amnis ImageStream, made by a single manufacturer, and a Luminex employee co-authored the paper — an access_gate and an industry funding flag on the automation claim itself. It also has no ring trial (no independent multi-laboratory validation), is not part of the guideline, and was still around 25% wrong on the micronucleus-positive class, needing roughly 19,000 hand-labelled cells to train. And the vault should note a bias in its own searching: it reads the automation literature, so it over-finds machine learning and under-finds instrumentation.

Half two — making the call. This is the blocker, and it does not look like a technology problem.

Counting micronuclei produces a number. Turning that number into "this chemical is genotoxic" requires the guideline's data-interpretation step: a statistical comparison against historical control data, plus a judgement about biological relevance and whether the cells were simply being poisoned rather than genetically damaged.

And in 2025 the European Commission's Joint Research Centre — the unit that hosts EURL ECVAM, writing alongside the European Food Safety Authority and Germany's federal risk-assessment institute — put the position in writing:

"AI algorithms excel at automated tasks, human expertise remains irreplaceable for confirming and interpreting the outcome and toxicologists will maintain responsibility for final interpretation and decision-making."

Read that precisely, because the precision is the point. It does not say the scoring cannot be automated — the same paper documents assays scored by convolutional neural networks. It says responsibility for the interpretation stays with a person.

That is an accountability constraint, not a capability one. It does not expire when the models get better.

And it rhymes with something arriving from the other side of the Atlantic: the US Food and Drug Administration's framework for AI in regulatory decisions prices model influence — how alone the model is in the decision — as a component of risk, and states explicitly that a human cross-check "would reduce the AI model influence" and therefore the evidence you must supply. Two regulators, two continents, both structurally rewarding a human in the loop.

So the honest summary of this assay's automation story is: the readout can move, the call may not. The vault had been modelling the promotion path as build → validate → guideline adopts → tier promotes. Step three may terminate short of the tier, for reasons that have nothing to do with how good the scorer gets.

The blind spot, and why it is worse for a machine than for a person

This vault flags a correlated blind spot whenever two tests in the same battery fail on the same chemicals for the same structural reason. When that happens, the battery casts one vote, not two.

This test carries one, and it has three members. The micronucleus assay, TGx-DDI, and the human donor-cell variability panel all run in lymphoblastoid cells — a family of human cells that does not perform xenobiotic metabolism natively. Two of them borrow it, by adding S9: a preparation of rat liver enzymes tipped into the dish. The third adds nothing at all.

So a pro-mutagen — a chemical that is harmless as drawn and becomes DNA-damaging only after an enzyme modifies it — reads negative in all three, for one shared reason: rat liver enzymes in a tube do not perform every route a human liver does.

Why this matters more to an automated system than to a person. A human assessor holding two concordant negatives on a chemical with a structural alert for bioactivation has somewhere to go: distrust the panel, order something different. An automated loop reads concordance as confirmation. Its decision rules both returned negative and they agree, which is what the loop is built to treat as a settled answer. It records the chemical as clean, and the model that picks its next experiment learns from that. Speed converts a known bias into a compounding one.

There is a candidate repair, and the vault holds it open rather than closed. Allemang & Pfuhler 2026 swapped the standard TK6 lymphoblast line for HepaRG, a metabolically competent human liver line, and accuracy rose from 75% to 86% across 28 chemicals — entirely by removing false positives (9 of 9 correct on the known-safe-but-flagged set, against 3 of 9 before). That is exactly the right move: the fix for a correlated blind spot is a measurement that fails differently, not a bigger battery.

Three brakes, all load-bearing. First, and it is remarkable: "Compounds requiring metabolic activation were not selected for this study" — the metabolic fix was evaluated on the chemical set that excludes the failure mode it fixes. Second, the trade runs under-protective: sensitivity fell from 14 of 14 to 12 of 14. Third, HepaRG is patented and single-licensor (Lonza), so the accuracy upgrade makes the assay harder to replicate elsewhere — an access_gate where the old cell line had none. The study is funding_flag: [industry] (Procter & Gamble), one laboratory, no ring trial. The blind spot is credibly addressed, not demonstrably fixed.

What changed on 2 August 2026, and why it makes this test more important rather than less

Two new methods entered this vault tonight: the reconstructed skin Comet assay and the reconstructed skin micronucleus assay (RSMN) — non-animal follow-up tests that run inside lab-grown three-dimensional human skin, for chemicals people put on their skin. They exist to adjudicate exactly the false positives described above.

The RSMN counts the same biological event as this test. It is also scored by a person down a microscope — 500 cells per tissue to find the divided ones, then 1,000 of those to count micronuclei.

That makes three eye-scored micronucleus assays in this vault: this one, the buccal cytome assay for human biomonitoring, and now RSMN. The demonstrated automated scorer covers only this one, because it was shown on cells flowing in suspension, and reading micronuclei in cells recovered from a three-dimensional tissue is a different imaging problem that nobody in this vault has shown to work.

Last month's cross-method review concluded that a validated automated micronucleus scorer would be the single highest-leverage fix available in the genotoxicity column, because one eye-scored assay blocks a whole battery from running end to end. Tonight the leverage went up and the demonstrated coverage did not.

What to take away

On the science: this is as solid as non-animal toxicology gets — adopted, reproduced, required. Its specificity is genuinely poor and that is a designed trade, not a defect, but it means a positive from this test is a question rather than an answer.

On acceptance: fully adopted, and stable. Nothing here is contested.

On automation: human-in-the-loop. The blocker is not that micronuclei cannot be counted by machine — that has been demonstrated twice, by machine vision and by flow cytometry. The blocker is that the regulatory call stays with a person by design, on accountability grounds that two major regulators have now put in writing and that better models will not dissolve. The specific things still missing before the tier could move are concrete: an independent multi-laboratory validation of an automated scorer, and its adoption into the guideline itself.

And the caution the vault repeats every time: none of the above says this test should be automated. It carries a documented correlated blind spot with two other methods, an instrument dependency on the automation side, and a cell-line dependency on the accuracy-repair side. "A machine could read it" was never the same sentence as "a machine should decide it," and this is the assay where the two came apart most clearly.

This is learning synthesis from public literature and public guidance, not regulatory advice. It does not certify any method for a real testing programme, and it does not certify any method as safe to automate.
