Validated by everyone, adopted by no one

If you want to know whether a new drug will crash a patient's white-blood-cell count, there is a non-animal test that predicts it well, was proven to work by an independent European referee more than twenty years ago, and has never been turned into an official guideline — because the test cannot run without first doing an experiment on a mouse. That last clause is not a footnote. It is the whole reason this method is stuck, and it is a cleaner lesson about how non-animal methods actually get built than any success story in this vault.

The short answers first, because this vault exists to give them plainly. Is the science sound? Yes — as sound as it gets here; this is the best-validated method in the entire vault by the standard that matters most, which is who ran the validation. Has a regulator adopted it? No. Nothing. Twenty years and not a single Test Guideline. Could a machine run it end-to-end? No — and the reason it can't is the same reason it never became a guideline, which is what makes it worth an article. Everything interesting about this method lives in the gap between the first answer and the other two.

What the test measures, in plain terms

Some drugs — chemotherapy agents above all — are toxic to the bone marrow. They damage the factory that makes your blood cells, and the first casualty is usually the neutrophils, the white blood cells that fight bacterial infection. When their count falls far enough you have neutropenia, and a patient in that state can die of an infection that would otherwise be trivial. So a drug developer badly wants to know, before dosing a human, how much of a new compound the marrow can take.

The CFU-GM assay answers that. The name is literal: colony-forming unit — granulocyte/macrophage. You take the immature progenitor cells that live in bone marrow and would normally grow up into those white blood cells, suspend them in a soft gel, add your drug, and count how many still manage to divide and form visible colonies. Fewer colonies means the drug is more toxic to the marrow. It is a clonogenic assay — the readout is just a count of surviving colonies, which matters later.

Here is the clever, and ultimately fatal, part. Counting dead colonies in a dish does not, by itself, tell you a safe human dose. So the method pairs the dish with a small piece of arithmetic. It runs the same assay on mouse progenitor cells and on human progenitor cells, works out the ratio of how sensitive the two species are, and then takes the dose a live mouse is known to tolerate — the mouse maximum tolerated dose (MTD) — and scales it by that ratio to predict the dose a human would tolerate. The model is deliberately mechanism-naïve: it never needs to know how the drug hurts the marrow, only how much more or less human cells feel it than mouse cells do. Read that sequence again, because the mouse experiment sitting inside step three is the entire story.

On the science: the cleanest validation lineage in the vault

Most methods in this vault carry an asterisk on their evidence, and the most common asterisk is who paid for the study. A method validated by the company that sells it is graded more sceptically than one validated by an independent referee — not because the numbers are assumed to be wrong, but because the vault never launders a developer's self-assessment into certainty.

The CFU-GM assay has no asterisk. It went through an international blind trial coordinated by ECVAM — the European Centre for the Validation of Alternative Methods, a public body whose entire job is to referee whether a non-animal method actually works (Pessina et al., Toxicological Sciences 75(2):355–367, 2003; PMID 12883091). Twenty drugs were tested blind across multiple laboratories: fourteen anticancer (antineoplastic) drugs, two antivirals, three other therapeutics, and one pesticide. The result: the model correctly predicted the safe human dose for 17 of the 20 — an 85% hit rate. Of the three it missed, two were off by no more than sixfold and one by roughly tenfold, which for a first-pass dose prediction is close. And crucially, different labs running the same written protocol got the same answers — the method is a property of the assay, not of one skilled technician's hands.

No developer ran this. No sponsor funded it. An independent European reference laboratory ran a blind, multi-lab trial against real human clinical outcomes and the method held up. In the vault's own bookkeeping the funding flag reads none, and nothing here has a cleaner provenance. (One honest note on sourcing: the archived copy of the 2003 paper is an image-only scan with no readable text, so the 17-of-20 figure is taken from the paper's abstract of record on PubMed rather than re-read from the article body. The source note records this so no later reader assumes otherwise.)

On acceptance: nothing, and the reason is in the endorsement itself

Given that record, you would expect an OECD Test Guideline — the internationally agreed spec that lets a company submit a result instead of running the animal test. There isn't one. There has never been one.

What exists instead is a single statement from ECVAM's scientific advisory committee, ESAC, dated 21 March 2006. Its exact wording carries the whole finding, so here it is verbatim:

"unanimously endorsed the CFU-GM assay for predicting acute neutropenia in humans as a substitute to using a second species, such as the dog, for this purpose. It should be noted that the test relies on the availability of mouse MTD data and is, therefore, not a full replacement method, but is intended to reduce the overall numbers of animals needed in toxicity testing. Performance standards for the assay should be developed."

Three things in that paragraph are load-bearing. The endorsement was unanimous — the referees had no doubts about the science. It was also deliberately narrow: the assay may replace the second animal species (drug safety testing has historically used two, typically a rodent and then a dog), not the first. And the committee said out loud why it could never be more than that — the test relies on mouse MTD data, so by construction it is "not a full replacement method." The performance standards ESAC asked for, which are a prerequisite for writing a Test Guideline, were never developed. The method has sat exactly where that 2006 statement left it ever since.

The ceiling is architectural, not political — and that is the lesson

The default story in this vault, and in the field generally, is good science waiting on slow regulators: a sound method sits validated for years because adoption is bureaucratic and unhurried. The CYP-induction metabolism guideline waited twelve years. That story invites optimism — the science is done, acceptance will eventually catch up.

This method breaks that story, because here the ceiling is built into the method's own design, and no amount of further validation or regulatory patience can lift it. Toxicology's guiding ethic is the 3Rs — Replacement, Reduction, Refinement — and those first two words are not synonyms. A replacement method removes an animal from the process. A reduction method uses fewer animals but still needs some. The CFU-GM model's arithmetic consumes a live mouse experiment as an input, so it can never be a replacement — the best it can ever be is a reduction, cutting the second animal species while keeping the first. And OECD Test Guidelines for human health are essentially all replacement methods; there is no natural shelf for "runs a dish, but only after you dose a mouse."

The precise location of that line is worth pinning down, because it is not where you would guess. Compare OECD Test Guideline 430, an adopted skin-corrosion test that works by measuring the electrical resistance of a patch of excised rat skin — real animal tissue, in an accepted guideline. So "uses something from an animal" is plainly not disqualifying. The difference is that TG 430 consumes a piece of rat tissue, while the CFU-GM model consumes a whole rat-or-mouse study — a live animal dosed to find its MTD before the dish can produce a number at all. A method that uses animal-derived material can be a replacement; a method that cannot run without a prior in-vivo experiment cannot. That is the real replacement line, and the better-validated method is the one that falls on the wrong side of it.

Loop readiness: the most instructive "un-automatable" case here

Now the question this vault adds to the usual two: could a machine run this end-to-end, with no human in the loop? On every surface test, this one looks ready. The readout is a colony count — a clean number, not a human squinting down a microscope and making a judgment call (which is exactly what strands the micronucleus test, an adopted and validated assay, at un-automatable). The decision step is fixed arithmetic: multiply the mouse dose by a measured sensitivity ratio. There is no expert-judgment gate, no ambiguous curve to correct by hand, no missing decision rule. By the criteria that promote or demote every other method on this axis, the CFU-GM assay reads as loop-ready.

It is nonetheless graded bench-bound — the vault's term for "cannot realistically be automated at all" — and the reason is the single input to that tidy arithmetic: the mouse MTD is itself a live-animal experiment. You cannot close a loop around a calculation whose first operand is a mouse. This is why the vault keeps a separate field, bench_bound_reason, whose value here is animal-input: the blocker is not that the science is soft or the readout messy — both are excellent — but that the method's own math depends on a number no automated in-vitro system can generate. It is architectural, not political, in exactly the same way the guideline ceiling is. No better microscope, no better model, and no more validation fixes it. Only a genuinely different prediction model — one that does not eat a mouse dose — would, and that would be a different method.

There is a small, sharp irony worth noting. Most in-vitro methods produce a hazard label and then need a separate modelling layer to convert an in-dish concentration into a real human dose. The CFU-GM assay already outputs a dose — a predicted human MTD — so it needs that bridge less than almost anything else here. It built its own interspecies bridge. It built it out of a mouse.

Where it sits next to its neighbour

One more thing makes this method more than a curiosity, and it concerns the assay it is supposed to work alongside. Immunotoxicity — chemical harm to the immune system — has more than one arm, and this vault now holds two of them. OECD Test Guideline 444A (the IL-2 Luc assay) reads whether a chemical disrupts T-cell signalling; it is adopted, and its own guideline explicitly describes it as one component of a larger battery that is meant to include a myelotoxicity test — which is what CFU-GM is. So the two are written to be used together, and they fail in exactly opposite directions. TG 444A is regulatorily adopted but carries no published sensitivity or specificity figure at all — nobody has stated in numbers how often it is right. CFU-GM has a real, independently validated predictive record against human clinical data and no regulatory standing whatsoever. One has the stamp and no proof; the other has the proof and no stamp. Neither is a stand-alone immunotoxicity test, composing them would cover two different immune compartments — and no defined approach exists that would let anyone do so. The vault flags candidate batteries like this; it does not adopt them.

So: can you rely on it, and could you automate it?

For the practical question — will this drug damage the marrow, and at roughly what dose? — the CFU-GM assay is a genuinely good, independently validated screen, and it is used as one in pharmaceutical practice. Read it as what it is: a well-proven reduction method that lets you drop the second animal species, not a full replacement, and not something with an OECD Test Guideline behind it if you need one for a regulatory submission.

For the question this vault cares about — could a machine run it? — no, and the "no" is the whole point. This is the case that shows readout quality and decision-rule clarity, the two things that usually determine automatability, are not sufficient. A method can have a clean number and a fixed rule and still be un-loopable, because automatability is a property of the entire dependency chain, and this chain begins with a live animal. An autonomous lab does not fail to run this because the science is weak or the readout is subjective. It fails because the very first input is a mouse — and no robot in the loop can produce one.

This is a learning ledger, not regulatory advice, and it certifies nothing for any real testing programme. But the thing to carry away from CFU-GM is this: before asking whether a method is validated, or adopted, or automatable, trace what it consumes. The best-validated non-animal test in this vault is held back by a single animal experiment buried inside its own arithmetic — and that dependency, not the quality of the science, is what decides whether it can ever be a replacement or ever run itself.
