The endpoint where the animal test won and the alternative never showed up

Almost every story in non-animal toxicology has the same shape: a test that used animals, and a challenger trying to replace it. Thyroid disruption does not have that shape. Here the animal tests are adopted, official and in routine use — and after roughly fifteen years of effort, not one non-animal method for the endpoint has been written into a Test Guideline anywhere in the world. Sixty-seven of them exist. Zero are adopted.

That is unusual enough to be worth understanding on its own. It is also the single clearest natural experiment this vault contains in what it actually takes to get a method accepted — because thyroid has had the science for years and has been missing everything else.

On 18 July 2026 the picture moved for the first time. Not much, and not in the direction most summaries will report it. This article is about what moved, what did not, and why the difference matters.

What "thyroid disruption" means, and why anyone screens for it

Your thyroid is a small gland in your neck that makes two hormones. Those hormones set your body's metabolic rate — how fast you burn energy — and, far more consequentially, they govern how a brain develops before and shortly after birth. Thyroid hormone shortage during development is one of the well-established causes of permanent cognitive impairment.

So "is this chemical a thyroid disruptor?" is not an abstract regulatory box. It is a question about whether an industrial chemical, a pesticide or a drug can interfere with the machinery that builds a child's brain. It is also a question with an unusually large number of possible answers, because there are many ways to break the system without ever touching the gland:

  • Block the enzyme that builds the hormone. The enzyme is thyroperoxidase; inhibit it and the body makes less hormone. This is the most-screened mechanism by a wide margin.
  • Knock the hormone off its carrier protein. Thyroid hormone rides around the bloodstream on transport proteins, one of which is transthyretin. Displace it and you disrupt signalling without going near the thyroid at all.
  • Accelerate the hormone's destruction. Induce the liver enzymes that clear thyroid hormone and levels fall — again, nothing wrong with the gland.
  • Interfere further downstream — with the deiodinase enzymes that convert one hormone form to the active other, with the transporters that carry hormone into cells, or with the receptor that finally reads the signal.

This matters for method design in a way that turns out to be the whole problem. Each of those mechanisms is easy to build a test for, and each test sees only its own slice. A chemical that disrupts thyroid signalling by a route your panel does not cover reads as clean.

The blank, as it stood

The vault's picture of this endpoint came from a 2024 systematic inventory (Vergauwen et al., PMID 39931575), which catalogued the field and reached a conclusion it did not soften:

"validated Level 2 assays for thyroid hormone system disruption are still completely lacking today."

"Level 2" is the rung in the OECD's testing framework where the in-vitro mechanism screens live — the rung occupied, for the other hormone systems, by adopted Test Guidelines. Set the four hormone modalities side by side and the asymmetry is stark:

Sixty-seven in-vitro methods, none adopted. Eight in formal European validation. One had already failed — a thyroperoxidase assay using a luminol readout, rejected as not specific to the enzyme it claimed to measure. And the one method the inventory graded "validated" was not a thyroid assay at all: it was a liver enzyme-induction test, catalogued under thyroid because inducing those enzymes speeds up hormone clearance. The thyroid column's only validated entry was borrowed from another column.

The inversion. Thyroid does have adopted OECD guidelines. They are animal tests — an amphibian metamorphosis assay, a fish embryo thyroid assay, an extended amphibian growth study, plus thyroid measurements written into the standard rodent protocols. Everywhere else in this vault the non-animal method exists and is fighting for acceptance. Here the regulatory answer to "is this a thyroid disruptor?" is still: dose an animal — not because anyone decided that was better, but because the alternative never finished validating.

What actually landed on 18 July 2026

An interlaboratory validation, published in Toxicological Sciences (Deisenroth et al., DOI 10.1093/toxsci/kfaf166), of something called the human thyroid microtissue assay.

What the assay is. Take primary human thyroid cells and grow them as small three-dimensional tissues rather than a flat layer in a dish. Expose them to a chemical. Measure how much thyroid hormone they make.

Why that is scientifically interesting, and it is genuinely interesting: nearly every other method in that inventory of sixty-seven measures one mechanism — an enzyme inhibited, a carrier protein displaced, a receptor fired. This one measures the output the whole system exists to produce. A chemical that disrupts hormone synthesis by a route nobody thought to build an assay for still shows up, because the readout is the answer rather than one of the steps.

What the study did. Three laboratories. Standard operating procedures written for the purpose, then method transfer and training. Then a blinded test: four reference chemicals — three known positives, one known negative — run across three independent human thyroid donors. The assay transferred, the hormone-synthesis effects reproduced, and the metrics for dynamic range, precision and screening quality met the criteria set in advance.

The study was coordinated by the US Environmental Protection Agency with NICEATM, the US body that runs method validation, with an independent international team monitoring.

Now the part that gets rounded up, and should not be

This study asked whether the assay gives the same answer in different hands. It did not ask whether the answer is right. Those are two different questions, and only the first has been answered.

Concretely, here is what does not exist as of tonight:

  • No sensitivity or specificity figure. Nobody has published how often this assay catches a real thyroid disruptor, or how often it flags a safe chemical. Not a weak number — no number.
  • Four chemicals. That is enough to check that a method travels between labs. It is nowhere near enough to describe which chemicals it can be trusted on.
  • No decision rule. Nothing published converts a hormone-synthesis measurement into a regulatory call. A robot could run this assay tomorrow and produce data that no agreed procedure turns into "thyroid disruptor: yes or no."
  • No Test Guideline. The inversion above is untouched. The regulatory answer is still: dose an animal.

And the funding has to be stated plainly. The industry collaborators were LifeNet Health LifeSciences, Bayer Crop Science and Corteva Agrisciences. Two of those three are agrochemical companies whose own products get screened for thyroid disruption — a direct interest in the performance of the test that will judge their compounds. That is recorded, not laundered. The counterweight is real and belongs in the same sentence: this was a regulator-coordinated exercise with independent monitoring, not a developer quietly validating its own product. Both things are true, and the honest read is "interested parties inside a supervised process," which is how most modern validation actually looks.

So why does this count as movement at all?

Because of what it does to the structure of the problem, which is more interesting than its size.

Before tonight, thyroid was blocked twice over. There was no validated method, and there was no agreed procedure for interpreting one. Those two gaps locked each other: you cannot write an interpretation rule for a method that has not been shown to work, and a method with no rule attached has nothing to be validated toward.

The vault's grading moves accordingly — scientific validity from "published method" to "formally validated", regulatory standing from "nothing" to "in a validation pipeline" — and the map's overall status moves from emerging to consolidating, which means exactly one thing: independent laboratories reproduced it. Not that it is correct. Not that it is accepted.

What that buys is a single blocker instead of two. Thyroid has stopped being this vault's most extreme case of its dominant problem and has joined the ordinary version of it — the same position as the developmental neurotoxicity battery and as Tox21 itself: the wet work is roughly solved, and the interpretation layer was never built. That is not a triumph. It is a demotion from exceptional to typical, and given where thyroid started, it is real.

The thing nobody is tracking: there are now two tracks

The eight methods in European validation are one effort. The thyroid microtissue assay is not one of them — it is a US effort, on a different method, under a different validation body.

So this endpoint is now being worked on from both sides of the Atlantic, on method sets that do not overlap, with no shared procedure for interpreting either. More shots on goal, and also two bodies of evidence that will not straightforwardly add up. The question worth asking about thyroid is no longer just "will a non-animal method validate?" It is "will the two tracks produce one rule, or two?"

Anyone who has watched two teams independently build the same subsystem, each convinced theirs is the standard, will recognise the failure mode.

One more thing, and it is the useful one

There is a design lesson buried here that generalises well beyond thyroid.

This vault keeps a running warning it calls the correlated blind spot: when two tests in the same panel fail on the same chemicals for the same underlying reason, that panel casts one vote, not two. Thyroid already had a clean instance — two of the eight European candidates measure the same event (hormone knocked off its transport protein) by the same trick (a fluorescent probe competing for the same site), differing only in the probe. Two methods, one vote. Neither has metabolism, so a chemical whose breakdown product is the actual disruptor reads as negative on both.

The remedy for that is not a bigger panel. It is a measurement that fails differently.

Which is, more or less by accident, what the thyroid microtissue assay is. Intact human tissue producing actual hormone fails on genuinely different things than a cell-free probe-displacement assay does. It is the orthogonal vote this endpoint needed — and nobody involved appears to have chosen it for that reason. The two efforts are in different jurisdictions and are not being designed as a panel at all.

That is the sharpest thing thyroid has to teach. The methods that make a battery trustworthy are the ones that break in unrelated ways, and right now nothing in the process is set up to notice whether that property has been achieved — or lost. Thyroid's battery does not exist yet; the eight European candidates are still in validation and how they will be combined is still being decided. It is the one endpoint in this vault where that question could still be asked before the concrete sets.

This is a learning ledger, not regulatory advice. It organises public literature and public guidance; it does not certify any method for a real testing programme, and "a machine could run it" is never "a machine should."
