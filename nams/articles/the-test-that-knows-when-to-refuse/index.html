The test that knows when to refuse

A safety test that can measure, on the spot, that it is not qualified to answer — and says so — is a different kind of object from one that always returns a verdict. The eye column of this vault just acquired its first. The same test also made its battery bigger and less independent at the same moment, which is the more important lesson and the less comfortable one.

The method is the Vitrigel-Eye Irritancy Test, adopted as OECD Test Guideline 494 — one of a numbered series of internationally agreed lab protocols; this one covers a non-animal way of asking whether a chemical will damage an eye. The current edition is dated 2 July 2026. It is the third such method this vault has mapped, and if you only read the top-line grades it looks like more of the same: scientifically adopted, regulatorily adopted, machine-runnable. Three for three.

The interesting parts are underneath.

What it actually does

Human corneal cells — the cells of the clear front surface of the eye — are grown on a thin collagen membrane until they form a tissue with a working barrier. That barrier is the eye's real defence: a continuous sheet that keeps things out.

The assay applies the chemical for three minutes and, throughout, measures how well the tissue still blocks a small electric current. Intact barrier, high resistance. Damaged barrier, the current gets through and the resistance drops. The measurement is not a single number but a curve — how long before the resistance starts falling, how steeply it falls, where it settles.

There is a reason for measuring the barrier rather than the cells, and the guideline states it plainly: irritating chemicals "first destroy tear film and epithelial barrier function of the eye, subsequently induce epithelial cell death." Barrier failure comes first. Cell death comes after. Hold onto that sentence — it becomes the whole second half of this article.

The decision rule is arithmetic, and that is why a machine can run it

Three quantities are computed from the curve: how long before it starts falling (time lag), how fast it falls (intensity), and where it ends up (plateau level). Then:

Clear the chemical only if time lag > 180 seconds and intensity < 0.05% per second and plateau level ≤ 5.0%.

That is the entire interpretation step. Three numbers, three thresholds, one and. No expert reads anything; no judgment enters.

This is what earns the method the vault's top automation grade, closed-loop-ready — meaning a machine could take the measurement and make the call, with no human in the decision. Its loop_blocker is none: nothing stands in the way of automating it.

That is a statement about automatability, not about quality, and certainly not an endorsement. The vault now holds twenty-one methods at that grade. Another one is not news. What follows is.

The asymmetry: this test can clear a chemical, but it cannot condemn one

TG 494 identifies chemicals that do not need a hazard warning. A clean result clears the chemical outright, with no further testing needed.

Anything else is not a positive finding. The guideline's own label for it is "No Stand-alone Prediction Can Be Made." It says, in the guideline's words, that the method "is not intended to identify nor differentiate between" the serious-damage and the reversible-irritation hazard categories. It routes onward.

So this is a one-way filter, and it is graded accordingly: you judge it on how much you can trust a clean result, not on how precise its alarms are.

On that measure the numbers are good — with a large asterisk.

The catch rate is 96%, and it is a conditional number

Against the traditional rabbit test, TG 494's reported performance is:

  • catches 96% of chemicals that really are eye hazards (51 of 53) — it rarely clears something dangerous;
  • wrongly flags about a third of harmless chemicals as needing further testing (specificity 67%);
  • overall right 81% of the time.

Two chemicals slipped through as safe when they were not, and both were in the mildest irritant class. Reproducibility is solid: the same lab got the same answer 80–100% of the time, and different labs agreed 92% of the time.

But the first number in that list only exists because of a filter. Before the filter, on the full set, the same method catches 83% of real hazards — and the guideline's own verdict on that configuration is bluntly unflattering: it "would not be the method of choice because of its limited predictivity."

The gap between 83% and 96% is not an improvement to the assay. It is 51 of 158 chemicals — 32% — being removed from the question.

The thing that is actually new

Here is why that filter is worth an article rather than a footnote.

Every non-animal method has an applicability domain: the set of chemicals it is actually valid for. Outside it, the answer means nothing. Every method in this vault carries one. And in every previous case, the domain is a fact somebody has to know — a lab-grown skin corrosion test that is unreliable across a stated pH range; a fish-cell toxicity test bounded by how fat-soluble the chemical is; a protein-based eye test that cannot see hazards created by the body's own metabolism. These are caveats printed in a document. A machine running the assay respects them only if a human wired that knowledge in.

TG 494's domain is a measurement. Before the real test runs, a pre-test checks three things, each with a number attached:

  1. Is the preparation acidic (pH 5 or below)? Out of domain.
  2. Does it separate out of solution within the test window? Out of domain.
  3. Does its light absorbance shift by more than 0.1 between the start and the three-minute mark? Then, in the guideline's words, "the chemical should not be tested."

A pH probe, a plate reader, a timer. A robot can determine, from the sample in front of it, that this assay has no business answering for this chemical — no lookup, no prior knowledge, no human.

The vault's own grading rules warn that the top automation grade "does not mean 'trust the decision rule on any chemical' — it means trust it inside the domain." That has always been an obligation on whoever builds the system, discharged by knowing things. TG 494 moves the check inside the protocol, where a loop cannot skip it by not knowing. For anyone building automated safety testing, that is a better structural property than a higher score.

Two honest limits on the praise. A pre-test catches the failures somebody anticipated — TG 494 remains completely blind to hazards created by the body's metabolism, and no pre-test detects that. And nobody reports the refusal rate. A system announcing "96% catch rate" while silently declining a third of its inputs is describing itself inaccurately, and no OECD guideline template asks for that number.

Now the uncomfortable half: three tests, about two opinions

The point of a battery is that several tests, failing in different ways, catch what any one of them misses. The eye battery — assembled under OECD Test Guideline 467, which defines how to combine eye tests into a regulatory answer — now has three mapped components. It does not have three independent opinions.

  • TG 492 grows human corneal tissue and measures whether the cells are still alive.
  • TG 494, the new one, grows human corneal tissue and measures whether the barrier still holds.
  • TG 496 puts the chemical in a tube with a protein mixture that mimics the cornea's structure and measures how much of that protein it wrecks — no cells, no tissue at all.

TG 496 is the odd one out, and being the odd one out is its value. It can fail in ways the tissue tests cannot, and vice versa. When it agrees with a tissue-based test, that agreement carries real information.

TG 492 and TG 494 are a different story. Same tissue type. Neither has any metabolic capability. And recall the sentence from earlier: barrier damage happens first, and "subsequently" causes the cell death that TG 492 measures.

The guideline is telling you that one component measures the precursor of what the other component measures. They are not two ways of being wrong about an eye. They are one process, sampled early and late.

This vault flags exactly this pattern — a correlated_blind_spot — and it is now set on both methods. What makes this instance unusual is that the vault did not have to infer it. Every previous case here was detective work: two fish tests that both lack a nervous system; three hormone tests quietly sharing a piece of genetic machinery, none of them citing the others. This one is stated in the source text.

So a chemical that damages the cornea by some route neither tissue model supports — most obviously one that is harmless until the body chemically transforms it, which neither test can do — reads clean on both. And it reads clean in agreement.

Why this is worse for a machine than for a person

A human toxicologist looking at two concordant clean results may well pause and ask whether those tests were ever going to disagree. It is the kind of question experience produces.

An automated system will not ask it. It reads two passes, scores them as independent confirmation, becomes more confident, and proceeds — quickly, and on to the next chemical. The vault's own framing: automation converts a known bias into a compounding one. You do not get one wrong answer; you get a fast, confident, self-reinforcing series of them.

Which produces the number worth carrying out of this article: the eye battery has three components and about two independent mechanisms. Those are different numbers, and only one of them appears anywhere in the paperwork. A system counting components will overstate its own evidence by roughly fifty percent.

The remedy is not a fourth eye test. The remedy is a measurement that fails differently — which, in this battery, means the cell-free one, and would mean adding something with metabolic capability, since all three current components lack it.

Where this leaves the method

Say it plainly, on all three axes:

  • Scientifically: adopted, and validated properly — a three-laboratory study run by JaCVAM, Japan's national body for validating non-animal methods, coordinated internationally and independently peer-reviewed. Not a developer marking its own homework. (Evidence tier: regulatory-adopted.)
  • Regulatorily: an adopted OECD Test Guideline. (Regulatory status: OECD-TG-adopted.) But narrow — it can clear a chemical, and that is all it can do.
  • Automatability: closed-loop-ready, blocker none. A machine can run it end to end, including the decision to decline.

Two things to hold against it. The test system is proprietary and the guideline names a specific commercial instrument for the measurement — recorded as funding_flag: method-developer, a supply dependency rather than sponsored science, and one that decides whether anyone else can stand this loop up. And that 96% catch rate is real only for the 68% of chemicals the method agrees to look at.

The thing worth remembering

Two lessons landed here at once, and they point in opposite directions.

The good one: a test that can measure its own incompetence is better infrastructure for automation than a test with better numbers. Machines fail worst when they answer questions they should have declined. TG 494 is the first method in this vault that hands the machine a way to decline — and the same night, from an entirely different literature, a machine-learning model aimed at reproductive toxicity turned out to carry its own version of the same idea, refusing to predict for chemicals too unlike anything it was trained on. A wet assay and a model, independently, building the ability to say not this one.

The uncomfortable one: adding a test made this battery bigger and its evidence weaker per component. Not by anyone's error — TG 494 is a good method, honestly validated and honestly described. The independence just was not tracked, because no axis in this vault, and no field in an OECD guideline, records it.

That gap is where an automated system will go wrong first. Not because the tests are bad. Because it will count them.

This is a learning ledger, not regulatory advice. It organises the public evidence; it does not certify a method for a real testing programme, and it does not certify one as safe to automate.
