Validated, adopted, and impossible to run

If you need to know whether a chemical activates the human estrogen receptor, you can do that today, without animals, using an internationally adopted test method — but not the one you might reach for first, because nobody can currently run it. That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the literal content of a formal correction the OECD issued to an adopted Test Guideline on 2 July 2026.

The short answer to "can I rely on this?" is yes — the estrogen receptor transactivation assay is a mature, adopted, well-behaved mechanism screen, and the endpoint is fully testable. But the way it is currently broken is more interesting than the fact that it works, because it exposes something this field's grading systems — including the one this vault uses — genuinely cannot see.

What the method is, and what it honestly tells you

Estrogens work by binding the estrogen receptor, mostly ERα. The bound receptor latches onto stretches of DNA called estrogen response elements and switches on transcription. The assay reproduces that in a dish: take a cell line, stably insert a luciferase reporter gene downstream of those response elements, add your chemical, and measure the light. More light means the chemical activated the receptor. Run it in the presence of a fixed dose of estradiol instead, and a drop in light means the chemical blocked it.

OECD TG 455 is the adopted guideline covering this, and on both of the axes worth tracking it scores at the top: the scientific evidence tier is regulatory-adopted, and the regulatory status is OECD-TG-adopted. It was adopted on 14 June 2021 and corrected on 2 July 2026. It is a performance-based guideline, meaning it defines the endpoint and a set of performance standards rather than mandating a single protocol; three test methods currently qualify, built on three different cell lines.

Here is what it does not tell you, in the guideline's own words: results "should not be directly extrapolated to the complex signaling and regulation of the intact endocrine system in vivo." It sits at Level 2 of the OECD's framework for endocrine disrupters — an in-vitro mechanism screen, for screening, prioritisation, and feeding a weight-of-evidence argument. It says a chemical engages the estrogen receptor. It does not say the chemical harms anyone.

And it says nothing whatsoever about fertility. This matters because the mistake is common and the literature is explicit about it: a 2026 review (PMID 42021917) singles out TG 455 and its siblings as exactly the assays that measure "receptor activation and hormone synthesis, not fertility." A completed panel of endocrine mechanism screens is not a reproductive toxicity test, and no amount of adding more of them makes it one.

The part that should stop you

The entire content of the 2 July 2026 correction to TG 455 is a single note, printed twice — once in the general introduction and again at the head of the relevant annex:

"Users of Test Guideline 455 should be aware that the VM7Luc ER TA assay is currently not implementable because the cell line used is not available from the source mentioned. Actions are underway to remediate this situation."

The VM7Luc ER TA assay is one of the two reference methods TG 455 was founded on. It was validated by ICCVAM and NICEATM — independent US public bodies, not the method's developers. It is named in an adopted international Test Guideline. And the cell line it requires is no longer obtainable from the licensors who supplied it, so the number of laboratories on Earth that can currently perform it is zero.

Now look at what the grading says. The scientific validity axis: unchanged, top tier. The regulatory acceptance axis: unchanged, top tier. Both of the axes anyone uses to judge whether a non-animal method is trustworthy report that this method is in perfect health. Neither of them can represent the one fact that actually matters to somebody trying to use it.

This is worth sitting with, because the reflex is to file it as a supply-chain footnote. It is not. It is a demonstration that a test method is not a protocol — it is a protocol plus a physical dependency. A cell line, a reconstructed tissue, a proprietary kit, a licensed piece of software, a radioisotope. The scientific axis grades the protocol. The regulatory axis grades the protocol. The physical dependency is where methods actually die, and nothing in the standard grading watches it.

Once you start looking, the pattern is everywhere in the adopted canon. The only validated reference kit for one skin-corrosion guideline is a commercial product. The cell line behind the newly adopted metabolism guideline is patented and licensed to a single company. One leg of an adopted skin-sensitisation defined approach runs on licensed software. The estrogen-binding guideline, TG 493, needs a radioisotope — which means it needs a radiochemistry permit, which excludes most laboratories that could otherwise run it perfectly well. In all of those cases, access is bounded. In TG 455's case it is zero. Same axis, different magnitude, and the second one is impossible to wave away.

One honest qualification, and it cuts the other way. TG 455 itself is fine. Because it is a performance-based guideline rather than a single fixed protocol, the loss of one method does not take the endpoint down with it: the other two methods still run, and estrogen receptor transactivation is fully testable today. That containment is a real argument for writing guidelines as performance standards instead of recipes, and it deserves to be stated as one rather than buried under the bad news.

The cells were never what anyone thought they were

There is a second fact about the same cell line, and putting the two together is where this stops being a story about one assay.

Until 2016 the line was called BG1Luc, and everyone believed it derived from BG1 human ovarian carcinoma cells. Then somebody ran DNA authentication. The guideline records the result in a footnote: the line used to develop the assay "was not the BG1 human ovarian carcinoma cell line, but was instead a variant of the MCF7 human breast cancer cell line." Hence the rename — VM7, where "V" is for variant and "M7" for MCF7.

The OECD's ruling on what that does to the assay's standing is one sentence, and it is remarkable:

"While this changes the origin of the cell line upon which the assay is based, it does not affect published validation studies nor the utility and application of this assay."

They are right, and the reason they are right is the interesting part. Validation in this field is an empirical performance record, not a derivation from biology. Nobody ever argued "ovarian carcinoma cells are the correct model for estrogen signalling, therefore this assay predicts estrogenic activity." They argued "we ran reference chemicals through it and measured what came out." No conclusion depended on the cells being ovarian, so no conclusion broke when they turned out to be breast. A validated assay is, in this sense, a black box with a measured input–output relationship. Its internals are documented but not load-bearing.

That is a defensible position. It is also true that a cell-line misidentification — one of the best-documented systemic failures in biomedical research — passed through assay development, formal validation by an independent public body, and adoption as an international standard, and was caught a decade later by outsiders doing genetic authentication. Both readings are correct. The field should hold them at once.

Why this is the moment the two-axis model breaks

Track a non-animal method along two axes — is the science sound and has a regulator accepted it — and you will get most questions right. That separation is the single most useful thing to know about this field: a method can be scientifically excellent and regulatorily nowhere (the CYP-induction guideline sat validated for twelve years before adoption), and the two clocks genuinely run independently.

But 2026 has produced three separate demonstrations that the acceptance axis, in particular, bundles together claims that come apart:

  • TG 455 is adopted — and one of its reference methods cannot be run at all. Adoption does not imply availability.
  • TG 493 is adopted — and states, in its own text, that "there is currently no universally agreed method for interpreting ER binding data." Two labs can run it flawlessly, agree on the number to the decimal, and disagree on whether the chemical is a binder, because the guideline supplies no threshold. Adoption does not imply an agreed decision rule.
  • The FDA's March 2026 draft guidance says a method needs neither. In CDER's words, "for a NAM to be considered for review in drug development, the test does not necessarily need to be validated… A fit-for-purpose NAM, even if not validated, may adequately address specific toxicological concerns." Non-adoption does not imply unusability.

Read together, those three say something specific: regulatory acceptance is not a property of a method. The grading treats it as one — a rung on a ladder, attached to the assay. On the FDA's route it attaches instead to a combination: this method, for this specific question, in this specific submission. The same assay can be sufficient for one drug application and inadequate for the next, because the question is different. That is not a lower rung on the ladder. It is a different ladder, and it is not bolted to the method at all.

Note the caution this deserves: the FDA document is a draft, it is explicitly nonbinding, comments closed in May 2026 with criticism from both animal-protection groups and industry, and no final version exists. Nobody can yet say whether the fit-for-purpose route will be used well, or at all. But the direction it points is the same direction TG 455 and TG 493 point from the opposite side — the acceptance axis is measuring whether a guideline exists, and it is being read as whether the method is usable. Those are not the same question, and 2026 is the year the gap between them became impossible to ignore.

So: can you rely on it?

For the actual question — does this chemical activate the human estrogen receptor? — yes. The endpoint is adopted, harmonised, and testable today with two working methods, and the assay is about as well-characterised as in-vitro toxicology gets. Use it as what it is: a Level 2 mechanism screen for prioritisation and weight-of-evidence, not an apical prediction, and emphatically not a fertility test.

For the broader question — what does "adopted" guarantee? — less than you think. It guarantees that a committee agreed on how to run the assay. It does not guarantee that anyone can obtain the reagents, that the guideline tells you what the answer means, or that the absence of adoption would have stopped a regulator from accepting the method anyway.

This is a learning ledger, not regulatory advice, and it certifies nothing for any real testing programme. But if there is one thing to carry away from TG 455, it is this: before trusting a validated method, check that you can still buy the cells.
