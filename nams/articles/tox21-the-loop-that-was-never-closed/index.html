The robots arrived in 2013

If you want to know why toxicology has not been automated, the answer is not that the robots are missing. The robots have been running, continuously, for thirteen years.

Since 2013, a robotic arm at the US National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences has been screening about ten thousand chemicals against a panel of cell-based tests — each chemical at fifteen different concentrations, each run three times on three separate days, in plates holding 1,536 tiny wells — and it gets through the entire library in a week. The paper describing it uses the phrase "hands-free assay completion." Compound storage, liquid handling, incubation, measurement: all of it, untouched.

That programme is Tox21, and this vault has been quietly leaning on it for a month. When it grades a method as automatable-open-loop — the robots can run this, but a human still has to say what the result means — the definition in shared/evidence-model.md says, in as many words, "this is Tox21's state." Fourteen method maps now carry that grade.

The vault had never read a Tox21 paper. It was asserting the anchor claim of its own third axis on reputation. Tonight it read the primary source (automation-tox21-qhts-platform), and the source is harder on the field than the assertion was.

Where the human still is

The platform fits every chemical's dose–response to a curve and reads two numbers off it: the concentration producing half the maximal effect, and how big that maximal effect is. Curves get sorted automatically into classes. And then:

"Class 5 curves and other cases … are manually inspected to correct the curve class, if necessary, normally by masking or unmasking data points."

Read that slowly, because it is the whole article. When the automated fit is ambiguous, a trained human opens the plot and decides which data points count. Masking a point changes the curve. The curve sets the number. The number is the answer.

The human is not supervising the decision layer from a distance. The human is inside it.

And it goes further than the curves. There is no published procedure — none — that converts a Tox21 result into a regulatory statement like "this chemical is a hazard." The platform manufactures measurements, at a scale nothing else in the field approaches. Turning a measurement into a decision remains a research activity, done by people, case by case.

Three axes, and this one splits them wide

This vault grades every method on three things that move independently, and Tox21 pulls them apart about as far as they go.

Does the science hold up? Yes — peer-reviewed-method. It is a real, published, decade-old production system, and its output feeds work across the US Environmental Protection Agency and the Food and Drug Administration.

Has a regulator adopted it? No — regulatory_status: none. There is no Test Guideline for the Tox21 platform, because it is not a regulatory method. It generates data; it does not make calls.

Could a machine run it end to end? automatable-open-loop — and the blocker is not the robotics. The robotics are magnificent and thirteen years old. The blocker is that nobody has written down the rule for turning the number into a call, and where the automation is uncertain, a person is still editing the inputs.

Automating the wet work did not automate the epistemology. That sentence is the finding, and it generalises: the gap between "a robot runs the assay" and "an AI closes the loop" is an interpretation gap, not an engineering gap. The OECD's own estrogen-receptor binding guideline says the quiet part out loud on a different assay — "there is currently no universally agreed method for interpreting ER binding data" (endocrine-er-binding-hrer). Same disease, smaller scale.

Which is also, quietly, the good news. Interpretation gaps are cheap to close compared with wet-lab science. Nobody needs a new organoid to write a decision rule. What they need is agreement — and agreement is a committee problem, not a biology problem.

The trap the automation was built to avoid

There is a second finding in this paper, and it is the most useful piece of engineering advice the vault has extracted from anywhere.

Almost every assay on the Tox21 panel is a living cell engineered to emit light when some biological event happens. Which means they all share one way of being wrong: kill the cell, and the light changes too — not because the chemical did anything interesting, but because the cell is dying.

That failure mode is not a quirk of one assay. It belongs to the entire technology class. So a chemical that is simply toxic lights up everywhere.

Now imagine an AI running this panel and doing the obvious thing: looking for agreement across assays. Ten tests light up on the same chemical, so confidence goes up, so it investigates more chemicals like that one.

But if the chemical is just poisoning the cells, all ten lit up for the same wrong reason. Agreement across assays that share a blind spot is not corroboration. It is the same error, ten times, wearing the costume of independent confirmation — and a system that treats confidence as a signal to explore further will accelerate into it.

This is the vault's correlated_blind_spot rail, and it has caught the same shape four times now — two fish assays that both lack a nervous system and both under-predict neurotoxicants (fish-embryo-acute-toxicity-fet and ecotox-rtgill-w1-fish-cell-line); two phototoxicity assays that neither metabolise, so a harmless-looking parent chemical with a dangerous breakdown product passes the whole test cascade untouched (phototoxicity-ros-assay and phototoxicity-3t3-nru); three hormone assays built on the same genetic backbone, whose selectivity had to be engineered in, one of them by deleting a receptor with CRISPR (endocrine-ar-transactivation).

Tox21 shows the fix, and the fix is not more assays. It measures cell viability in the same well, as a separate readout, to distinguish a real biological response from a dying cell. The two measurements fail differently — that is the entire point — and the mismatch rate against a standalone viability test is under half a percent.

The remedy for a correlated blind spot is a measurement that fails differently, not a bigger battery.

A third fish assay adds nothing to the two the vault already has. A metabolic-activation step adds everything. Anybody designing an automated testing battery should have that sentence on the wall.

And they knew why it mattered

The paper is explicit that Tox21 cannot tolerate the error rate a drug-discovery screen shrugs off. Its stated reason is that the output is not a list of hits — it is a training set for predictive models.

"the accuracy of activity assignment … is of paramount importance" — because the goal is to build predictive models — whereas in traditional drug discovery "a certain level of noise and the associated false positives and negatives is expected and largely tolerated."

A false positive in a screen wastes one follow-up experiment. A false positive in a training set is inherited by every prediction the model ever makes. The people who built the world's largest toxicology robot understood in 2013 that automation converts a tolerable error rate into a compounding one. That is this vault's central warning about closed loops, stated a decade early, from the inside.

What it honestly does not tell you

Be sceptical of the automation claims, and the vault is. This paper is the builders describing their own robot — the authors work at the institutions that operate it — and it is flagged funding_flag: [method-developer] accordingly. Phrases like "fully integrated" and "hands-free" are marketing until somebody independent checks them.

The claim worth keeping is the one that cuts against them. Nobody advertising a hands-free robot volunteers that humans are manually correcting its output. They said it anyway, which is why it is believable — and it is the single most important sentence in the source.

Two other honest limits. The platform's chemical library is curated, and compounds that will not dissolve properly are outside the range it can fairly assay — a negative result on an insoluble chemical means very little. And this paper describes the machine, not the ~50 million data points it has since produced. The vault holds the robot and not yet its output, which is the obvious next thing to read.

Refreshed 2026-07-28: three things landed after this article was written, and one of them is a correction to it

This article was written on 2026-07-13. Four dated findings have since landed on the map underneath it. Two sharpen the argument. One qualifies a claim the article makes, and it is worth the space.

The human is not only in the decision layer — part of the work is upstream of it

The article says "the human is not supervising the decision layer — the human is inside it," pointing at the sentence where ambiguous dose–response curves are "manually inspected … by masking or unmasking data points."

A 2025 paper by 32 authors — including the OECD Secretariat, the European Food Safety Authority, the US Food and Drug Administration and NICEATM (the US body that supports the interagency validation committee) — sets out the six software levels that sit between an instrument's raw output and a hazard call. In that taxonomy, masking and unmasking data points is a level II/III operation: raw-data flagging and exclusion. It is not a missing decision rule at all. It is data cleaning, and it would still be needed if a decision rule existed.

That is a sharper statement of the problem than the article originally made. The human at Tox21 is doing two different jobs, and only one of them is the job a decision rule would retire.

The data is now documented as a way for the model half of the loop to learn the wrong thing

A 2026 commentary in Archives of Toxicology — an argument, with no data attached, which is how the vault grades it — makes a point about what happens downstream. A high-throughput "active" can mean three quite different things: the chemical really did the biological thing being asked about, or the chemical killed the cells and a dead cell stops doing nearly everything, or the chemical interfered with the instrument by glowing at the detector's wavelength or blocking the enzyme the assay uses to report.

Train a model on the hit calls without those flags, the commentary argues, and "they can learn how the assay behaved rather than how the chemical harms."

Then comes the load-bearing clause: "because the confounders are structurally systematic, they are exactly the kind of signal a structure-based model will capture." Read that twice. It means a high held-out accuracy is what this failure mode looks like, not evidence against it. The model agrees beautifully with the data it was trained on, because it learned the data's artefacts.

This is the article's own blind-spot argument with the second assay removed. And note where the fault is not: Tox21 built the counter-screens. It measures cell viability in the same well precisely so that a dying cell can be distinguished from a real response. The control exists. The modellers drop it.

⚠️ And the correction: "hands-free" was doing more work in this article than the evidence supports

The article's closing argument is that the missing piece was never the machine. That still holds — the missing piece is still the rule.

But a July 2026 preprint from Argonne and the University of Chicago has, for the first time, measured how well an automated laboratory run can be checked, and the numbers are a caution against how casually the word hands-free gets read.

The system bolts two guards onto a cheap open-source liquid-handling robot. The first reads the protocol code before the run and asks whether it violates a rule specific to the assay. Its motivating example is not a programming error: reusing the same pipette tip between a DNA sample and its no-template control — the deliberately empty tube whose only purpose is to prove the reagents are not contaminated. The code is valid, the robot executes it happily, and the run produces a plausible number rather than an error.

Checking the plan scores an F1 of 0.97 — a combined measure of "catches the real problems" and "does not cry wolf," where 1.0 is perfect. Watching the robot physically execute it, with a camera, scores 0.71: roughly seven physical failures in ten caught, with real false alarms. And on the smallest-volume pipette, the score falls to 0.47, which is close to a coin flip. The smaller the volume, the less there is to see.

That ordering runs the wrong way for high-throughput screening. The entire economic argument for 1,536-well plates is that small volumes are cheap. The assurance is worst exactly where the assays are densest.

None of this is a criticism of Tox21's engineering, and the vault does not demote anything on it. Tox21's in-well viability control is exactly the kind of internal check this article holds up as best practice — though it is a biological control, catching a chemical that poisons cells, not an execution control catching a pipette that failed to dispense. Nobody publishes a per-well execution error rate for this platform, and nobody has asked for one, including this vault.

So the honest revision: automatable-open-loop says a robot can run the assay. It says nothing about whether anyone can tell when the robot didn't. The article's claim that the wet work is solved should be read as solved for throughput, not solved for verified correctness.

One more thing, from a field that has never heard of Tox21

Twenty-eight authors from US national laboratories, universities and industry published a two-year roadmap for autonomous science in July 2026 — AI proposes, robots execute, results feed back. It contains no toxicology, cites nothing in this field, and states:

"Producing a candidate discovery is no longer the hard part, but verifying it is, and this asymmetry now limits autonomous science more than raw model capability."

That is this article's thesis, derived independently, from chemistry and materials. The counter-currents the roadmap reports about its own field are all verification failures: a corrected flagship result, agents that match experts on questions with known answers while completing only a fraction of open-ended research, and fabricated citations turning up in refereed proceedings.

And toxicology's version is worse than theirs. In materials, verifying a proposal means trying to make the thing; the answer arrives and it is unambiguous. Even there, the record is poor — a generative model proposed 380,000 stable crystal structures and fewer than 5% have been independently confirmed experimentally. In toxicology the ground truth is human harm. It arrives late, it arrives confounded, and for most chemicals it never arrives at all.

So what would it actually take?

Tox21 is the best evidence available for what an autonomous toxicology lab is missing, and the answer is short.

Not the arm. The arm has been running since 2013, and it will out-throughput any lab you can build.

The rule. Somebody has to publish a defensible, fixed procedure that turns a dose–response curve into a hazard call — and defend it well enough that a regulator will accept it. That is the gap on fourteen of this vault's maps, and it is the same gap at Tox21's scale.

And an honest reckoning with the price. FDA has written down what it costs to let a machine decide alone: its draft framework rates an AI model's risk partly by how much of the decision rests on the model rather than on anything else — so a closed loop, by its very nature, maximises the thing the regulator charges for (regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility). Automation is not free. It has an invoice, and the regulator has itemised it.

Thirteen years of the most automated toxicology on earth, and the loop is still open — because the missing piece was never the machine.

And, as of the 2026-07-28 refresh, one line has to be added to that: the machine is not the missing piece, but the machine is also not as checked as thirteen years of the word hands-free has made it sound.
