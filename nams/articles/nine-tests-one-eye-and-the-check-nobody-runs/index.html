Nine tests, one eye, and the check nobody runs

Is this method ready, what for, and could a machine run it? For the eye, the answer is unusually specific and unusually awkward. There are nine officially adopted laboratory tests for "will this chemical hurt an eye," which is more than any other endpoint in this vault has. Every one of them produces a clean number a plate reader can read. And the document that tells you how to combine them into an answer is the one part of the process a machine cannot run — because combining them is defined as a judgement call, not a calculation.

That document also contains a single sentence that this vault got wrong for two weeks, and the correction is the most useful thing in the article.

What the eye problem actually is

The regulatory question is a three-way sort. Under the UN's global chemical-labelling system, a chemical is either Category 1 (serious eye damage — the harm does not fully heal), Category 2 (eye irritation — the harm heals), or No Category (no eye warning needed). Those categories are the labels on the bottle under your sink.

The old way to decide was the Draize test: put the chemical in a rabbit's eye and watch for up to 21 days. It is exactly as unpleasant as it sounds, and it is also noisy — the current lab-grown-tissue guideline records that the rabbit test disagrees with itself about 12% of the time on repeat runs. Keep that number; it matters later.

The non-animal replacements come in an awkward shape. Most of them can only answer half the question:

  • Some can only say "no warning needed" — a clean result means you're done, a dirty result means "keep testing," not "this is dangerous."
  • Some can only say "serious damage" — a positive classifies, a negative tells you nothing.
  • Almost none could resolve the middle class, plain irritation, which is where most consumer chemistry actually lives.

So the eye endpoint has always needed an assembly manual. That manual is OECD Guidance Document 263 — Integrated Approaches to Testing and Assessment for Serious Eye Damage and Eye Irritation, now in its third edition (2024). "Integrated Approach to Testing and Assessment," or IATA, is the field's term for a structured strategy that combines many kinds of evidence rather than relying on one test.

The nine tests, and two of them still use animals

GD 263's Module 3 lists the adopted laboratory methods. Written out plainly:

Read the first two rows again. OECD Test Guideline 437 runs on cow eyes and Test Guideline 438 on chicken eyes, both taken from slaughtered animals. They cut down on animal testing; they do not take animals out of the process. This vault keeps a hard line between replacement (the animal is gone) and reduction (fewer animals, still animals) — because the line is architectural, not rhetorical. Two of the nine adopted "alternatives" for the eye are on the reduction side of it, and the guidance does not remark on this at all.

The newest test buys the middle category with a stopwatch

OECD Test Guideline 492B (adopted 30 June 2022, corrected 25 June 2024) is worth understanding because it is the first single adopted method in this vault that returns a category rather than a rule-out.

Here is the whole trick. Test Guideline 492 and Test Guideline 492B use the same lab-grown human corneal tissue and the same readout — a dye called MTT that living cells convert into a coloured product, so the colour intensity read by an ordinary plate reader tells you how many cells survived. Test Guideline 492 applies the chemical once and checks one threshold. Test Guideline 492B applies it for several different lengths of time and reads the pattern across them.

Why that works: the Category 1 / Category 2 boundary is defined on whether the damage heals. A single snapshot of dead cells cannot see healing. But how fast the tissue dies is a decent stand-in — something that destroys the tissue in five minutes undiluted behaves like something that permanently damages a cornea; something that only kills it after two hours of dilute exposure behaves like an irritant.

The full decision rule, for liquids, is four lines of arithmetic:

  • more than 50% of cells alive at all exposure times → No Category
  • 50% or fewer alive at all exposure times → Category 1
  • any other pattern → Category 2

No expert judgement anywhere. loop_readiness: closed-loop-ready — a machine can read the plate and make the call. evidence_tier: regulatory-adopted, regulatory_status: OECD-TG-adopted. All three axes agree, which is rare in this vault, and it is not the interesting part.

Read the matrix, not the headline

The guideline's headline is 74.4% balanced accuracy across 151 chemicals — the weakest headline of any adopted eye method here. But this is the vault's first three-way result, and a three-way result has six ways to be wrong that matter differently. The full table, from the guideline:

Truth (rabbit data)Called serious damageCalled irritantCalled safe
Serious damage (50 chemicals)79%21%0%
Irritant (44 chemicals)18%69%12.5%
Safe (57 chemicals)2%23%75%

The best cell in that table is the zero. Of fifty chemicals that permanently damage a rabbit's eye, this test called none of them safe. Every one of its serious-damage mistakes is an under-grade — it said "irritant" instead of "serious damage," which still puts a hazard warning on the bottle. That is the direction you want a safety test to fail in.

The dangerous cell is not on the diagonal either. 12.5% of genuine irritants were called "no warning needed" — about one in eight real irritants declared harmless. That is the failure with a path to somebody's eye, and it is invisible inside the summary "69% correct for Category 2."

And a quarter of harmless chemicals get labelled anyway — 23% of safe chemicals came back as irritants. Nobody gets hurt; a formulator adds a warning that isn't warranted, or reformulates a product that was fine.

Two more things belong beside those numbers, plainly:

  • The company that sells the tissue ran the validation study. The guidance records that the method "underwent a prospective validation study conducted by industry" — EPISKIN, a L'Oréal subsidiary, which manufactures the one tissue the guideline names. It was independently reviewed by a panel drawn from the US, EU and Japanese validation bodies, and it did run in three laboratories including labs new to the assay. But it was not independently executed. funding_flag: [industry, method-developer].
  • There is one supplier. access_gate. "Adopted and machine-runnable" means less than it sounds if the loop can only be built by buying tissue from a single company — the same problem as the estrogen-receptor test whose cell line cannot be obtained at all.
  • And the 74.4% is measured against the rabbit test, which disagrees with itself 12% of the time. How much of the missing quarter is the method and how much is the noisy reference is not determinable from the guideline. This is not a defence of the method; it is a limit on what the number can tell you.

The sentence that corrected this vault

Now the part that changed how this vault describes itself.

This vault carries a rule it calls the correlated blind spot: when two tests in a battery fail on the same chemicals for the same structural reason, that battery casts one vote, not two. The rule matters more for automation than for people, because an automated system reads agreement between two tests as confirmation, trusts its decision rule, and iterates — fast, and confidently — into a systematic error. The vault's own documentation calls this "the vault's sharpest contribution to the autonomous-lab problem."

OECD Guidance Document 263, chapter 3:

"when using two or more test methods, the conditional independence of these test methods should be considered (Adriaens et al. 2017a; Hoffman et al., 2008)."

Conditional independence is the standard statistical name for the property. It has a literature going back to at least 2008. It is written into adopted OECD guidance as an instruction to assessors. The vault's rule is not an original contribution, and saying otherwise was wrong.

What survives the correction is sharper than what was claimed:

One — the instruction is a sentence, and there is no procedure attached. GD 263 says conditional independence "should be considered." It does not say how to measure it, what threshold makes two tests too dependent to stack, or what to do when they are. It cites two papers and moves on. Across all nine method descriptions that follow, it records the answer for no pair at all.

Two — the document contains the most correlated pair imaginable and never names it. GD 263 describes each method in a standard template with a field called "Scientific basis incl. MoA" (mode of action). For Test Guideline 492B, that field reads, in full: "See Section 4.3.4." Section 4.3.4 is Test Guideline 492. The guidance does not assert a separate mechanism for 492B because there isn't one — same tissue, same property, same readout, different stopwatch. So if a formulator runs both and they agree, that is one result reported twice. And in the same document, GD 263 advises combining several high-sensitivity no-warning methods "to increase the overall specificity of the testing strategy" — which this particular pairing structurally cannot do, because their false alarms come from the same population.

Three — every regulatory proposal this vault has caught building a correlated battery was written by a body with that sentence available. The UK's national strategy proposes replacing the live-fish toxicity test with two fish assays that both lack a nervous system and full metabolism, plus a computer prediction, and does not mention the shared gap. The EU's chemicals-regulation revision proposes the same pair. A thyroid test battery in European validation contains two methods measuring the same molecular event by the same technique. The problem was never that regulators lacked the concept. It is that nobody runs the check.

Four — the loop argument is genuinely this vault's own, and the statistical literature has no reason to make it. Neither cited paper, nor GD 263, mentions automation. A human may discount a suspiciously concordant battery. An automated system has no representation for "these two agreed for a bad reason."

And one more thing GD 263 gets right that this vault derived independently. Asked what to do about a shared failure mode, the guidance says: use "additional in vitro test methods … based, if possible, on different mechanism of actions." That is the same conclusion this vault drew from the Tox21 robotic screening platform, which solves the identical problem not by looking for agreement across assays but by adding an independent measurement into the same well. The fix for a correlated blind spot is a measurement that fails differently, not a bigger battery. It was already in eye guidance.

What the guidance admits about its own top tier

Two more findings, both stated by GD 263 about the methods it recommends.

The two slaughterhouse-eye tests fail together, for a reason that is structural.

"Currently accepted methods for identifying UN GHS Cat. 1, like BCOP and ICE, underpredict 14-47% of the in vivo Cat. 1 chemicals … mostly those inducing persistent effects without occurrence of initial high level injuries."

Somewhere between one in seven and one in two of the chemicals that permanently damage an eye are graded milder by these tests. The shared cause: the regulatory category is defined on whether the damage persists, and both tests measure how bad the damage is right now on tissue that is already dead and cannot be watched for three weeks. Different measurements — cloudiness, leakiness, swelling, dye retention, two species — and they fail together anyway, because the missing thing is not a biological subsystem, it is time.

That is a new species of correlated blind spot for this vault, and it has an uncomfortable property: the remedy the guidance prescribes cannot fix it. Adding a mechanistically different acute test does not add a temporal measurement. Mechanistic diversity is the right axis when the shared cause is a missing biological subsystem. It is the wrong axis when the shared cause is a missing dimension. And note that Test Guideline 492B has the same missing dimension — it approximates persistence from speed of killing, cleverly, which is precisely why its errors pile up at the two category boundaries.

And low specificity in these tests is the regulator's choice, not the method's flaw.

"due to the very high sensitivity required by regulatory authorities for accepting the use of in vitro test methods to identify chemicals not requiring hazard classification … their specificity will never go beyond 60-80% (the highest the specificity, the more limited the applicability)."

In plain terms: if a test is licensed to declare a chemical harmless, regulators demand it catch nearly every genuine hazard — and every threshold adjustment that buys that catch rate spends the ability to correctly clear safe chemicals. The best performers here, the chicken-eye and short-exposure tests, manage only 76–81% correct clearance of safe chemicals at a 97% catch rate for hazardous ones.

This re-frames several numbers this vault had recorded with mild alarm. Test Guideline 492 clears 63–72% of safe chemicals correctly. Test Guideline 494 manages 67%. The skin-allergy dendritic-cell test manages 66%. Four adopted methods with specificities in the sixties is a design consequence, not four independent disappointments. The parenthesis carries a second warning worth keeping: "the highest the specificity, the more limited the applicability" — a method can post a better number by simply answering fewer questions. A better specificity may just mean a smaller domain.

Could a machine run this? The honest answer is: all of it except the part that matters

Here is where the eye IATA earns its place in a vault about autonomous labs.

Every component is machine-runnable. Nine methods, all producing instrument numbers — optical density, electrical resistance, fluorescence, breakthrough patterns. Several are closed-loop-ready on their own: a plate reader takes the measurement and published arithmetic makes the call.

The assembly is not. GD 263's Part 2 is a weight-of-evidence judgement — the field's term for "an expert weighs everything available and reaches a conclusion." Its chapter headings are a thought process (coverage of sources, data quality, adequacy, consistency, conclusions); its annexes offer "an example of matrix" and "examples of WoE evaluations." Examples, not an algorithm. And even the choice of which route to take is a judgement: the assessor must first "formulate a hypothesis of the most likely eye hazard potential" and only then pick the top-down or bottom-up ladder. A human-set prior selects the experiment.

So: loop_readiness: human-in-the-loop. loop_blocker: the integration step has no published rule.

That blocker is a different animal from the vault's other human-in-the-loop cases, and the difference decides where money should go. The vault's four other maps at this tier are blocked at the scoring step — a trained person counting damaged cells down a microscope — and for those, machine vision is a demonstrated escape hatch (demonstrated, never validated, and not in any guideline). The eye IATA is blocked after every scoring step. No image classifier reaches it. What moves this blocker is a defined approach: a published rule over the module outputs. That is exactly what Test Guideline 467 already is for one fixed pair of assays, and exactly what the developmental-neurotoxicity battery has been waiting for with no date attached.

Two of the six battery architectures in this vault are now blocked on the same missing object. That is worth saying out loud, because "we need better assays" and "we need a rule for combining the assays we have" are completely different investments, and only the second one is cheap.

One part of the guidance does escape. For suspected middle-category chemicals, GD 263 routes to Test Guideline 467 or Test Guideline 492B — both of which have fixed published rules returning all three categories. That sub-path is fully specified and machine-runnable. A loop could execute the machine-runnable middle of this guidance; it would need a human to decide whether to enter it, and for every inconclusive result that comes out.

Where this actually leaves things

Ready? For the eye, yes — more so than almost any endpoint in this vault. Nine adopted methods, a lab-grown-human-tissue option that resolves all three hazard categories on its own, and an official position that the rabbit test is "only as a last resort." That is a genuine replacement story.

Ready for what? Hazard classification and labelling. Not for the finer distinctions: no adopted method separates the reversible-irritation sub-classes, and none of them can measure whether damage heals — the property the categories are actually defined on.

Could a machine run it? Every part except the assembly. And the assembly is not blocked by missing technology; it is blocked by a rule nobody has written.

And the thing to carry away, if only one thing: the guidance that assembles nine tests into an answer tells its readers to check whether those tests are independent, gives them no way to do it, and contains a pair that isn't. The check exists. It has a name and a literature. Nobody runs it — and an automated system that reads two agreeing tests as two pieces of evidence will run faster, and be wrong in exactly the same direction, much more confidently.

Grading, stated plainly. The eye-hazard IATA (OECD Guidance Document 263, 3rd edition, 2024): evidence_tier: formally-validated — every method it composes is an adopted, validated Test Guideline. regulatory_status: proposed — it is guidance, applied case-by-case, not a Test Guideline itself. loop_readiness: human-in-the-loop, blocker: the integration step is an unspecified weight-of-evidence judgement. funding_flag: none. No performance figure exists for the assembled strategy — every number in the document belongs to a component, so there is no baseline an automated version could be measured against. OECD Test Guideline 492B: regulatory-adopted / OECD-TG-adopted / closed-loop-ready, with a sole-supplier access gate, an industry-run validation, and a correlated blind spot with Test Guideline 492.
