Reproducible is not right

Is this method ready, what for, and could a machine run it? Short answers: it is reproducible and it is not validated; it is for early development decisions inside a company, not for a regulatory submission; and a machine could run it end to end today, which is a statement about the plumbing and not about whether the answer would be correct. Nobody has shown this test predicts a tumour in anything.

That combination is worth an article, because the vault does not usually get to see all three axes disagree this cleanly on one endpoint.

What is being tested, and why the current test is bad

Gene-edited cell therapies — the kind where CRISPR/Cas9 is used to cut and repair DNA in cells that are then put into a patient — carry a specific fear. The editing tool cuts somewhere it was not supposed to, the cell becomes cancer-like, and the patient develops a tumour years later. The first such therapy was approved in 2023 and many more are in development, so somebody has to check.

The standard check is called a tumorigenicity study: inject the human cells into mice bred to have almost no immune system, so they cannot reject foreign tissue, and watch for six to twelve months to see whether a tumour grows.

Two problems with that, and both are stated by the people who use it. It is slow and expensive. And it often does not work — whether human cells take root in a mouse at all varies enormously by cell type, because a mouse does not reproduce the tissue environment a human tumour needs. A negative result from a test that frequently fails to grow anything is weak evidence.

The dish alternative

Normal human cells will not grow unless they are attached to something. Cancer-like transformed cells will. That property is called anchorage-independent growth, and it is one of the oldest and most dependable laboratory signatures of a cell having gone wrong.

Two assays exploit it:

  • SACF — soft agar colony formation. Suspend the cells in a soft gel that gives them nothing to hold onto. Four weeks later, count whatever grew into a colony.
  • GILA — growth in low attachment. Culture them for two weeks on a surface treated to prevent attachment, then measure how many are still alive by reading ATP, the cell's energy currency, as a bioluminescent glow. Brighter means more living cells.

Both readouts are instrument readings. Colonies are counted by an imaging machine and its analysis software; the ATP is a light measurement. No human looks at a cell and decides what it is — which will matter in a moment.

What the study did

A consortium called HESI Global convened four laboratories, at Sanofi, AstraZeneca, Bristol Myers Squibb, Novartis and Bayer, to run the same experiment (Dorsheimer et al. 2026, Gene Therapy, PMID 42527625).

The test system is a spike-in: take a normal human breast cell line called MCF10A, and mix in a known percentage of the same cells with a tumour-suppressor gene called PTPN12 knocked out — an edit reliably known to make these cells grow anchorage-independently. If you know exactly what fraction of bad cells you put in, you can measure the smallest fraction the assay can still see. That number is the limit of detection.

Result one: the limit of detection was 0.8% edited cells at most sites, for both assays. Roughly one transformed cell in 125 is detectable — which is the kind of sensitivity this application actually needs, since a real product would contain a tiny minority of mis-edited cells.

Result two, and the one that matters: the four laboratories agreed with each other.

Agreement was measured with Spearman's rank correlation — a number between −1 and 1 asking whether two labs put the samples in the same order, without demanding they report the same absolute values. The authors did something honest here that is worth noticing: they stated their own noise floor. At their sample size, random data would produce coefficients anywhere between −0.64 and +0.64, so only coefficients above 0.64 count as agreement at all.

  • Soft agar colony formation: 0.988 to 1 between laboratories, and 0.952 to 1 within a laboratory across repeats. Essentially perfect ranking, everywhere.
  • Growth in low attachment: 0.333 to 0.583 between laboratories — below the noise floor, i.e. no demonstrated agreement at all.

The authors then found the reason and it is a real one, not a rescue. Above about 12.5% edited cells the ATP signal saturates: adding more transformed cells stops producing more glow, so the ranking breaks by construction. Exclude the saturated points and inter-laboratory agreement rises to 0.9 to 1.

That fix is legitimate and it narrows the assay. The usable window for the ATP assay is now stated: below 12.5%. The colony-counting assay showed no saturation across the whole range, and moves about 200-fold from 0% to 100% edited cells against the ATP assay's 15-fold — a much wider dial to read.

The three axes, kept apart

Scientific validity: peer-reviewed-method. Not the top rung, and the reason is specific. Four laboratories reproducing a result is genuine inter-laboratory reproducibility, which is the single property separating this vault's top two evidence rungs and the only one that cannot be faked by writing a better paper. But this was not a validation study: no independent validation body — not the European Union's EURL ECVAM, not the United States' ICCVAM, not Japan's JaCVAM — designed or ran it, there is no reference set of chemicals or edits with known answers, and there is exactly one test article, chosen because it gives the strongest possible signal.

So what four laboratories demonstrated is that the protocol transfers. It is silent on whether the assay gets the right answer, because nothing with a known right answer was tested.

Regulatory status: none. No Test Guideline, no qualification programme, no regulator has looked at it. The authors position the assays as "development-stage characterization tools contributing to tumorigenicity evidence generation … rather than as a standalone determinant" — which is a careful and accurate scope, and narrower than a reader skimming the abstract might assume.

Loop-readiness: automatable-open-loop. A machine reads the plate — an imager counts colonies, a luminometer reads ATP — and the whole thing is plate-based and demonstrably transferable between sites. What a machine cannot do is make the call, and the blocker is precise: nothing published states what colony count in a real edited product constitutes a tumorigenicity concern. The limit of detection in this study was computed per site by statistical test against a 0% spiked control — a control that exists only because somebody deliberately made a spike-in series. A therapeutic product arrives without one.

That puts this method in the vault's largest category: 21 maps whose assay is machine-readable and whose interpretation rule has never been written down.

Two things a reader should not skip

The hand-tuned setting inside the automated readout

At one site an incubator malfunctioned and the colonies grew smaller. The imaging and analysis settings had to be changed to count them, and the authors generalise from that:

"the imaging settings used herein need to be re-evaluated for each experiment."

So the 0.988-to-1 agreement was obtained with people tuning the analysis to each experiment's plates. Whether the assay would agree that well with the settings frozen is not reported and cannot be inferred.

This is not a criticism of the work — it is an unusually candid disclosure. It matters because "the readout is an instrument" and "the readout is objective" are different claims, and only the first is demonstrated here. A parameter re-tuned per experiment by a person is a human judgement sitting below the decision rule, where nobody looks for it, and where this vault's automatability grading does not reach.

The mouse said no, and the authors said the mouse was wrong

Buried in the discussion:

"PTPN12-edited MCF10A cells were also evaluated in an in vivo tumorigenicity study in NSG mice showing no tumor formation despite their clear anchorage-independent growth in vitro … the results of the in vivo highlight the usefulness of the assays for capturing transformation events that may not manifest in animal models."

The exact cells used here as the guaranteed-positive control produced no tumours in mice. The animal test — the thing these assays are alternatives to, and the thing they would conventionally be scored against — said negative where the dish said strongly positive. And the authors concluded that the animal test had missed something real.

They have grounds, and they are the same grounds that motivate the whole exercise: human cell engraftment in mice is documented as unreliable. But the symmetric reading is equally available. Anchorage-independent growth in a gel is a cellular property, not a tumour; the assay may be detecting a transformation-like state that a living body — with an immune system, a blood supply, and time — would clear without incident. That is what a false positive looks like from the inside.

With one test article, no negative-control edit and no reference set, this study cannot distinguish those two readings, and does not claim to.

For anyone thinking about closing a loop, this is the important part. An automated system needs a target — something to optimise toward or be scored against. In regulatory toxicology every target is a historical animal result. Here the target returned the opposite answer, and resolving that took a mechanistic argument about why the reference test fails. That argument is not an algorithm. It is exactly the human interpretation step the automatable-open-loop tier means.

The blind spot, and what it does to a battery

This method and the vault's older carcinogenicity entry — the cell transformation assays (SHE and Bhas 42), which score whether cultured cells pile up into visually abnormal colonies called foci — look like two independent lines of evidence. They are not, and the reason is architectural.

Both measure a transformation phenotype in a cultured cell line. Neither has an immune system, supporting tissue, a blood supply, or the years a real tumour takes. A cell state that a body would eliminate, and a genuine carcinogenic mechanism that needs tissue architecture to express itself, are invisible to both. In a weight-of-evidence package citing both, their agreement is close to one vote reported twice.

That matters more for a machine than for a person. A human assessor reading two concordant transformation assays can be told to discount the concordance. An automated system trusting the package's decision rule reads agreement as confirmation, moves on, and iterates — compounding a shared architectural gap quickly and confidently rather than catching it. This is a vault inference from the assay designs, not a claim either paper makes.

And the same problem exists inside this single map. Soft agar colony formation and growth in low attachment measure the same property, in the same cell line, under the same manipulation — differing only in the substrate and the readout chemistry. Running both is not two assays agreeing. It is one measurement reported twice.

Who paid, and why it is load-bearing here

Every experimental author works for a pharmaceutical company: Sanofi, AstraZeneca, Bristol Myers Squibb, Novartis, Bayer. Funding and coordination came from HESI Global's cell-and-gene-therapy committee, which also supplied the culture medium. No independent validation body took part.

The four laboratories are the parties who would otherwise pay for a twelve-month mouse study, and their paper concludes that a cheaper dish assay is reproducible.

That does not make the correlation coefficients wrong — they are arithmetic on data the paper reports, and the paper is unusually candid about its own weak spots (the saturation problem, the per-experiment tuning, the disagreement with the mouse). But this is precisely the configuration a bias watch exists for, and it is worth holding alongside the result rather than after it.

The uncomfortable general point

This vault's status vocabulary has five values, and they grade two things: how strong the evidence is, and whether anyone has accepted it. Neither of them asks whether the method is right.

A method can be perfectly reproducible and completely wrong. Four laboratories running an assay that measures the wrong thing will agree beautifully — reproducibility measures the stability of a measurement, not its correspondence to anything. That is not a hypothetical here: this is a method with excellent reproducibility, zero predictivity data, and a documented disagreement with the only reference test available.

"Reproducible" is the property that lets you build a loop. "Right" is the property that makes the loop worth building. They are independent, and only one of them has been demonstrated.

This is a learning synthesis of public literature, not regulatory advice. It does not certify this method for any testing programme, and "a machine could run it" is not "a machine should run it."
