Predicting carcinogens in a dish: how far the cell transformation assays actually got

Is there a non-animal way to ask whether a chemical causes cancer — and can you rely on it? The honest answer is "there's a genuinely validated in-vitro test that even catches the carcinogens the standard genetic tests miss — but it never became an adopted Test Guideline, and you can't use it on its own." Carcinogenicity is the endpoint NAMs find hardest to replace, and the cell transformation assays (CTAs) are the clearest case in this vault of the gap between scientifically validated and regulatorily accepted sitting wide open for years without closing.

Why cancer is the hard one — and what a CTA actually measures

The two-year rodent bioassay CTAs are meant to reduce is the most expensive, animal-intensive study in toxicology. The in-vitro cell transformation assay takes a different route: it grows cells in a dish, exposes them, and scores whether they take on a cancer-like phenotype — colonies that pile up, lose contact inhibition, and grow in a transformed morphology. It's a functional readout of the transformation process itself rather than a proxy marker.

That matters because of a real blind spot in the existing non-animal toolkit. The in-vitro genotoxicity battery — the Ames bacterial reverse-mutation test (OECD TG 471) for gene mutation and the in-vitro micronucleus test (OECD TG 487) for chromosome damage — only catches carcinogens that work by damaging DNA. A large class of non-genotoxic carcinogens (tumour promoters, hormonal and epigenetic mechanisms) sails straight through those tests. The two CTAs in the OECD guidance — the Syrian Hamster Embryo (SHE) assay on primary cells, and the Bhas 42 assay, built on a v-Ha-ras-transfected BALB/c 3T3 line that is already "initiated" so it reads tumour-promotion activity — are among the few in-vitro methods that respond to both genotoxic and non-genotoxic carcinogens. That coverage is the whole reason to care about them.

Where the science axis sits: genuinely, independently validated

This is not one lab's proof-of-concept. The Bhas 42 validation was run by the Hadano Research Institute under the supervision of JaCVAM (the Japanese validation centre), then peer-reviewed by EURL ECVAM's Scientific Advisory Committee (ESAC) in December 2012 and fully endorsed by EURL ECVAM, which described both the 6-well and 96-well formats as "standardised, transferable and reproducible between laboratories." That is an independent-body validation — a regulatory validation centre, not the method's own developer signing off on it — which is exactly why this vault records funding_flag: none and weights it more heavily than a self-validation would deserve. In this vault's grading, inter-laboratory reproducibility peer-reviewed by a validation body earns evidence_tier: formally-validated — the tier just below a fully adopted method.

Where the regulatory axis sits — and why it hasn't moved

Here is the part it would be easy to overstate. The CTAs have OECD Guidance Documents — GD 214 for the SHE assay, GD 231 for Bhas 42 — but there is no OECD Test Guideline. A Guidance Document tells you how to run the method; a Test Guideline is what a regulatory programme can require and accept for a decision. EURL ECVAM explicitly recommended that an OECD Test Guideline be developed for the transformation assay — and that recommendation has not yet been acted on. So on the acceptance axis this vault marks the method regulatory_status: proposed, deliberately kept distinct from its strong science score.

Two honest limitations explain the stall. First, a CTA cannot be used stand-alone: EURL ECVAM is explicit that it is "unlikely to fully replace any particular animal-based test method" and that results must feed a weight-of-evidence / integrated testing strategy alongside genotoxicity data and — as this vault maps it — repeat-dose bioactivity as the indirect route to carcinogenic potential. Second, transformation is scored by morphology, and morphological-focus scoring carries a well-known subjectivity concern that the field has never fully put to rest. Validated and reproducible, yes; a push-button replacement for the rodent bioassay, no.

Could a machine run it? The third axis — and the escape hatch that just showed up

This vault grades every method on a third axis the science and regulatory ones don't cover: could an automated system actually run the assay and make the call? The CTA sits at human-in-the-loop — a robot can grow the cells and image the dish, but the decision that matters, which colonies count as transformed foci, is made by a trained expert's eye, and the guidance itself names that subjectivity as the weak point. That eye is the blocker.

Until mid-2026 this was the only one of this vault's three eye-scored methods — the others being the micronucleus test and zebrafish teratogenicity — with no demonstrated way around the human eye. On 17 July 2026 that changed. Masumoto and colleagues (Scientific Reports, 2021) trained a standard image-recognition neural network to classify a colony image as a transformed focus or not. It scored very well on the chemical it trained on and removed the wild disagreement between novice human scorers (who labelled anywhere from a fifth to four-fifths of the same images as positive). So the escape hatch — machine vision replacing the human eye — is now demonstrated for all three of these methods.

But keep the three caveats, because they are why this does not promote the CTA to machine-runnable, and why this is the thinnest of the three demonstrations. It is one lab's result, trained largely on one chemical, and it already gets shakier on chemicals it hasn't seen. It classifies individual colonies — it does not turn that into a "this chemical is a carcinogen" verdict, which is the step that actually matters (the micronucleus version went further and reproduced the real regulatory number; this one stops short). And it learns from the subjective human labels it is meant to replace, so it makes the scoring more consistent rather than more objective. Crucially, no independent body has validated it and it is in no guideline — so a trained human is still the one who signs the regulatory call. The one place it scores clean is bias: it comes from academic and public-sector labs with no competing interest, unlike the micronucleus scorer, which was co-written by the maker of the instrument it runs on. Better science, cleaner funding — pick at most one; none of the three has all of it, and none is validated. And note the irony worth sitting with: carcinogenicity is the hardest endpoint to replace, and it got the flimsiest escape hatch.

One thing has since been said more precisely about that scorer, and it is worth the extra sentence. The drop from "very good on the chemical it trained on" to "noticeably worse on chemicals it hadn't seen" — 0.95 to 0.87 on the standard measure of how well a classifier separates two classes — is not just weaker evidence. It is the model being pushed outside the range of things it was fitted to, and the cost being visible only because someone happened to test for it. In July 2026 the European Commission's research centre named exactly this as the operative risk when a trained image scorer is deployed, and said such a model needs its own stated range of applicability, separate from the assay's. Of this vault's four machine-vision scorers, this is the only one anyone has measured that way. That is a point in the study's favour — and a reason to distrust the other three more than their headline numbers suggest.

The blind spot that only shows up when you look at two assays together

In July 2026 a second in-vitro route to the same endpoint arrived, and putting it beside the CTA exposes something neither test reveals on its own.

The newcomer is the anchorage-independent growth assay: instead of a trained eye judging colony shape, an imager counts colonies growing while suspended in soft gel — cancer-like cells can do this, normal cells mostly cannot. Same biological property, different readout. And the two tests land in opposite places on two different axes. The CTA has an internationally endorsed validation and two OECD Guidance Documents; the newcomer has no regulatory standing at all. The CTA needs a human to make the call; the newcomer's counting step is fully machine-readable. Regulatory standing high, automatability low — and exactly the reverse next door, on the same endpoint.

Now the part that matters if anyone ever runs both. Both tests ask a cultured cell line whether it has turned cancer-like. Neither has an immune system, surrounding tissue, a blood supply, or time. So a cell that looks transformed in a dish but that a real body would clear reads positive in both — and a cancer mechanism that needs tissue architecture to happen reads negative in both. When two tests fail on the same things for the same underlying reason, agreement between them is close to one vote, not two.

This vault flags that pattern wherever it finds it, because it is much worse for an automated system than for a person. A human reading two concordant results might pause and ask whether the tests are really independent. A machine following a rule that says "two positives means act" will take the agreement as confirmation, and iterate — fast, confidently, in the same wrong direction every time. Speed turns a known bias into a compounding one. That is the single most important thing this vault knows that the vendor literature about laboratory automation does not say.

Two honest qualifications. This particular pairing is this vault's inference from how the two assays are built, not a claim either research group makes. And the newer assay is itself thin evidence: four laboratories reproduced the protocol closely, on one cell line with one engineered change, and nobody tested whether it gets the right answer — four labs agreeing about one positive control establishes that a procedure travels, and nothing about whether it is correct.

Bottom line. For carcinogenicity, the cell transformation assays are real, independently validated, and uniquely able to flag non-genotoxic carcinogens that the genotoxicity battery misses — formally-validated on the science axis. But they live in OECD Guidance Documents, not an adopted Test Guideline (regulatory_status: proposed), cannot be used on their own, and rest on subjective morphological scoring that keeps them human-in-the-loop on the automation axis — even though, as of July 2026, a machine-vision scorer has finally been demonstrated (unvalidated, single-lab, foci-only). All three axes point the same way: take them seriously as one input to a weight-of-evidence carcinogenicity assessment; do not treat them as a cleared stand-alone replacement for the cancer bioassay, and do not read "a neural network can score the foci" as "a machine can make the call." And if a package ever cites both this test and the anchorage-independent growth assay, do not count that as two independent answers — they share the same architectural blindness, and an automated system that treats their agreement as corroboration will be wrong faster than a human would be.

One late signal, and it points at use rather than at evidence. In July 2026 the EU's flagship chemical-risk partnership put the Bhas 42 assay into the first-tier screen of a nine-country programme testing eight replacements for bisphenol A — the plastics chemical restricted on hormone-disruption grounds — precisely because it reaches the non-genotoxic carcinogens the mutation tests cannot see. That programme has published no results yet (they are due in early 2027), so it says nothing about whether the assay works. What it says is that a serious regulatory-facing effort, designed with Europe's food-safety and chemicals agencies, picked this method for a real job in 2026 — which is a different fact from validation, and worth keeping separate from it.

This is a learning synthesis of the public evidence, not regulatory advice. Read the method map for the dated through-line as this keeps moving — three things are worth watching: whether ECVAM's Test-Guideline recommendation is ever taken up, whether the demonstrated automated scorer ever earns a ring trial or a place in the guidance (either would move this method toward machine-runnable), and what the European programme's 2027 results say about eight chemicals nobody has properly tested.
