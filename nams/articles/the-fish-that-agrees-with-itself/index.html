The fish that agrees with itself

Four laboratories ran the same non-animal test on the same 33 chemicals. On the question "is this animal harmed," they agreed 84–100% of the time. On the question "is the harm to its nervous system," the same four labs, reading the same kind of data from the same protocol, agreed only 68–84% of the time. Nothing about the measurement changed between those two numbers. This vault has spent a month describing the thing that blocks automation in toxicology as a missing decision rule, in a tone that implies somebody just has not written it down yet. Here is a case where you can watch four expert groups fail to agree on what the rule should be.

Short answer to the three questions this vault always asks. Is the science good? Reasonably — it is the first multi-laboratory reproducibility evidence anywhere in the developmental-neurotoxicity column, which had none, and the potency numbers transfer well. It is not a formal validation and this article will be specific about the difference. Is it accepted by a regulator? No. Nowhere, by nobody. Could a machine run it? The measurement, yes, completely — a camera and a tracking algorithm, no human anywhere. The call, no, and this method is the clearest evidence the vault holds about why that second answer is expensive.

What the test is

Developmental neurotoxicity is the question of whether a chemical damages a nervous system while it is still being built. It is one of the hardest endpoints in toxicology and one of the most consequential — the classic cases are lead and certain pesticides, where the damage is done during development and shows up years later as changed behaviour rather than as visible injury.

The traditional test is a mammalian study lasting many months. The non-animal alternative this vault has tracked longest is the developmental neurotoxicity in-vitro battery — 17 separate laboratory tests on human stem-cell-derived nerve cells, each probing a different step of building a brain.

This method takes a different route. Zebrafish embryos are put one to a well in a plastic plate, dosed with a chemical while they develop, and then filmed. On a fixed schedule the lights go out and come back on. A healthy larva reacts to sudden darkness with a burst of swimming — it is an innate, reliable behaviour. A larva whose developing nervous system has been damaged reacts differently, or not at all. A camera tracks each larva's position over time and turns it into a number: distance moved per interval. That is the Light–Dark Transition Test.

Nobody looks down a microscope. Nobody grades anything. The whole measurement is a video file and a tracking algorithm.

The study, and the number that matters

Truong and colleagues ran it in four independent laboratories on 33 compounds — 28 suspected developmental neurotoxicants (organophosphate and pyrethroid insecticides, flame retardants, neonicotinoids, nicotine) and 5 chemicals expected to be clean (DOI: 10.1016/j.neuro.2026.103504, PMID: 42392410).

Two different calls come off the same recording:

  • Developmental toxicity — is this larva damaged at all? Dead, deformed, stunted.
  • Developmental neurotoxicity — is the damage specifically neurological, at doses where the animal is otherwise alive and functional?

Up to a third of chemicals got a different neurotoxicity verdict depending on which laboratory ran them.

That is not sloppiness in one lab, and the giveaway is a third number: where the labs did agree a compound was active, their dose estimates sat within a mean 3.2-fold of each other. For a whole-organism biological assay across four sites, three-fold is a good result. The measurement transfers. The classification does not.

The reason is structural. Calling something a neurotoxicant means separating "this animal's nervous system is damaged" from "this animal is sick." A dying larva also swims less. So you have to pick a concentration window where the animal is alive and functioning but behaving differently — and where that window starts is a judgement. Four expert groups drew it four ways.

Why this changes how to read "no agreed decision rule"

This vault sorts every method by whether a machine could run it end to end. The largest category — 18 methods — is "the readout is machine-readable, but nobody has published a rule for turning the number into a hazard call." It is the state the Tox21 robotic screening platform has been in since 2013: robots running ten thousand chemicals a week, humans still deciding what the results mean.

Written that way, it sounds like an administrative backlog. Somebody should convene a committee.

This method shows what is actually underneath it. Here the rule is missing not because the paperwork is late, but because four laboratories with the same data disagree about a third of the answers. You could write down whichever rule any one of them used. You would then have a perfectly reproducible automated pipeline that reproduced one laboratory's judgement, at speed, forever. That is not the same thing as automating the field's answer, and an automated system has no way to tell the difference.

It sharpens something this vault recorded a night earlier about self-driving laboratories. The most complete closed-loop result in the literature — an AI running a robotic laboratory for six months and beating the published state of the art — worked because its goal was dollars per gram: a number the instrument reports, computable in the well, with no external authority needed. Regulatory toxicology's goal is a classification defined by a committee.

Here you can see both, coming off the same camera. "Is this larva harmed" behaves like a computable objective — 84–100% agreement. "Is this neurotoxicity" behaves like a committee classification — 68–84%. Same fish, same plate, same file.

Same fish, same plate, two different answers about automation

The sharpest thing about this method is what sits next to it. This vault also maps zebrafish teratogenicity — zebrafish embryos of comparable age, in comparable plates, frequently the same experiment — and grades it one tier lower on automatability, because a human is load-bearing in the call.

The difference is not the fish. It is what gets measured.

One is a measurement. The other is a taxonomy rendered by a trained eye. "Pericardial edema, moderate" is a category drawn from a list somebody learned; distance moved is a number.

There is a practical warning in this, and it applies well beyond fish. Anyone building an automated laboratory reasons about equipment and test systems — "we have zebrafish larvae in plates, so zebrafish work is automatable." That reasoning is wrong at exactly the wrong level of detail. The same larvae in the same plate are automatable for one question and not for another, and which is which is a property of the question, not of the animal, the plate, or the robot. The unit that carries automatability is the readout, not the test system.

The good part nobody put in the title

Buried in the paper is a sentence worth more to an automated laboratory than the headline result. The authors built "a novel three-tiered quality control framework" that "removed 10.9% of plates passing basic viability criteria."

Read that carefully. These are plates whose fish were alive and healthy enough to pass the ordinary acceptance check. They were discarded anyway, by a stated rule, because something else made the behavioural data untrustworthy. One plate in nine.

A human operator does this constantly and invisibly — glances at a plate, notices the edge wells look wrong, quietly reruns it. None of that is written down, and none of it survives being handed to an unattended machine. A loop with no way to refuse its own data does not become careful when the data is bad. It becomes confidently wrong, quickly.

This is only the second such rule this vault has found. The first is inside an adopted guideline — OECD Test Guideline 494, a non-animal eye test, which runs a pre-test letting the assay measure that a chemical is outside what it can validly assess and decline to answer. That one refuses chemicals. This one refuses runs. A real automated laboratory needs both, and until this month the vault had only ever seen one.

And a refusal always has a price that nobody reports. TG 494 refuses 32% of chemicals, and its impressive 96% catch rate is measured only on the 68% it accepted. The same arithmetic applies here: this assay's 93% detection figure is measured on the 89.1% of plates that survived quality control. A performance number and a refusal rate belong in the same sentence and almost never appear in one.

Three things landed after this was written, and one of them undercuts the headline

Updated 2026-08-15.

One: the four laboratories may not have been putting the same thing in the wells. The whole force of this article is that the measurement transfers across sites and the judgement does not — 84–100% agreement on "is the larva harmed," 68–84% on "is the harm neurological." That contrast assumes the four labs started from comparable material. In August 2026 somebody measured the step that supplies it. A group at Qatar University bought a commercial artificial-intelligence embryo sorter — a machine that images zebrafish eggs as they flow past, counts them, grades them and physically routes them into tubes — and benchmarked it against their own technicians across 117,956 embryos (Al-Absi et al. 2026, Biology). The manufacturer had no role in the study and the authors say so explicitly, which makes this the rare case of a buyer measuring a lab robot rather than a vendor describing one.

It undercounted by 13.41% — one embryo in seven, consistently in the same direction, with roughly three times a technician's run-to-run spread. It missed 50.8% of the fluorescent-labelled embryos it was told to pick out, on the one task where the right answer can be checked independently. And its quality grades — the four categories it sorts embryos into, with names like fertilized and unfertilized — turned out to have no statistical relationship to what the embryos went on to do. Survival, hatching and deformity rates were indistinguishable across every category (p > 0.05). 96.1% of the embryos it labelled "unfertilized" hatched. An unfertilised egg cannot hatch.

None of this was done in a toxicology experiment, and none of it was done to this assay. It is the step before it. But a miscount is an unrecorded change in how many animals were in the well, and every percentage in a behavioural screen divides by that number. This vault has added it as a caution on the reproducibility figures above rather than a correction to them: nothing here says the four laboratories' inputs differed — only that nobody checked, and that the tooling now available for checking is, on this evidence, worse than a person at exactly the part that matters.

Two — and this is the general lesson, worth more than the device. A classifier can be trained to high accuracy against labels a human assigned by looking, and still carry no information about the outcome the label is a proxy for. Human graders at that stage are themselves unreliable; the machine may have learned their grading perfectly. It learned a proxy, and the proxy did not predict survival. For an automated laboratory this is the worst available failure mode, because the system is fast, internally consistent, and optimising against the wrong quantity — and nothing inside the loop can detect it, since the loop's ground truth is the label. It took raising 117,956 embryos for four days and counting who hatched, an experiment entirely outside the loop, to see it. Set that beside this article's quality-control section: a rule that lets a machine refuse its own data is necessary and is not sufficient, because a machine can also be confidently wrong about data that is fine.

Three: a second group found this assay the most sensitive member of the battery, and the person reporting it sells it. A 95-chemical screen published in August 2026 (DOI: 10.1016/j.neuro.2026.103485) reports "the zebrafish assay was the most sensitive for detecting potency" — firing at lower concentrations than the cell-based assays across drugs, flame retardants, fungicides, herbicides, PFAS and insecticides. Three reasons it changes nothing here, each sufficient alone. A commercial zebrafish screening company co-authored it, which is the same conflict this article already flags on the four-laboratory study. Sensitivity is the cheap half of the measurement — a test that fires early is only useful if it fires early selectively, and this article's entire finding is that selectivity is where the four labs diverged; the new paper reports selectivity "varies significantly by assay and chemical class" without giving this assay's number. And the paper labels the zebrafish leg an in-vivo assay, which is a live and unresolved disagreement about whether a five-day-old larva is an animal test at all.

What it does not do, said plainly

It is not validated. Four laboratories reproducing a method is real evidence, and it is not the same thing as a ring trial — a formally managed study where a validation body sends blinded chemicals to independent labs. No validation body ran this. No blinding is stated. The protocol is described as "agreed-upon protocol elements" rather than a single harmonised one. This vault downgraded the 17-assay in-vitro battery in July for exactly the error of treating multi-party standardisation as validation, and it is not going to make the same mistake in the opposite direction here.

It is accepted by no regulator anywhere. Not an OECD Test Guideline, not in a validation pipeline.

"100% of negatives detected" is five chemicals. Five. That is not a claim about how often the test wrongly flags a safe chemical; it is a number with no denominator worth having.

A contract research organisation that sells zebrafish screening co-authored the paper that recommends zebrafish screening as the first tier of testing. That is exactly the conflict this vault flags on every method-developer study, and it does not disappear because respected public laboratories — the US National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, the National Research Council of Canada, Oregon State University — are also on the author list. The public participation raises the weight of the evidence. It does not remove the flag.

And behaviour is not a mechanism. A changed swimming pattern says something is wrong with the animal. It does not say what, or where, or why. The whole reason the 17-assay battery exists is to answer which developmental process was disrupted. This method is offered as a cheap first filter feeding that battery, not as a replacement for it.

The blind spot, which is this vault's own inference

The paper proposes a cascade: this assay first, then the in-vitro battery, then mammalian studies if needed. A cascade is a battery with an order.

This vault applies one rule to every battery it maps: do the components fail on the same chemicals for the same reason? Because if they do, the battery casts one vote and looks like two — and an automated system reads the agreement as confirmation and iterates confidently into the error.

Ask it here. Zebrafish larvae at this stage have limited ability to metabolise foreign chemicals — the enzyme systems that turn a harmless parent molecule into a dangerous one are not fully running yet. That limitation is already recorded in this vault as the documented reason two other fish-based methods share a blind spot. The in-vitro battery at tier two is human stem-cell-derived nerve cells, with no liver and no systemic metabolism either.

So a chemical that is harmless until the body activates it can read clean at tier one and clean at tier two, for one shared reason. And in a cascade it is worse than in a parallel battery: a clean result at tier one means tier two never runs. A parallel battery with a shared blind spot gives you one vote where you thought you had two. A cascade with a shared blind spot gives you silence — and there is no concordance left to be suspicious of.

The paper does not say any of this. It is this vault's inference, recorded as one, for a human to confirm or throw out.

And since this article was written the shared blind spot got wider, not narrower. A third whole-organism developmental screen entered this vault in July 2026 — a nematode worm assay for developmental and reproductive toxicity — and its arrival made visible something already true of the other two. None of the three has a placenta, a maternal-fetal circulation, or mammalian maternal metabolism. A chemical that harms a mammalian embryo only by crossing a placenta, by poisoning the placenta itself, or through a metabolite the mother's liver manufactures is invisible to a fish larva in a well and to a worm on a chip alike. Because the three cover different endpoints — neurodevelopment here, malformation and reproduction on the siblings — a battery assembled from them presents as broader coverage than it is. That is mechanistic reasoning, not a measured miss rate: nobody has run these three against a placenta-dependent chemical set, which is itself the gap worth recording.

Where that leaves it

A cheap, fast, whole-organism screen with a fully machine-read measurement, reproduced across four laboratories, accepted by nobody, sold in part by one of its authors, and blocked from automation by a rule that four expert groups cannot presently agree on.

Machine-runnable is not the same as ready, and it is nowhere near the same as endorsed. What this method actually contributes to the automation question is not another automatable assay. It is the first clear look at why the last step is hard: the field's missing decision rules are not missing documents. Some of them are missing agreements.

This is a learning ledger, not regulatory advice. It organises public evidence about non-animal methods; it does not certify any method for a real testing programme, and it does not certify any method as safe to automate.
