Experiment selection — the half of the loop that chooses what to run next — loop-infrastructure map

Sub-threads: none — one argument: can anything choose the next experiment well enough to be trusted with the choice. Selection schemes and the benchmarks that score them are not two strands — each is proposed with the other and is worthless without it. Underneath every bullet sits one recurring finding: the uncertainty estimate the selection rests on is the part that has not been shown to hold. [reviewed 2026-08-26 @ 10 bullets]

Why this map exists

Automation of the doing half of a laboratory is a solved-enough problem that the field has moved on. The parent map's clearest statement of it comes from a company that builds these systems: "As execution is automated, the remaining bottleneck moves to experiment selection."

That bottleneck is a different object from everything else the parent map tracks. Robot hands, transparent labware perception, protocol validation, runtime failure detection, benchmark validity — those are about whether a machine can carry out an experiment and whether anyone can tell if it did. This map is about whether the machine picks the right experiment to carry out, which is the leg that makes a loop a loop rather than a very fast pipetting service.

And it is the leg with the most direct structural analogue in regulatory toxicology, which is why it earns a map rather than a footnote: tiered testing is an experiment-selection problem. Run a cheap screen across many chemicals, spend the expensive capacity — an organ-on-chip, a repeat-dose study, an animal test — only where the screen pointed. That is multi-fidelity optimisation, and toxicology has been doing it by committee for thirty years.

What we believe now

Four findings, from four unrelated groups, and they compound into one uncomfortable conclusion.

1. The selection layer consumes the interpretation layer's output, so it cannot be adopted first. A cost-aware selection policy needs, for every cheap diagnostic it might buy, a likelihood model: how much does this measurement tell me about which hypothesis is right? In materials chemistry those are hand-specified from domain knowledge, and robustness to getting them wrong is stated by the authors as "an open question." In toxicology that number does not exist. Nobody has published how much a cheap in-vitro reporter assay tells you about a chronic in-vivo outcome — which is a restatement of the missing decision rule that sits on the majority of this vault's method maps. So the maps stuck at automatable-open-loop are not stuck on one missing piece blocking the back of the loop; the same missing piece blocks the front.

2. Nobody had told the optimiser what things cost. Standard acquisition functions "do not price actions, so a $100 diagnostic and a $5,000 optimization plate cannot be weighed within one budget." Bayesian optimisation — the machinery behind most self-driving-lab results — treats every candidate experiment as equally expensive, and in a real laboratory they differ by orders of magnitude.

3. ⚠ The engine's two most-trusted properties do not do what the field assumes. Benchmarked across fifteen surrogate models and nine problems: uncertainty-driven exploration is not reliably better than plain greedy exploitation (it helped on some benchmarks and roughly doubled the error on others), and how well-calibrated a model's uncertainty is does not predict how good an answer it finds — the correlation ran negative on every benchmark tested. The best-calibrated family was the weakest optimiser group on the chemistry problems.

⚠ Confirmed independently 2026-08-22. A cancer combination-screening model found the same divergence with a different architecture, a different uncertainty estimator (Monte-Carlo dropout) and a different objective: uncertainty-weighted selection gave the best prediction accuracy and 6 percentage points worse hit recall than weighting by predicted effect size, because "uncertainty-guided selection targets regions of model ignorance rather than regions of differential drug sensitivity." Two unrelated fields, no cross-citation, same result. → uncertainty-guided-selection-finds-ignorance-not-answers

Why (3) matters beyond the loop. Calibrated uncertainty is close to word-for-word what regulatory guidance on AI models asks for. The property that gets a model accepted and the property that makes it a good engine for the loop are, on this evidence, different properties — see regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility and calibrated-uncertainty-does-not-predict-optimisation-performance. That is not an argument against the requirement; a regulator asking a model to know what it does not know is asking for something reasonable, and "it optimises better" was never the justification. It is an observation that the field has been assuming the two point the same way.

4. ⚠ Added 2026-08-14 — the language model does not beat the classical machinery, and the domain knowledge that was supposed to be its edge is a measured liability. Against GP-UCB (Gaussian-process upper-confidence-bound Bayesian optimisation, textbook since 2010, no language and no domain knowledge), on 45 biology optimisation tasks, no model's confidence interval for "fraction of tasks beaten" excludes the 50% coin flip. And running every task twice — once with the real parameter names, units and subject, once with all meaning stripped — the semantically informed condition matched the published-best design 10.4 percentage points less often by round 30, with the gap entirely in the response to feedback rather than the starting guess, and reversing on 6 of 6 tasks where the literature-typical answer is not the best answer. The sign flips on a control set where the literature consensus is correct, which is what rules out confusion: the knowledge is not being misunderstood, it is being obeyed.

Why (4) compounds (1) rather than replacing it. Finding 1 says the front of the loop is blocked by a missing input. Finding 4 says it is also blocked by an input that hurts — and regulatory toxicology, with thirty years of standard test systems, standard reference chemicals and a validation culture that rewards agreement with precedent, is the most anchor-rich setting either literature has looked at. Both obstacles sit at the front of the loop, and one of them is worse the more expert your model is.

  • 2026-08-12 — ⚠⚠ THE DECIDING LEG'S ENGINE GETS BENCHMARKED, AND THE TWO PROPERTIES THE FIELD TRUSTS MOST BOTH FAIL TO DO WHAT IS ASSUMED. Map forked from automation-self-driving-labs tonight; Status emerging; no method anywhere in the vault moves. Ingested lee-2026-transfer-learning-multifidelity-bayesian-optimization (arXiv 2607.23404, submitted 26 July 2026, preprint, not peer reviewed; King's College London, funding_flag: none commercially, with the ordinary method-developer pressure recorded — the authors advocate transfer-learning surrogates and are also the party scoring them). Fifteen surrogate models (four Gaussian-process methods, eleven transfer-learning architectures) across nine benchmarks, 20 seeds, with the acquisition rule, fidelity budget and model size held identical across all fifteen so a difference is attributable to the surrogate rather than the search policy — the study's best design decision. (1) The horse race, which is the least interesting part. Gaussian processes — the default engine of self-driving labs for two decades — win on smooth low-dimensional synthetic functions (final regret 0.07 and 0.05 against 0.43 and 1.24) and lose badly on the real chemistry and materials problems (0.41 against 6.55 on porous framework materials; 0.06 against 0.17 on molecular polarizability; 1.89 against 3.09 on materials band gaps). Regret is how far short of the true best answer the campaign ended; lower is better. The default tool is the worst tool exactly where the search space is a chemical one. (2) ⚠ Exploration is not reliably worth it. Replacing greedy exploitation with five standard uncertainty-driven acquisition rules produced case-dependent results, not uniform improvement. On the porous-framework benchmark greedy beat all five for ten of eleven surrogates and the best of them roughly doubled the regret; the Gaussian process's own regret went 0.88 → 6.13 when its uncertainty was used to drive exploration. On two other chemistry benchmarks the pattern reversed. The authors' summary: greedy is the most robust single default, and exploration must be selected per problem rather than assumed. The entire argument for Bayesian optimisation over sorting a list is the exploration term, and it earns its keep only sometimes. (3) ⚠⚠ Calibration does not predict optimisation performance, and the sign is backwards. Across all fifteen surrogates the correlation between expected calibration error and final regret was negative on every one of the nine benchmarks, from −0.88 to −0.08. The best-calibrated family was the weakest optimiser group on the molecular problems. This is the finding with reach beyond the loop, because calibrated uncertainty is what regulatory AI guidance asks a model for. → calibrated-uncertainty-does-not-predict-optimisation-performance (4) A test for when the machinery is unnecessary at all, and it transfers to tiered testing. Against a surrogate-free baseline that simply ranks by the cheap measurement, two regimes appear. Where the top 1% of cheap candidates and the top 1% of expensive ones coincide (overlap ≈ 1, screening regret ≤ 0.09), every method including the no-model baseline performed identically — the learned surrogate bought nothing. Where they diverge, the surrogate choice swung the outcome by more than an order of magnitude. The deciding quantity is agreement at the TOP of the ranking, not correlation across the range — and a screening assay's job in a tiered strategy is exactly to put the right chemicals at the top of the queue. A screen with excellent overall concordance and poor agreement about which chemicals are worst is a screen that looks validated and misdirects the budget. → a-cheap-screen-earns-its-keep-only-when-it-disagrees-at-the-top (5) Honest ceiling. Not peer reviewed. No physical experiment anywhere in it — every "evaluation" is a lookup into a pre-computed dataset or a synthetic function, two of the nine benchmarks being synthetic functions the authors constructed in favourable and unfavourable variants. Status HOLDS at emerging; loop_readiness HOLDS at not-applicable. Ingested zhang-2026-leap-trajectory-llm-iterative-design (LEAPBench, arXiv 2605.15341v1, submitted 14 May 2026, preprint, not peer reviewed; all five authors at Pareto.ai, funding_flag: [industry] with a strong mitigation — the headline is bad news for the product category, and all tasks, oracles, prompts and baselines are released; full text read and archived).

    The setup. 55 sequential design tasks (45 biology, 10 education) built from 2,719 experimental datapoints across 375 published studies. The model proposes parameters, gets one number back, proposes again, for 30 rounds. Scored on best-so-far area under the curve — integrate the running best over the 30 rounds, so arriving early counts. Baseline: GP-UCB, textbook Gaussian-process Bayesian optimisation, no language and no domain knowledge. Eight frontier models, four runs per cell, web search disabled.

    (1) The measurement problem, which lands directly on how this vault reads every number it holds. Existing benchmarks score the final answer at a fixed round count. In a real laboratory each round is a plate, a reagent and a week. Switching to trajectory scoring names a different best model on 24 of 45 tasks (53%), twelve of them by three ranks or more; on 14 of those 24 the two winners land within 1% of each other by round 30 and the trajectory winner got there a median of 8 rounds earlier (round 7 vs 15). Nearly every performance figure in this vault answers "was it right?" and not one answers "how much did it cost to get there?" — and the second is the question that decides whether a loop is worth building. → no-language-model-beat-a-twenty-year-old-optimiser-at-choosing-experiments

    (2) Against a classical baseline, no model clears the coin flip.     (3) ⚠ THE FINDING TO KEEP LONGEST: the domain knowledge is not being misunderstood, it is being obeyed. Every task runs twice — domain-aware (real parameter names, units, categories, the task's subject named) versus domain-agnostic ("optimize a black-box function", parameters renamed X1, X2, C1: A, B — same ranges, all meaning stripped). On the 16 biology tasks where feedback can in principle find the best answer, domain-aware matched the published-best design 10.4 percentage points LESS often at round 30 (p = 0.004), and 7 of 8 models show it. Three controls make it a finding rather than an artifact: round-1 match rates are indistinguishable (+0.01, p = 0.60) while the round-1-to-30 climb is 11.3 points smaller (p = 0.004) — a feedback-responsiveness gap, not a starting-quality one; parameter-space diversity is higher under domain-aware, so it is not under-exploration; and the sign flips on the education control set, where literature consensus generally is correct (73% domain-aware pair-win vs 42% on biology). Narrowed to the 6 tasks where the literature-typical answer differs from the published best, domain-agnostic wins 6 of 6 (36.1% of rounds vs 20.7%, p = 0.031). The worked case: a microalgae task whose optimum is a raceway pond at 1.13 g/L while the literature's usual reactor is a photobioreactor — domain-aware sat on photobioreactor 57.5% of rounds and reached the right answer 6.7%; domain-agnostic explored its way there on 84.2%. → telling-the-model-what-the-experiment-means-made-it-learn-slower

  • 2026-08-16 — Two sources, one argument, from opposite ends: what a loop's verdict would have to be, and what happens when you build the best available substitute. Status HELD at emerging; no tier moves.

    (a) The mechanism — why "add a checker model" is not a fix. ding-2026-autonomous-research-agents-verification-gap (filed on the parent map for its count) argues the deciding leg's verification problem structurally rather than empirically. A judge model prefers its own output because it assigns that output lower perplexity, "and lower perplexity is exactly what the generator was trained to produce" — so the quantity the judge rewards and the quantity the generator maximises collapse toward one another, and an agent can improve the judge-facing surface of its work without improving the work. Countermeasures rank: an external or process signal is the only one that recovers real independence; a jury of diverse judges recovers a fraction "bounded by the diversity of the jury," degrading toward zero as models converge; calibration recovers none — "calibration audits the estimator, not the oracle." → calibration-audits-the-estimator-not-the-oracle. This is the vault's correlated-blind-spot rail with the substrate changed — a generator and its same-family judge share a statistical failure mode exactly as two assays share a metabolic one, and an automated system reads the agreement as confirmation either way.

    (b) The instance — someone built the best in-family judge available and reported its reliability. falck-2026-replica-faraday-replication-training trains an AI-scientist agent on paper replication: read a paper with one figure removed, rerun the experiments, regenerate the figure. There is no correct answer to check against, so the reward is an auto-generated per-task rubric graded by a coding agent with ten minutes of access to the attempt's container. The reliability table is the finding: two draws of the rubric judge agree 0.66, two human experts agree 0.30, and the judge agrees with humans 0.19. The judge is more than twice as self-consistent as the experts and less aligned with them than they are with each other — and low noise is precisely what makes it trainable as a reward. → a-judge-consistent-with-itself-and-not-with-experts. Same shape as the-instrument-that-audits-the-benchmarks-is-itself-unreliable, one layer up.

    (c) What the same paper contributes to the loop-move column, kept separate from the judge problem. A 27-billion-parameter model post-trained this way beats Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on 73% of in-distribution machine-learning tasks and 60% of held-out tasks in scientific domains it never trained on — protein and structural biology, climate, materials and chemistry — while directing GPT-5.5 as a tool. What was trained is the outer layer this map is about: deciding what to investigate and scoping an experiment to a budget. Alongside it, an unsought result worth more than the win rate: replication performance falls with the paper's publication year for every agent tested (roughly 2.7 to 8.4 rubric points per decade; the untrained base model degrades fastest). If capability partly reflects how thoroughly work has been absorbed into training data, then benchmark numbers on published science overstate capability on the thing an autonomous lab exists for — work nobody has done yet, which is where a regulatory toxicology loop lives by construction. → replication-skill-transfers-and-decays-with-paper-recency

  • 2026-08-20 — A PAIRED ARM, WHICH THIS MAP HAS NEVER SEEN — AND THE AUTHORS NAME THE CONFOUND THAT STOPS IT BEING A MEASUREMENT. Status HELD at emerging; nothing on this map moves. Ingested sheng-2026-llm-warm-start-closed-loop-synthesis (arXiv 2608.15928, submitted 16 August 2026, not peer reviewed; MIT and Toyota Research Institute, funding_flag: [industry, method-developer] — four of seven authors at an industrial laboratory that builds self-driving-lab infrastructure, reporting that their framework beats a baseline they also ran).

    (a) What is new for this map: somebody ran the control. Six sources on this map argue about how a loop should choose its next experiment. Every previous one reports a campaign and asserts the counterfactual. This one runs two arms of the same closed loop — identical five-parameter space, identical characterisation, identical Bayesian optimiser after the first batch — differing only in where the search starts. Arm A: a language model reads the published literature, distils it into a structured corpus, and proposes the parameter ranges and 12 initial conditions. Arm B: Latin hypercube sampling, a standard space-filling design that uses no prior knowledge, over the wider generic ranges.

    (b) The result, and the part that is genuinely informative. 9 of 36 films from Arm A landed on the Pareto front against 3 of 36 from Arm B — a three-fold difference in best-trade-off yield from initialisation alone (Mann–Whitney U over pooled films, p = 0.0013 phase purity / p = 0.0003 uniformity, though 12 conditions per arm coated in triplicate are treated as 36 independent observations, which inflates the effective n). The advantage did not wash out. After initialisation the two arms were mechanically identical for three further rounds, and Arm A held a higher hypervolume at every matched sample count, ended on a higher best sample, and had a rising success rate while the baseline added no passing samples in its final round. The standard objection to a warm start is that the optimiser catches up; here it did not, over eighteen further experiments.

    (c) ⚠ And then the confound, raised by the authors before anyone else could. "The LLM set both the search-space bounds and the initial conditions … whereas the baseline arm sampled the wider generic spin-coating ranges … the comparison therefore measures the combined effect of narrowing the search space and of choosing points within it." On top: one run per arm, one seed, 18 optimisation samples over 5 parameters, magnitude "cannot be separated from run-to-run variability"; one model, never swapped.     (d) The transferable move, and it sidesteps this map's hardest blocker. This map's standing finding is that a cost-aware selection policy needs a likelihood model — how much does this cheap measurement tell me about which hypothesis is right — and that in toxicology that number does not exist. This campaign never needs one: the language model bounds the search rather than weighing evidence. That is a cheaper form of prior injection and it is available to regulatory toxicology today — a model proposing which concentration range and exposure duration are worth running is not making a hazard call. On Hit@1 out of 16 (chance 6.25%) GPT-5 reaches 0.166 [0.146, 0.186] — above chance at picking the winner, below chance at ordering the field, meaning its signal actively pushes real hypotheses down more often than up. The authors' stated mechanism is the bias they set out to route around: verbal judges "favor conventional ideas … while discarding genuinely novel hypotheses." This is sharper than every prior result on this map because it crosses zero — the others report a language model losing to a better method; this reports the shipped configuration losing to no method at all, and a systematically-inverted selector walks steadily away from the answer while its confidence grows. (b) The rail again, at the opposite pole. Seven models, same 1,323 problems: all seven rank the correct hypothesis first for 167/1,323 (12.6%) under likelihood scoring and 3/1,323 (0.2%) under the raw-logit scoring that produced the best individual results — with the two paradigms comparable in aggregate quality.   • 2026-08-27 — THE MECHANISM UNDER THIS MAP'S RECURRING FINDING, NAMED: THE DEFAULT KERNEL IS MOST CONFIDENT EXACTLY AT A STEP, AND TOXICOLOGY'S ANSWER IS A STEP. Status HELD at emerging; no tier moves. wakabayashi-2026-bayesian-optimization-self-driving-labs (arXiv:2608.26016; NTT, Inc. corporate research, funding_flag: [industry], a review with no primary data) is the most complete account this map has of the machinery that picks the next experiment.

    (a) The finding, and it is a mathematical property rather than an empirical one — stationary-kernels-underestimate-uncertainty-at-thresholds. Bayesian optimization runs on a Gaussian process whose kernel encodes how similar two settings are; the standard RBF and Matérn kernels are stationary, meaning similarity depends only on distance, never on position — the model believes the world is equally smooth everywhere. The review's sentence: a stationary kernel "may cause a model misspecification: it may over-smooth the response, underestimate uncertainty near discontinuities or guide BO toward misleading regions." Unpacked: where the truth has a step, the model does not merely predict the wrong value — it reports high confidence in the wrong value, because smoothness tells it that neighbouring points constrain this one tightly when they are on the far side of a cliff. And the acquisition function is driven by that uncertainty, so the selection policy stops sampling near the boundary precisely because it has been told there is nothing left to learn there. The misspecification suppresses the sampling that would expose it.

    (b) Why it transfers completely, and this is the part that is not a materials-science curiosity. The review's discontinuities are phase boundaries, growth-mode transitions, solubility limits, percolation and degradation thresholds. Toxicology's equivalents are not incidental features of its response surfaces — they are the answer. A point of departure is the location of a boundary. A cytotoxicity threshold is a cliff across which the assay means different things. TG 439's ≤50% viability, the SENS-IS assay's 1.25-fold gene threshold (skin-sensitization-sens-is, coined tonight), the dendritic-cell assay's 150% stimulation index — a regulatory verdict changes discontinuously across every one. An experiment-selection engine miscalibrated at steps is miscalibrated at the only place a toxicology loop cares about.

    (c) What this adds to what the map already held. The 2026-08-20 and 2026-08-25 bullets recorded that calibration did not predict optimisation performance across 15 surrogates and 9 problems, correlation negative on every benchmark. That said the uncertainty estimate is the untrustworthy part. Tonight supplies the mechanism and localises it: unreliable at steps, from an assumption baked into the default kernel, propagating into the policy that consumes it. The map's standing declaration — "the uncertainty estimate the selection rests on is the part that has not been shown to hold" — is unchanged and now has a cause.

    (d) The remedies, with the authors' own verdict on their maturity. Input warping; local, partitioned and change-point Gaussian processes; and — the one that reads most directly onto toxicology — modelling feasibility and failure explicitly as objects rather than as noise in one smooth surface. A plate that dissolved nothing, a concentration above cytotoxicity, a tissue that failed its own quality gate are not noisy observations of the response; a surrogate that averages them in will mislocate the boundary. The review: "although these approaches are well established in the broader GP and BO literature, their application to experimental materials optimization remains limited." Known in theory, thin in materials practice, untried in toxicology.

    (e) A second finding, filed separately — drift-detection-needs-reference-experiments-not-better-models. The review names nonstationarity and experimental drift as its first open challenge ("source materials are gradually depleted, chamber surfaces evolve, catalysts lose activity, precursor batches are replaced, and samples age") and prescribes not a better surrogate but "reference experiments that detect drift and assess whether historical observations can still be reused." That is a control chart, and it costs experiments — a model cannot infer drift from data generated under drift, because the drift is confounded with the effect being learned. Every adopted OECD Test Guideline in this vault already does this under other names: per-run positive and negative controls, and proficiency substances a lab must reproduce before its results count. The autonomous-lab literature is rediscovering the control run. Tonight's other ingest supplies a concrete toxicological instance: the SENS-IS threshold is calibrated to one commercial tissue brand and the draft guideline states it must be re-derived for another.
