Experiment selection — the half of the loop that chooses what to run next — loop-infrastructure map

🚩 AUTO-FORK, 2026-08-12 — CONVENTION DEPARTURE, flagged for human review. Carved out of automation-self-driving-labs, which crossed the fork threshold tonight: 24 dated bullets and, on re-reading, three genuinely separable strands rather than the single argument its declaration had claimed since 2026-08-01. This is the clearest of the three — a small, self-contained set of sources all asking one question that the rest of the parent map does not ask. The parent keeps the other two strands (execution/physical reliability, and verification/measurement) and retains a hub pointer at each carved bullet. Git is the undo.

Status: emerging (2026-08-12). Every source here is computational or simulation-only, none is peer reviewed, and not one of them touches a toxicological endpoint, an assay, or a Test Guideline. evidence_tier: in-silico-mechanistic or single-lab-exploratory throughout. regulatory_status: none.

Why this map exists

Automation of the doing half of a laboratory is a solved-enough problem that the field has moved on. The parent map's clearest statement of it comes from a company that builds these systems: "As execution is automated, the remaining bottleneck moves to experiment selection."

That bottleneck is a different object from everything else the parent map tracks. Robot hands, transparent labware perception, protocol validation, runtime failure detection, benchmark validity — those are about whether a machine can carry out an experiment and whether anyone can tell if it did. This map is about whether the machine picks the right experiment to carry out, which is the leg that makes a loop a loop rather than a very fast pipetting service.

And it is the leg with the most direct structural analogue in regulatory toxicology, which is why it earns a map rather than a footnote: tiered testing is an experiment-selection problem. Run a cheap screen across many chemicals, spend the expensive capacity — an organ-on-chip, a repeat-dose study, an animal test — only where the screen pointed. That is multi-fidelity optimisation, and toxicology has been doing it by committee for thirty years.

What we believe now

Four findings, from four unrelated groups, and they compound into one uncomfortable conclusion.

1. The selection layer consumes the interpretation layer's output, so it cannot be adopted first. A cost-aware selection policy needs, for every cheap diagnostic it might buy, a likelihood model: how much does this measurement tell me about which hypothesis is right? In materials chemistry those are hand-specified from domain knowledge, and robustness to getting them wrong is stated by the authors as "an open question." In toxicology that number does not exist. Nobody has published how much a cheap in-vitro reporter assay tells you about a chronic in-vivo outcome — which is a restatement of the missing decision rule that sits on the majority of this vault's method maps. So the maps stuck at automatable-open-loop are not stuck on one missing piece blocking the back of the loop; the same missing piece blocks the front.

2. Nobody had told the optimiser what things cost. Standard acquisition functions "do not price actions, so a $100 diagnostic and a $5,000 optimization plate cannot be weighed within one budget." Bayesian optimisation — the machinery behind most self-driving-lab results — treats every candidate experiment as equally expensive, and in a real laboratory they differ by orders of magnitude.

3. ⚠ The engine's two most-trusted properties do not do what the field assumes. Benchmarked across fifteen surrogate models and nine problems: uncertainty-driven exploration is not reliably better than plain greedy exploitation (it helped on some benchmarks and roughly doubled the error on others), and how well-calibrated a model's uncertainty is does not predict how good an answer it finds — the correlation ran negative on every benchmark tested. The best-calibrated family was the weakest optimiser group on the chemistry problems.

Why (3) matters beyond the loop. Calibrated uncertainty is close to word-for-word what regulatory guidance on AI models asks for. The property that gets a model accepted and the property that makes it a good engine for the loop are, on this evidence, different properties — see regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility and calibrated-uncertainty-does-not-predict-optimisation-performance. That is not an argument against the requirement; a regulator asking a model to know what it does not know is asking for something reasonable, and "it optimises better" was never the justification. It is an observation that the field has been assuming the two point the same way.

4. ⚠ Added 2026-08-14 — the language model does not beat the classical machinery, and the domain knowledge that was supposed to be its edge is a measured liability. Against GP-UCB (Gaussian-process upper-confidence-bound Bayesian optimisation, textbook since 2010, no language and no domain knowledge), on 45 biology optimisation tasks, no model's confidence interval for "fraction of tasks beaten" excludes the 50% coin flip. And running every task twice — once with the real parameter names, units and subject, once with all meaning stripped — the semantically informed condition matched the published-best design 10.4 percentage points less often by round 30, with the gap entirely in the response to feedback rather than the starting guess, and reversing on 6 of 6 tasks where the literature-typical answer is not the best answer. The sign flips on a control set where the literature consensus is correct, which is what rules out confusion: the knowledge is not being misunderstood, it is being obeyed.

Why (4) compounds (1) rather than replacing it. Finding 1 says the front of the loop is blocked by a missing input. Finding 4 says it is also blocked by an input that hurts — and regulatory toxicology, with thirty years of standard test systems, standard reference chemicals and a validation culture that rewards agreement with precedent, is the most anchor-rich setting either literature has looked at. Both obstacles sit at the front of the loop, and one of them is worse the more expert your model is.

  • 2026-07-21 — The experiment-selection leg arrives in the indicative rather than the subjunctive, and it lands on a dose-response assay. Ingested Chakraborty et al. 2025, hybrid Bayesian optimal experimental design for dose-response assays (bioRxiv 10.1101/2025.09.18.677072, not peer reviewed). A method that picks which concentrations to test next in a dose-response cytotoxicity assay by maximising Expected Information Gain — the Kullback-Leibler divergence from prior to posterior, i.e. how much the experiment is expected to change what you believe. A 6-dose informative design scored EIG 7.64 against 0.24 for an evenly-spaced design — roughly 30× more statistical information per plate. Demonstrated on ER+ breast cancer cells in stromal co-culture. ⚠ Not a NAM and not regulatory toxicology — cancer pharmacology, a statistical method with a human still at the bench, single laboratory. It is here as a transferable instance: dose-response cytotoxicity is the readout of NAMs this vault holds (OECD Test Guidelines 439, 432, 249), and the sweep contract ranks active-learning results "in any domain that could transfer to a tox assay" as high-value. evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory, strength: single-study, funding_flag: none. → experiment-selection-leg-lands-on-a-real-dose-response-assay

  • 2026-08-12 — ⚠⚠ THE DECIDING LEG'S ENGINE GETS BENCHMARKED, AND THE TWO PROPERTIES THE FIELD TRUSTS MOST BOTH FAIL TO DO WHAT IS ASSUMED. Map forked from automation-self-driving-labs tonight; Status emerging; no method anywhere in the vault moves. Ingested lee-2026-transfer-learning-multifidelity-bayesian-optimization (arXiv 2607.23404, submitted 26 July 2026, preprint, not peer reviewed; King's College London, funding_flag: none commercially, with the ordinary method-developer pressure recorded — the authors advocate transfer-learning surrogates and are also the party scoring them). Fifteen surrogate models (four Gaussian-process methods, eleven transfer-learning architectures) across nine benchmarks, 20 seeds, with the acquisition rule, fidelity budget and model size held identical across all fifteen so a difference is attributable to the surrogate rather than the search policy — the study's best design decision. (1) The horse race, which is the least interesting part. Gaussian processes — the default engine of self-driving labs for two decades — win on smooth low-dimensional synthetic functions (final regret 0.07 and 0.05 against 0.43 and 1.24) and lose badly on the real chemistry and materials problems (0.41 against 6.55 on porous framework materials; 0.06 against 0.17 on molecular polarizability; 1.89 against 3.09 on materials band gaps). Regret is how far short of the true best answer the campaign ended; lower is better. The default tool is the worst tool exactly where the search space is a chemical one. (2) ⚠ Exploration is not reliably worth it. Replacing greedy exploitation with five standard uncertainty-driven acquisition rules produced case-dependent results, not uniform improvement. On the porous-framework benchmark greedy beat all five for ten of eleven surrogates and the best of them roughly doubled the regret; the Gaussian process's own regret went 0.88 → 6.13 when its uncertainty was used to drive exploration. On two other chemistry benchmarks the pattern reversed. The authors' summary: greedy is the most robust single default, and exploration must be selected per problem rather than assumed. The entire argument for Bayesian optimisation over sorting a list is the exploration term, and it earns its keep only sometimes. (3) ⚠⚠ Calibration does not predict optimisation performance, and the sign is backwards. Across all fifteen surrogates the correlation between expected calibration error and final regret was negative on every one of the nine benchmarks, from −0.88 to −0.08. The best-calibrated family was the weakest optimiser group on the molecular problems. This is the finding with reach beyond the loop, because calibrated uncertainty is what regulatory AI guidance asks a model for. → calibrated-uncertainty-does-not-predict-optimisation-performance (4) A test for when the machinery is unnecessary at all, and it transfers to tiered testing. Against a surrogate-free baseline that simply ranks by the cheap measurement, two regimes appear. Where the top 1% of cheap candidates and the top 1% of expensive ones coincide (overlap ≈ 1, screening regret ≤ 0.09), every method including the no-model baseline performed identically — the learned surrogate bought nothing. Where they diverge, the surrogate choice swung the outcome by more than an order of magnitude. The deciding quantity is agreement at the TOP of the ranking, not correlation across the range — and a screening assay's job in a tiered strategy is exactly to put the right chemicals at the top of the queue. A screen with excellent overall concordance and poor agreement about which chemicals are worst is a screen that looks validated and misdirects the budget. → a-cheap-screen-earns-its-keep-only-when-it-disagrees-at-the-top (5) Honest ceiling. Not peer reviewed. No physical experiment anywhere in it — every "evaluation" is a lookup into a pre-computed dataset or a synthetic function, two of the nine benchmarks being synthetic functions the authors constructed in favourable and unfavourable variants. No toxicology, no assay, no hazard endpoint, no Test Guideline appears in the paper; the transfer to tiered testing is this vault's inference from a structural identity, labelled as such. And the calibration result is a correlation across fifteen models, not a causal account — the authors themselves frame uncertainty as "a secondary, benchmark-dependent factor."

Status HOLDS at emerging; loop_readiness HOLDS at not-applicable (this map holds a component of the loop, so the axis is what it is measured against rather than a thing the axis grades). (1) It answers this map's FIRST finding by refusing its premise. The map's founding complaint (2026-08-01) was a dependency: a cost-aware selection policy needs a likelihood model — how much does this cheap measurement tell me about which hypothesis is right — hand-specified from domain knowledge in materials chemistry, and non-existent in toxicology. The Model Discovery Agent does not need one up front. A language model proposes candidate mechanisms; sequential Monte Carlo maintains a posterior over which is right; value of information picks the next intervention; the result updates the posterior; and when the truth lies outside every hypothesis on the table, an out-of-sample predictive check notices and the proposer is asked to extend the list. Informativeness is derived per step from the current hypothesis set rather than looked up. The author's summary: "discovery and design reinforce each other." (2) It does NOT answer this map's SECOND finding, and the vault must not let one stand in for the other. The 2026-08-01 complaint was that standard acquisition functions "do not price actions, so a $100 diagnostic and a $5,000 optimization plate cannot be weighed within one budget." Value of information prices information gain — the numerator. The denominator (money, time, animals, reagent) is absent here too. A loop that maximises information per experiment while every experiment costs the same is still not runnable on a real budget. → a-selection-loop-that-builds-the-model-it-selects-against (3) ⚠ THE FINDING THIS VAULT SHOULD KEEP LONGEST, and it is not about experiment selection at all. This is the measured instance of a worry the vault has carried as an argument since 2026-07-27 — that a model can score well on held-out data because it learned the artifact (models-trained-on-hit-calls-learn-the-assay), and that a rule fitted to a corpus will agree with that corpus (quantitative-aop-probabilistic-modelling).   • 2026-08-14 — ⚠⚠ THE PREMISE THIS MAP EXISTS TO TEST GOT TESTED. THE LANGUAGE MODEL DOES NOT BEAT A TWENTY-YEAR-OLD OPTIMISER — AND TELLING IT WHAT THE EXPERIMENT MEANS MADE IT LEARN SLOWER. Status HOLDS at emerging; loop_readiness HOLDS at not-applicable. Ingested zhang-2026-leap-trajectory-llm-iterative-design (LEAPBench, arXiv 2605.15341v1, submitted 14 May 2026, preprint, not peer reviewed; all five authors at Pareto.ai, funding_flag: [industry] with a strong mitigation — the headline is bad news for the product category, and all tasks, oracles, prompts and baselines are released; full text read and archived).

    The setup. 55 sequential design tasks (45 biology, 10 education) built from 2,719 experimental datapoints across 375 published studies. The model proposes parameters, gets one number back, proposes again, for 30 rounds. Scored on best-so-far area under the curve — integrate the running best over the 30 rounds, so arriving early counts. Baseline: GP-UCB, textbook Gaussian-process Bayesian optimisation, no language and no domain knowledge. Eight frontier models, four runs per cell, web search disabled.

    (1) The measurement problem, which lands directly on how this vault reads every number it holds. Existing benchmarks score the final answer at a fixed round count. In a real laboratory each round is a plate, a reagent and a week. Switching to trajectory scoring names a different best model on 24 of 45 tasks (53%), twelve of them by three ranks or more; on 14 of those 24 the two winners land within 1% of each other by round 30 and the trajectory winner got there a median of 8 rounds earlier (round 7 vs 15). Nearly every performance figure in this vault answers "was it right?" and not one answers "how much did it cost to get there?" — and the second is the question that decides whether a loop is worth building. → no-language-model-beat-a-twenty-year-old-optimiser-at-choosing-experiments

    (2) Against a classical baseline, no model clears the coin flip. Best Gemini 3.1 Pro at 64% of tasks beaten [47, 76]; Claude Opus 4.7 at 58% [44, 75] on 36 covered tasks, falling to ≈47% extrapolated over the 9 high-fit biology tasks its safety filter refused outright; worst Llama 3.1 8B at 11% [4, 22]. No confidence interval excludes 50%. Robust to a second Bayesian baseline (HEBO) and a 200-run GP. Honest reading: not shown to beat, not shown to lose — the intervals are wide by construction. But it is not the thing the deployments assume, which is that scientific priors buy fewer experiments. And the refusal count is an operational fact worth carrying into any toxicology loop, where the subject matter is by construction "which chemical does harm and how much."

    (3) ⚠ THE FINDING TO KEEP LONGEST: the domain knowledge is not being misunderstood, it is being obeyed. Every task runs twice — domain-aware (real parameter names, units, categories, the task's subject named) versus domain-agnostic ("optimize a black-box function", parameters renamed X1, X2, C1: A, B — same ranges, all meaning stripped). On the 16 biology tasks where feedback can in principle find the best answer, domain-aware matched the published-best design 10.4 percentage points LESS often at round 30 (p = 0.004), and 7 of 8 models show it. Three controls make it a finding rather than an artifact: round-1 match rates are indistinguishable (+0.01, p = 0.60) while the round-1-to-30 climb is 11.3 points smaller (p = 0.004) — a feedback-responsiveness gap, not a starting-quality one; parameter-space diversity is higher under domain-aware, so it is not under-exploration; and the sign flips on the education control set, where literature consensus generally is correct (73% domain-aware pair-win vs 42% on biology). Narrowed to the 6 tasks where the literature-typical answer differs from the published best, domain-agnostic wins 6 of 6 (36.1% of rounds vs 20.7%, p = 0.031). The worked case: a microalgae task whose optimum is a raceway pond at 1.13 g/L while the literature's usual reactor is a photobioreactor — domain-aware sat on photobioreactor 57.5% of rounds and reached the right answer 6.7%; domain-agnostic explored its way there on 84.2%. → telling-the-model-what-the-experiment-means-made-it-learn-slower

    (4) Why (3) is the sharpest thing this map has recorded about a TOXICOLOGY loop. This map's founding complaint was a missing input: a selection policy needs to know how much a cheap measurement tells you about an expensive outcome, and toxicology has never published that number. Tonight adds a second obstacle at the front of the loop, and it is the opposite — an input that hurts. Regulatory toxicology is the most literature-anchored field this vault touches: standard test systems, standard concentrations, standard reference chemicals, standard thresholds, and a validation culture that explicitly rewards agreement with what was done before. A model primed with all of that is, on this evidence, more likely to reproduce the field's habitual answer and less able to be moved off it by its own data. And the detector does not transfer — the audit works because a published-best datapoint exists to score against, and in regulatory toxicology the correct answer is the object being sought. Second instance in two days of the same shape, after numerically-right-and-mechanistically-wrong-with-a-number-on-it (2026-08-13): a real failure mode of AI-driven science, measured, with a check this field cannot run. Practical, and cheap: running both prompt conditions costs one extra pass, and the obvious deployment choice — give the model everything it knows — is on this evidence a testable liability rather than a free win.

Not peer reviewed; industry-authored; and no experiment is run anywhere — every "outcome" comes from a regression fitted to the same published literature the domain-aware prompt is anchored to (leave-one-out R² median 0.89 biology, 0.32 education). The published-best audit is the authors' own defence against that circularity and is a good one, but the vault's standing brake applies: a rule fitted to a corpus will agree with that corpus (quantitative-aop-probabilistic-modelling).   • 2026-08-16 — Two sources, one argument, from opposite ends: what a loop's verdict would have to be, and what happens when you build the best available substitute. Status HELD at emerging; no tier moves.

    (a) The mechanism — why "add a checker model" is not a fix. ding-2026-autonomous-research-agents-verification-gap (filed on the parent map for its count) argues the deciding leg's verification problem structurally rather than empirically. A judge model prefers its own output because it assigns that output lower perplexity, "and lower perplexity is exactly what the generator was trained to produce" — so the quantity the judge rewards and the quantity the generator maximises collapse toward one another, and an agent can improve the judge-facing surface of its work without improving the work. Countermeasures rank: an external or process signal is the only one that recovers real independence; a jury of diverse judges recovers a fraction "bounded by the diversity of the jury," degrading toward zero as models converge; calibration recovers none — "calibration audits the estimator, not the oracle." → calibration-audits-the-estimator-not-the-oracle. This is the vault's correlated-blind-spot rail with the substrate changed — a generator and its same-family judge share a statistical failure mode exactly as two assays share a metabolic one, and an automated system reads the agreement as confirmation either way.

    (b) The instance — someone built the best in-family judge available and reported its reliability. falck-2026-replica-faraday-replication-training trains an AI-scientist agent on paper replication: read a paper with one figure removed, rerun the experiments, regenerate the figure. There is no correct answer to check against, so the reward is an auto-generated per-task rubric graded by a coding agent with ten minutes of access to the attempt's container. The reliability table is the finding: two draws of the rubric judge agree 0.66, two human experts agree 0.30, and the judge agrees with humans 0.19. The judge is more than twice as self-consistent as the experts and less aligned with them than they are with each other — and low noise is precisely what makes it trainable as a reward. → a-judge-consistent-with-itself-and-not-with-experts. Same shape as the-instrument-that-audits-the-benchmarks-is-itself-unreliable, one layer up.

    (c) What the same paper contributes to the loop-move column, kept separate from the judge problem. A 27-billion-parameter model post-trained this way beats Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on 73% of in-distribution machine-learning tasks and 60% of held-out tasks in scientific domains it never trained on — protein and structural biology, climate, materials and chemistry — while directing GPT-5.5 as a tool. What was trained is the outer layer this map is about: deciding what to investigate and scoping an experiment to a budget. Alongside it, an unsought result worth more than the win rate: replication performance falls with the paper's publication year for every agent tested (roughly 2.7 to 8.4 rubric points per decade; the untrained base model degrades fastest). If capability partly reflects how thoroughly work has been absorbed into training data, then benchmark numbers on published science overstate capability on the thing an autonomous lab exists for — work nobody has done yet, which is where a regulatory toxicology loop lives by construction. → replication-skill-transfers-and-decays-with-paper-recency

    (d) Brakes. Neither source is peer reviewed. Falck et al. is industry-authored (Inherent Laboratories): one organisation built the task space, wrote the judge that defines the score, trained the model, and reports that its model wins on that score — with the mitigations (three-sample judge averaging, a 117-ranking human study, a held-out split, a prompt-optimised baseline) and the residual disclosed, including that the judge "was never validated on imagined tasks." And Faraday "did not have access to any physical lab equipment": every task is code in a container. The held-out AI-for-science split is other people's code, not other people's benches. Agenda drift, nineteenth instance — no assay, no endpoint, no Test Guideline, no regulator.

    (e) Global-ripple: walked every map. No Status and no loop tier moves anywhere. Edges recorded to automation-self-driving-labs (parent; the count and the mechanism live there), ml-model-reporting-reproducibility (the 38% seed-and-trace release rate is a reporting-practice measurement on the AI side of the same accountability problem that map grades on the toxicology side), and regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility (a judge that cannot be shown independent cannot be the credibility evidence a regulator asks for). This map records the same object blocking the front: a selection policy needs to know what a cheap measurement tells you about an expensive outcome, and that is the same missing number. One gap, two positions, and fixing it is a precondition for both. See phenotypic-profiling-cell-painting, oxidative-stress-redox-nrf2-profiling and transcriptomic-point-of-departure as representative instances.

No source on this map has ever been run on any of them, and the vault does not claim otherwise.
