Experiment selection — the half of the loop that chooses what to run next — loop-infrastructure map

🚩 AUTO-FORK, 2026-08-12 — CONVENTION DEPARTURE, flagged for human review. Carved out of automation-self-driving-labs, which crossed the fork threshold tonight: 24 dated bullets and, on re-reading, three genuinely separable strands rather than the single argument its declaration had claimed since 2026-08-01. This is the clearest of the three — a small, self-contained set of sources all asking one question that the rest of the parent map does not ask. The parent keeps the other two strands (execution/physical reliability, and verification/measurement) and retains a hub pointer at each carved bullet. Git is the undo.

Status: emerging (2026-08-12). Every source here is computational or simulation-only, none is peer reviewed, and not one of them touches a toxicological endpoint, an assay, or a Test Guideline. evidence_tier: in-silico-mechanistic or single-lab-exploratory throughout. regulatory_status: none.

Why this map exists

Automation of the doing half of a laboratory is a solved-enough problem that the field has moved on. The parent map's clearest statement of it comes from a company that builds these systems: "As execution is automated, the remaining bottleneck moves to experiment selection."

That bottleneck is a different object from everything else the parent map tracks. Robot hands, transparent labware perception, protocol validation, runtime failure detection, benchmark validity — those are about whether a machine can carry out an experiment and whether anyone can tell if it did. This map is about whether the machine picks the right experiment to carry out, which is the leg that makes a loop a loop rather than a very fast pipetting service.

And it is the leg with the most direct structural analogue in regulatory toxicology, which is why it earns a map rather than a footnote: tiered testing is an experiment-selection problem. Run a cheap screen across many chemicals, spend the expensive capacity — an organ-on-chip, a repeat-dose study, an animal test — only where the screen pointed. That is multi-fidelity optimisation, and toxicology has been doing it by committee for thirty years.

What we believe now

Three findings, from three unrelated groups, and they compound into one uncomfortable conclusion.

1. The selection layer consumes the interpretation layer's output, so it cannot be adopted first. A cost-aware selection policy needs, for every cheap diagnostic it might buy, a likelihood model: how much does this measurement tell me about which hypothesis is right? In materials chemistry those are hand-specified from domain knowledge, and robustness to getting them wrong is stated by the authors as "an open question." In toxicology that number does not exist. Nobody has published how much a cheap in-vitro reporter assay tells you about a chronic in-vivo outcome — which is a restatement of the missing decision rule that sits on the majority of this vault's method maps. So the maps stuck at automatable-open-loop are not stuck on one missing piece blocking the back of the loop; the same missing piece blocks the front.

2. Nobody had told the optimiser what things cost. Standard acquisition functions "do not price actions, so a $100 diagnostic and a $5,000 optimization plate cannot be weighed within one budget." Bayesian optimisation — the machinery behind most self-driving-lab results — treats every candidate experiment as equally expensive, and in a real laboratory they differ by orders of magnitude.

3. ⚠ The engine's two most-trusted properties do not do what the field assumes. Benchmarked across fifteen surrogate models and nine problems: uncertainty-driven exploration is not reliably better than plain greedy exploitation (it helped on some benchmarks and roughly doubled the error on others), and how well-calibrated a model's uncertainty is does not predict how good an answer it finds — the correlation ran negative on every benchmark tested. The best-calibrated family was the weakest optimiser group on the chemistry problems.

Why (3) matters beyond the loop. Calibrated uncertainty is close to word-for-word what regulatory guidance on AI models asks for. The property that gets a model accepted and the property that makes it a good engine for the loop are, on this evidence, different properties — see regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility and calibrated-uncertainty-does-not-predict-optimisation-performance. That is not an argument against the requirement; a regulator asking a model to know what it does not know is asking for something reasonable, and "it optimises better" was never the justification. It is an observation that the field has been assuming the two point the same way.

Through-line

  • 2026-07-21 — The experiment-selection leg arrives in the indicative rather than the subjunctive, and it lands on a dose-response assay. Ingested Chakraborty et al. 2025, hybrid Bayesian optimal experimental design for dose-response assays (bioRxiv 10.1101/2025.09.18.677072, not peer reviewed). A method that picks which concentrations to test next in a dose-response cytotoxicity assay by maximising Expected Information Gain — the Kullback-Leibler divergence from prior to posterior, i.e. how much the experiment is expected to change what you believe. A 6-dose informative design scored EIG 7.64 against 0.24 for an evenly-spaced design — roughly 30× more statistical information per plate. Demonstrated on ER+ breast cancer cells in stromal co-culture. ⚠ Not a NAM and not regulatory toxicology — cancer pharmacology, a statistical method with a human still at the bench, single laboratory. It is here as a transferable instance: dose-response cytotoxicity is the readout of NAMs this vault holds (OECD Test Guidelines 439, 432, 249), and the sweep contract ranks active-learning results "in any domain that could transfer to a tox assay" as high-value. evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory, strength: single-study, funding_flag: none. → experiment-selection-leg-lands-on-a-real-dose-response-assay

  • 2026-08-01 — ⚠️ NOBODY HAD TOLD THE OPTIMISER WHAT THINGS COST — AND THE FIX NEEDS A NUMBER TOXICOLOGY DOES NOT HAVE. Ingested ray-srinivas-2026-coactive-cost-aware-pathway-bo (arXiv 2607.23896; funding_flag: [industry, method-developer] — both authors at Coactive Science, method named after the company). Its framing is the cleanest statement of the bottleneck this map exists for: "As execution is automated, the remaining bottleneck moves to experiment selection." Its specific gap: "standard acquisition functions do not price actions, so a $100 diagnostic and a $5,000 optimization plate cannot be weighed within one budget." The proposal: buy cheap diagnostics chosen to discriminate among candidate approaches per dollar, then commit the optimisation budget to the survivor. The evidence is simulation only — author-designed synthetic response surfaces, evidence_tier: in-silico-mechanistic, no wet-lab experiment, "absolute numbers are benchmark results, not laboratory claims." Mean cost-to-target over 20 seeds: $4,985 against $24,090 on the case constructed so the obvious first guess is wrong (~5×), and near-zero or negative benefit on the two cases not so constructed. The value is insurance against a wrong first commitment, on an instance the authors call hypothetical. Credited for putting "synthetic" in the abstract and volunteering five limitations including that its cited theory does not cover its implemented algorithm. ⚠ And the transfer stops on this vault's own blocker: the policy needs a likelihood model per cheap diagnostic, hand-specified here from domain knowledge, and in toxicology that number does not exist. The selection layer consumes the interpretation layer's output; it cannot be adopted first and the decision rule solved later. → experiment-selection-under-known-cost-is-the-loops-other-half

  • 2026-08-12 — ⚠⚠ THE DECIDING LEG'S ENGINE GETS BENCHMARKED, AND THE TWO PROPERTIES THE FIELD TRUSTS MOST BOTH FAIL TO DO WHAT IS ASSUMED. Map forked from automation-self-driving-labs tonight; Status emerging; no method anywhere in the vault moves. Ingested lee-2026-transfer-learning-multifidelity-bayesian-optimization (arXiv 2607.23404, submitted 26 July 2026, preprint, not peer reviewed; King's College London, funding_flag: none commercially, with the ordinary method-developer pressure recorded — the authors advocate transfer-learning surrogates and are also the party scoring them). Fifteen surrogate models (four Gaussian-process methods, eleven transfer-learning architectures) across nine benchmarks, 20 seeds, with the acquisition rule, fidelity budget and model size held identical across all fifteen so a difference is attributable to the surrogate rather than the search policy — the study's best design decision. (1) The horse race, which is the least interesting part. Gaussian processes — the default engine of self-driving labs for two decades — win on smooth low-dimensional synthetic functions (final regret 0.07 and 0.05 against 0.43 and 1.24) and lose badly on the real chemistry and materials problems (0.41 against 6.55 on porous framework materials; 0.06 against 0.17 on molecular polarizability; 1.89 against 3.09 on materials band gaps). Regret is how far short of the true best answer the campaign ended; lower is better. The default tool is the worst tool exactly where the search space is a chemical one. (2) ⚠ Exploration is not reliably worth it. Replacing greedy exploitation with five standard uncertainty-driven acquisition rules produced case-dependent results, not uniform improvement. On the porous-framework benchmark greedy beat all five for ten of eleven surrogates and the best of them roughly doubled the regret; the Gaussian process's own regret went 0.88 → 6.13 when its uncertainty was used to drive exploration. On two other chemistry benchmarks the pattern reversed. The authors' summary: greedy is the most robust single default, and exploration must be selected per problem rather than assumed. The entire argument for Bayesian optimisation over sorting a list is the exploration term, and it earns its keep only sometimes. (3) ⚠⚠ Calibration does not predict optimisation performance, and the sign is backwards. Across all fifteen surrogates the correlation between expected calibration error and final regret was negative on every one of the nine benchmarks, from −0.88 to −0.08. The best-calibrated family was the weakest optimiser group on the molecular problems. This is the finding with reach beyond the loop, because calibrated uncertainty is what regulatory AI guidance asks a model for. → calibrated-uncertainty-does-not-predict-optimisation-performance (4) A test for when the machinery is unnecessary at all, and it transfers to tiered testing. Against a surrogate-free baseline that simply ranks by the cheap measurement, two regimes appear. Where the top 1% of cheap candidates and the top 1% of expensive ones coincide (overlap ≈ 1, screening regret ≤ 0.09), every method including the no-model baseline performed identically — the learned surrogate bought nothing. Where they diverge, the surrogate choice swung the outcome by more than an order of magnitude. The deciding quantity is agreement at the TOP of the ranking, not correlation across the range — and a screening assay's job in a tiered strategy is exactly to put the right chemicals at the top of the queue. A screen with excellent overall concordance and poor agreement about which chemicals are worst is a screen that looks validated and misdirects the budget. → a-cheap-screen-earns-its-keep-only-when-it-disagrees-at-the-top (5) Honest ceiling. Not peer reviewed. No physical experiment anywhere in it — every "evaluation" is a lookup into a pre-computed dataset or a synthetic function, two of the nine benchmarks being synthetic functions the authors constructed in favourable and unfavourable variants. No toxicology, no assay, no hazard endpoint, no Test Guideline appears in the paper; the transfer to tiered testing is this vault's inference from a structural identity, labelled as such. And the calibration result is a correlation across fifteen models, not a causal account — the authors themselves frame uncertainty as "a secondary, benchmark-dependent factor."

  • 2026-08-13 — ⚠⚠ THE CHOOSER BUILDS THE MODEL IT CHOOSES AGAINST — AND THE FAILURE IT EXPOSES HAS A NUMBER ON IT AND A DETECTOR TOXICOLOGY CANNOT RUN. Ingested murphy-2026-model-discovery-agent-voi-experiment-design (arXiv 2608.09696v2, not peer reviewed, single author, Univ. British Columbia; funding_flag: none; full text read and archived — the only source in tonight's run whose bytes were obtainable). Status HOLDS at emerging; loop_readiness HOLDS at not-applicable (this map holds a component of the loop, so the axis is what it is measured against rather than a thing the axis grades). (1) It answers this map's FIRST finding by refusing its premise. The map's founding complaint (2026-08-01) was a dependency: a cost-aware selection policy needs a likelihood model — how much does this cheap measurement tell me about which hypothesis is right — hand-specified from domain knowledge in materials chemistry, and non-existent in toxicology. The Model Discovery Agent does not need one up front. A language model proposes candidate mechanisms; sequential Monte Carlo maintains a posterior over which is right; value of information picks the next intervention; the result updates the posterior; and when the truth lies outside every hypothesis on the table, an out-of-sample predictive check notices and the proposer is asked to extend the list. Informativeness is derived per step from the current hypothesis set rather than looked up. The author's summary: "discovery and design reinforce each other." (2) It does NOT answer this map's SECOND finding, and the vault must not let one stand in for the other. The 2026-08-01 complaint was that standard acquisition functions "do not price actions, so a $100 diagnostic and a $5,000 optimization plate cannot be weighed within one budget." Value of information prices information gain — the numerator. The denominator (money, time, animals, reagent) is absent here too. A loop that maximises information per experiment while every experiment costs the same is still not runnable on a real budget. → a-selection-loop-that-builds-the-model-it-selects-against (3) ⚠ THE FINDING THIS VAULT SHOULD KEEP LONGEST, and it is not about experiment selection at all. On a 57-world enzyme-kinetics benchmark, the previous best system produces, on the hard tier, laws that are "numerically accurate but mechanistically meaningless" — the worked example fits held-out data at root-mean-squared log-error 0.001, inside even the strict pass threshold, and a symbolic-equivalence check marks it wrong. Head to head at a 60-experiment budget: the right mechanism recovered 56% of the time against 42%; a numerically usable answer 81% against 69%, winning on every difficulty tier. This is the measured instance of a worry the vault has carried as an argument since 2026-07-27 — that a model can score well on held-out data because it learned the artifact (models-trained-on-hit-calls-learn-the-assay), and that a rule fitted to a corpus will agree with that corpus (quantitative-aop-probabilistic-modelling). And the detector does not transfer: the gap was visible only "because all datasets are synthetic and the true model is known." In regulatory toxicology the true mechanism is the object being sought. A discovery loop pointed at a toxicological mechanism would behave exactly this way and nothing would fire. It supplies the vocabulary the vault lacked — exact accuracy (does it predict?) versus symbolic accuracy (is it the right mechanism?). Nearly every performance figure in this vault is the first kind. Not one is the second. → numerically-right-and-mechanistically-wrong-with-a-number-on-it (4) Honest ceiling, and the third item is the one that bites. Not peer reviewed, eight days old, single author. Zero physical experiments — no chemical, no plate, no robot, no assay, no toxicological endpoint; the nearest contact is a simulated neuron with simulated channel-blocking drugs. The author names the regime gap himself: of the stochastic biology setting, "This is the regime real experiments occupy, and the one setting our other benchmarks (deterministic ODEs + observation noise)." And the design space is a menu somebody already wrote — a fixed 13-launch menu in physics, chosen in advance by a language model; the nine elementary kinetic mechanisms supplied to the proposer as vocabulary in chemistry, with the contribution explicitly "identifying which multiplicative composition the data support, not blind symbol search." Menu construction is most of the problem in toxicology — see mixture-toxicity-in-vitro-nams, the vault's only map blocked at the front of the loop precisely because no rule selects which co-exposures to put on the plate. The head-to-head is also not fully like-for-like: the baseline row uses published numbers from a different, unreleased task subset and a weaker language model. (5) Global-ripple: walked every map. No status move anywhere, and one edge refused.
