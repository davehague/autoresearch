What REACH adaptations are

REACH is the EU's main chemicals law (Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals, EC No. 1907/2006). Sell a chemical in Europe above certain tonnage bands and REACH specifies the information you must supply; historically much of that information came from an animal study.

REACH already contains its own escape hatches. Annex XI permits adaptations to the standard information requirements — data sharing between registrants, read-across from a structurally similar chemical, and the general principle that testing happens only "when scientifically necessary." The instrument for reducing animal testing under REACH therefore already exists in law; the question this map tracks is whether it is used.

What we believe now

The Animal-Free Safety Assessment (AFSA) Collaboration — nineteen authors, twelve of them at companies that register chemicals under REACH — argues that the flexibility is real on paper and absent in practice:

"REACH already provides a framework to promote non-animal testing (Annex XI). Nevertheless, the challenge lies in translating its flexibility into consistent practice. New in vivo (vertebrates) studies are still requested to meet information requirements in cases where these tests may add limited or no regulatory or scientific value."

Their proposal is a set of objective criteria for identifying "DIM" testing — difficult, impossible, or meaningless — applied to screen real REACH registration dossiers for cases where a required vertebrate study should not be run. The three words hide three unlike failures, and the vault keeps them apart because they have different fixes:

What failsFix
Difficultthe substance fights the protocol — insoluble, unstable, volatile, corrosive at the required dosebetter protocol, or a different method
Impossiblethe study cannot physically be run on this substancea different method, or nothing
Meaninglessthe study runs perfectly and its number does not bear on the decisionnothing to fix — don't run it

The third is the one this map exists for, because it is an argument about information rather than about method availability, and it therefore does not require a replacement method to exist.

Why that is a third argument, not a variant of the first

Almost the entire vault is one argument for dropping an animal study: a non-animal method predicts what it would have said. One corner holds a second: nobody is exposed to enough of it for the answer to matter (risk-assessment-ttc, the threshold of toxicological concern — the field's one genuinely agreed numeric rule, and it is a rule for not testing).

This is the third: the study would have produced a number that means nothing. It is available precisely where the vault's coverage map is blank, which is most of the systemic endpoints.

The tension this map must carry, not resolve

  • The argument may be entirely correct. A required study on a substance that cannot be dosed accurately produces a number nobody should rely on, and running it anyway is animal use for a filing rather than for knowledge. Nothing here is obviously wrong.
  • The authorship is the people who pay for the tests. Twelve of nineteen authors work for registrants; two for an animal-protection organisation; one for an in-silico contract laboratory. The competing-interest statement declares none. The vault records that declaration and does not treat it as settling the question — the criteria are what would have to be independently checked, and no independent body has.
  • The failure mode is silent by construction. A wrong hazard call produces a wrong number that a later measurement can contradict. A wrong "meaningless" call produces no data at all, so nothing downstream can catch it. This is the same structural weakness named for automated systems in the-audit-unit-is-the-claim-not-the-call: what a system discarded is the hardest thing to audit and the least likely to be recorded.
