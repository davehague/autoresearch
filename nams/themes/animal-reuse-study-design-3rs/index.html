Why this map exists, and why it is not folded into an existing one

Every other map in this vault grades whether a non-animal method could replace or automate an assay. This paper's entire proposal sidesteps that question: it reduces animal numbers by reusing the same animals across studies rather than inventing a replacement method, and explicitly frames itself as Reduction, not Replacement. Filing it under an endpoint map would misrepresent it as a hazard-detection method; it is a scheduling and husbandry practice.

Its nearest structural sibling is study-design-virtual-control-groups — both operate on study structure (how the study is assembled) rather than on what is measured, and both leave a live, dosed animal at the centre of the study. They are complementary levers under the same Reduction umbrella, not duplicates: one deletes the separate control cohort (by generating or borrowing its data), the other reuses whole animals, cohort to cohort.

What we believe now

The default is naive animals, and the authors argue that default is often unexamined. Most nonclinical safety studies enrol previously-unused ("naive") animals to avoid confounding from prior dosing. Reuse is already standard practice in pharmacokinetic (PK) and safety-pharmacology studies but rare in toxicology proper. Reused animals skip transport, quarantine and acclimation stress and can retain trained behaviour and stable social hierarchies — most consequential for non-human primates, whose training investment is large and whose supply is constrained.

The challenges are named plainly, not glossed over. Cumulative lifetime burden across repeated studies; adequate washout/recovery between dosing periods (species-specific: 2–3 weeks for cynomolgus macaques, 1–2 weeks for dogs and minipigs in the paper's tables); immunogenicity (anti-drug antibodies against biologics, and pre-existing immunity to gene therapies that can blunt or confound a later study's readout); and rising rates of age-related background pathology in animals used repeatedly over a longer lifespan.

A three-tier decision framework. Tier 1 — reuse already standard (PK, safety pharmacology, some CRO colony practices). Tier 2 — case-by-case scientific/ethical justification needed. Tier 3 — reuse difficult or inappropriate for the study type, though enrolling non-naive animals at a new study's start may still be workable. Operational limits given by species: dogs have a reported ~2-year utilization limit at some contract research organisations; minipigs can gain up to 2 kg/month, which bears on dosing and housing; some non-human primates can live up to 30 years, which widens the reuse window but lengthens the cumulative-burden question correspondingly.

No quantitative reduction estimate is reported. The paper argues the case and proposes the framework; it does not measure how many animals a given adoption rate would actually save.
