Why this map exists

The vault has spent 45 sources asking "could a machine run this assay?" — and had never ingested a single source about the machines. stages/01-sweep says the crossover between the two literatures is the highest-value find available. This is the first source that sits in both.

And it crosses in the unexpected direction. It is not an AI researcher discovering toxicology. It is Thomas Hartung — former Head of ECVAM, the EU's method-validation body, now Director of Johns Hopkins' Center for Alternatives to Animal Testing — writing about agentic AI, in a venue for AI researchers.

What we believe now

Self-driving labs work. In chemistry.

A self-driving lab (SDL) is the closed loop this vault's question names: a model proposes an experiment, robots run it, results return, the model updates, and it picks the next one — autonomy in the loop, not in any single step.

Chemistry and materials — loops that ran and produced things:

  • ChemCrow — "a fully autonomous synthesis of twenty-nine organosilicon compounds, eight of which were previously unknown."
  • A-Lab — autonomous solid-state synthesis of inorganic powders.

Life sciences — architectures, in the subjunctive:

  • Organ-on-chip + high-content imaging + deep-learning analytics.
  • iPSC-derived organoid concentration–response feeding a Bayesian optimiser that picks the next dosing schedule.

Read the grammar. The chemistry entries are things that happened. The biology entries are things that could be assembled. No closed loop on a regulatory toxicology assay is cited — because there isn't one.

The vault's central question is genuinely open. It is not a question the literature answered somewhere the vault hadn't looked. Until tonight the vault was asserting that; now it is sourced — and sourced from the person with the strongest incentive to claim otherwise, who does not.

The 5% — the number to keep

DeepMind's GNoME proposed 380,000 stable crystal structures. Fewer than 5% have been independently experimentally validated.

That is the field's own measurement of the gap between what a generative loop proposes and what the world confirms — from the domain where SDLs work best, where ground truth is a crystal you can grow or not. Toxicology's ground truth is human harm, and it is enormously harder to check.

A loop that proposes confidently and validates rarely is not a discovery engine — it is a hypothesis firehose. In materials, the cost of a wrong confident answer is a crystal that fails to grow. In toxicology it is a chemical called safe.

The bottlenecks — and they are not the robots

  • Data, not hardware: "many 'FAIR' datasets are discoverable only through bespoke portals or lack harmonized ontologies." The loop's constraint is the input contract.
  • Amplification: "opaque AI pipelines risk magnifying the [reproducibility] problem." Automation does not average out a field's errors — it scales them.
  • Agenda drift (the one he takes most seriously): "research agendas may gradually shift from curiosity-driven exploration toward problems that are computationally convenient."
  • Regulatory friction: "AI can shave years off pre-clinical optimization, but agencies still require explainability dossiers that reconcile black-box embeddings with pharmacological intuition."
