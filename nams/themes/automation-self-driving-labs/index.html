Why this map exists

The vault has spent 45 sources asking "could a machine run this assay?" — and had never ingested a single source about the machines. stages/01-sweep says the crossover between the two literatures is the highest-value find available. This is the first source that sits in both.

And it crosses in the unexpected direction. It is not an AI researcher discovering toxicology. It is Thomas Hartung — former Head of ECVAM, the EU's method-validation body, now Director of Johns Hopkins' Center for Alternatives to Animal Testing — writing about agentic AI, in a venue for AI researchers.

What we believe now

Self-driving labs work. In chemistry.

A self-driving lab (SDL) is the closed loop this vault's question names: a model proposes an experiment, robots run it, results return, the model updates, and it picks the next one — autonomy in the loop, not in any single step.

Chemistry and materials — loops that ran and produced things:

  • ChemCrow — "a fully autonomous synthesis of twenty-nine organosilicon compounds, eight of which were previously unknown."
  • A-Lab — autonomous solid-state synthesis of inorganic powders.

Life sciences — architectures, in the subjunctive:

  • Organ-on-chip + high-content imaging + deep-learning analytics.
  • iPSC-derived organoid concentration–response feeding a Bayesian optimiser that picks the next dosing schedule.

Read the grammar. The chemistry entries are things that happened. The biology entries are things that could be assembled. No closed loop on a regulatory toxicology assay is cited — because there isn't one.

The vault's central question is genuinely open. It is not a question the literature answered somewhere the vault hadn't looked. Until tonight the vault was asserting that; now it is sourced — and sourced from the person with the strongest incentive to claim otherwise, who does not.

The 5% — the number to keep

DeepMind's GNoME proposed 380,000 stable crystal structures. Fewer than 5% have been independently experimentally validated.

That is the field's own measurement of the gap between what a generative loop proposes and what the world confirms — from the domain where SDLs work best, where ground truth is a crystal you can grow or not. Toxicology's ground truth is human harm, and it is enormously harder to check.

A loop that proposes confidently and validates rarely is not a discovery engine — it is a hypothesis firehose. In materials, the cost of a wrong confident answer is a crystal that fails to grow. In toxicology it is a chemical called safe.

The bottlenecks — and they are not the robots

  • Data, not hardware: "many 'FAIR' datasets are discoverable only through bespoke portals or lack harmonized ontologies." The loop's constraint is the input contract.
  • Amplification: "opaque AI pipelines risk magnifying the [reproducibility] problem." Automation does not average out a field's errors — it scales them.
  • Agenda drift (the one he takes most seriously): "research agendas may gradually shift from curiosity-driven exploration toward problems that are computationally convenient."
  • Regulatory friction: "AI can shave years off pre-clinical optimization, but agencies still require explainability dossiers that reconcile black-box embeddings with pharmacological intuition."

Cross-intervention / connect (§6.5)

  • ⚠️ Agenda drift is a prediction about THIS VAULT'S OWN TABLE, and it should be read as one. An active-learning loop maximises information gain over the space it can reach; it cannot value an experiment it cannot run. So as loop infrastructure matures, effort flows to what is already automatable and away from everything else. The vault's 19 closed-loop-ready maps gain; the 12 automatable-open-loop gain if someone writes a decision rule (cheap). And the bottom of the table loses: genotoxicity-in-vitro-micronucleus (a human at a microscope), carcinogenicity-cell-transformation-assay, developmental-toxicity-zebrafish-teratogenicity, immunotoxicity-myelotoxicity-cfu-gm (its model consumes a mouse MTD), and reproductive-fertility-multigenerational-nams — the endpoint with no non-animal method at all. The endpoints that most need a NAM are the ones a self-driving lab has least reason to build one for. Add tonight's endocrine-thyroid-disruption-nams (67 methods, zero validated, dose an animal) and the list of neglected-by-construction endpoints grows rather than shrinks. This is the strongest argument in the vault for keeping a human at the PORTFOLIO level even when the assay level is fully automated: the loop can pick the next chemical; it must not be the thing that picks the next endpoint.

The step from Robin to a closed tox loop is not just an engineering step; it is the step that flips the regulatory cost from "reducible" to "maximal." The vault predicted this pricing on 2026-07-13; Robin is the first system whose own design choice (keep humans executing) sits on the cheap side of it.

Added 2026-07-18 (STP agentic-AI roundtable)

  • ⚠️ Convergent design onto the cheap side of the regulatory price — two systems now, and neither says it meant to. regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility records FDA's model risk = model influence × decision consequence, where model influence is how alone the model is in the decision, and FDA's own worked example says a human cross-check "would reduce the AI model influence." Robin kept humans in execution. This white paper's target architecture keeps a pathologist in the call ("pathologist-in-the-loop report generation"). Two independent design efforts, in different fields, selecting the configuration that minimises regulatory burden. Flagged as a vault inference about convergent design — neither source cites the pricing rule as a reason, and the simpler explanation (professionals designing a system they would themselves be willing to sign) is at least as likely. But the pattern the vault predicted on 2026-07-13 now has two instances, and zero counter-instances.
  • ⚠️ Agenda drift, and this time the drift points AT THE ANIMALS — the map's own warning, inverted. The agenda-drift finding says effort flows toward what is computationally convenient, starving the endpoints hardest to automate. This source is a case the finding did not anticipate: the automatable thing turned out to be a step inside the animal workflow. Toxicologic pathology is expert-judgment-heavy, image-based, and sits on a mountain of existing labelled slides — a near-ideal AI target. It is also a step that exists because an animal study happened. So the first place agentic AI gets serious traction in toxicology may be the place that makes animal studies more efficient rather than less necessary. The vault must not let "agentic AI in toxicology" read as progress on its own question. Stated as a caution, not a prediction.
  • Limited by ml-model-reporting-reproducibility (tonight's new model-layer map). This paper calls for "shared standards, benchmarks, and governance frameworks" before deployment. Kappenberg et al. measured what the field currently produces without them: interpretation methods reported in about half of toxicology ML papers, with data and code availability substantially underreported. The white paper names the gap; the audit sizes it. Two 2026 sources, different instruments, same missing artefact.
  • Composes-with genotoxicity-in-vitro-micronucleus, carcinogenicity-cell-transformation-assay and genotoxicity-buccal-micronucleus-cytome — the eye-scoring problem is the same problem. A pathologist reading a tissue slide and a technician counting micronuclei down a microscope are the identical human-in-the-loop blocker at different scales. That the profession is now organising around automating its own version of it is the strongest signal in the vault that this blocker is being worked on as a field-level project, not just demonstrated in one-off papers. No tier moves on a signal.
