Why this is its own map

The developmental-neurotoxicity in-vitro battery is 17 assays across 8 stages of brain development. Roughly 650 chemicals have been through it. Not one of those runs produced a stand-alone regulatory conclusion, and the reason has never been the assays — it is what happens to their numbers afterwards.

Since 2026-07-26 the vault has accumulated four separate findings about that afterwards, from four different directions, and they do not fit as bullets under a battery map:

  1. The arithmetic before the rule is itself unsettled. A 32-author consensus paper including the OECD Secretariat, EFSA, the US FDA, NICEATM and BfR drew its worked example from this battery: "variations in curve fitting models can yield different results and impact subsequent level V data." Five different biostatistical procedures, five different final test results. The known blocker is at the interpretation step; this says there are outcome-changing choices several steps earlier.
  2. The scorer has its own applicability domain. Six of seven readouts on the battery's flagship human-cell assay are scored by convolutional neural networks. A trained scorer's competence is defined by its training-image statistics, and domain shift is the named failure mode. Automated scoring is real and it is not settled.
  3. The final step consumes a second model and then a judgement. The worked case of 2026-07-30 traced the whole path: seventeen assays returned numbers, one was non-zero, a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model said that concentration is unreachable in a living body, and a person decided the gap was big enough. No published procedure states how big is big enough.
  4. The integration is now fully computable, and still stops. Tonight's addition — see below.

What we believe now

The chain from plate to number is automated along its entire length. As of tonight it has three named, deterministic, published components at the integration step:

  • Benchmark concentration — fit a curve to each assay's dose–response, find where the response departs from control.
  • Pareto multi-objective optimisation — keep the chemicals no other chemical beats on both potency and selectivity.
  • ToxPi — score each chemical as a weighted pie of assay slices, and rank.

All three are software. Nobody has to look at a well. Across 95 chemicals — drugs, flame retardants, fungicides, herbicides, industrial chemicals including PFAS, insecticides, plus negative controls — this pipeline produced a rank-ordered priority list, with fungicides, drugs and insecticides ranking highest.

And a rank order is not a hazard call. It answers look at this one first; it does not answer this chemical is a developmental neurotoxicant. The second cannot be derived from the first without a threshold, and there is no published threshold and no published performance figure that would let anyone pick one.

Loop readiness

loop_readiness: automatable-open-loop — inherited from the parent and, as of tonight, held for a much more precisely located reason.

The gap is now narrow enough to write as a specification: a published rule mapping a ToxPi score or Pareto rank to a hazard classification, at a stated threshold, with stated performance against reference chemicals. That is one paper's worth of work, and it is the paper nobody writes — because publishing the threshold is what makes you wrong in public.

The pattern this map exists to hold. Four independent groups in eight days automated the arithmetic for four different endpoints and none of them wrote down the cut:

A threshold applied fast and confidently, to a battery with roughly 60% specificity (about two in five clean chemicals wrongly flagged), whose selectivity varies by chemical class, and which is blind to named mechanisms it cannot detect in advance, is a worse object than a slow human reading the same numbers. loop_readiness has never meant endorsement in this vault and it means it least here.
