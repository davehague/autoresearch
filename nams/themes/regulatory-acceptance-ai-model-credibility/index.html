The AI-acceptance gate — FDA's model-risk matrix and the EMA/FDA principles — framework map

What we believe now

This map answers the question regulatory-acceptance-fda-nam-framework opened and could not close. That map found that CDER names AI models as a NAM category and will accept a fit-for-purpose method with no Test Guideline and no validation. This map is how the AI gets judged once it walks through that door — and the answer is a two-factor risk matrix that prices autonomy directly.

The finding: model risk = model influence × decision consequence

"Model risk is a combination of two factors: (a) model influence, which is the contribution of the evidence derived from the AI model relative to other contributing evidence used to inform the question of interest and (b) decision consequence … The ratings … are independently determined." — FDA draft guidance, January 2025

Model influence does not measure how good the model is. It measures how alone it is in the decision.

A closed loop maximises model influence by construction — a loop is the configuration in which the model is the sole determinant. Decision consequence is fixed by the question being asked, not by the architecture. So the only multiplicand autonomy touches, it pushes all the way up — and everything downstream (stringency of credibility assessment, performance acceptance criteria, documentation) is required to be "commensurate with the AI model risk."

FDA's own worked examples state the mechanism in both directions. Model influence is "high" when "the AI model will be the sole determinant" (clinical example → risk HIGH). And in the manufacturing example, a human independently verifying a sample of each batch means "Measuring fill volume through release testing would reduce the AI model influence" → risk drops to MEDIUM despite an unchanged, high decision consequence.

Under the regulator's own arithmetic, the cheapest way to reduce your evidentiary burden is not to build a better model. It is to make the model matter less.

It is not one draft's quirk — EMA signed the same machinery

The EMA/FDA Guiding Principles of Good AI Practice (January 2026) import it wholesale. Principle #2 requires "proportionate validation, risk mitigation, and oversight based on the context of use and determined model risk." Principle #1 is "Human-centric by design." Principle #8 defines the object of performance assessment as "the complete system including human–AI interactions" — the regulators' unit of evaluation is a human–AI system, not an autonomous model. Principle #9 requires scheduled monitoring for data drift.

And this is a NAMs question, in the regulators' own words

  • FDA's guidance lists, first among AI uses supporting regulatory decisions: "reducing the number of animal-based pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and toxicologic studies."
  • The EMA/FDA principles' opening rationale: AI "decreases reliance on animal testing by improving the prediction of toxicity and efficacy in humans."

The AI-acceptance question and the NAM-acceptance question are the same question, at the same agencies, in the same eighteen months. This vault's two framework maps are two halves of one gate.

Loop readiness

loop_readiness: not-applicable — not a method, so it carries no tier. But it is the map that puts a price on the axis: loop_readiness measures whether a machine could make the call; model influence measures what it costs you when it does. The vault has been climbing its third axis like a ladder with 19 maps at the top. The regulator reads that same ladder as a risk gradient, and the vault has been tracking the capability while ignoring the invoice.

The tension this map must carry, not resolve

  • Nothing here forbids a closed loop. FDA's framework is a proportionality rule, not a bar — high risk means more credibility evidence, not none permitted. "Human-centric" is not "human-operated." A well-instrumented loop with full provenance, monitored drift, a characterised applicability domain and an orthogonal control could in principle clear a high-risk bar that a sloppy human-in-the-loop system would fail — and would satisfy principles #6 and #9 better than a human eyeballing plates ever did.
  • But the burden lands on the builder, under rules drafted with a human–AI centaur in mind. The friction is real and it is asymmetric.
  • And the honest caveat on this vault's own reading: FDA wrote this for AI models as evidence-producers in a dossier, not for AI agents running assays. Reading it as a verdict on autonomous labs is an extrapolation by this vault, flagged as one. What makes it worth making: the matrix keys on how alone the model is — the one property a loop cannot reduce without ceasing to be a loop.
