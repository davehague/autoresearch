What we believe now

Lipidomics is measuring the fat molecules in a sample. A cell contains hundreds of chemically distinct lipids — membrane builders, storage fats, signalling molecules — and which ones shift tells you something about what went wrong rather than merely that something did. Targeted lipidomics measures a pre-chosen list rather than cataloguing everything, which is what lets it run at plate scale.

The workflow: 148 lipid targets across 3 categories and 11 classes, measured by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry in multiple-reaction-monitoring mode — separate the molecules on a column, weigh them, smash them, weigh the pieces, and watch only for the specific signature of each molecule on the list. Applied to primary hepatocytes (real liver cells, not an immortalised line) from human, rat, monkey and dog in 96-well plates, dosed from culture day 4 to 8 with chlorpromazine (1–30 μM), bosentan (1–200 μM) and fialuridine (1–100 μM).

What it produced. Chlorpromazine gave the largest lipid changes in human, monkey and rat cells, with a human dose-dependent trend to 10 μM, and minimal response in dog. Bosentan moved lipids dose-dependently across all four species, strongest in human. Fialuridine barely registered. And overall, the lipid effects "closely aligned with conventional toxicity markers."

That last clause is the map's founding tension. Agreement with the cheap measurement is the right first sanity check and it means the signal is real biology rather than instrument artefact. It is also the open question: a measurement that agrees with a cheaper measurement has not yet earned a place in a decision procedure. Adding this to a screening loop costs a mass spectrometer, a column, a sample-prep step and run time per plate, and no case is shown where those costs bought a different call. → a-richer-liver-readout-that-agrees-with-the-cheap-one

The design worth more than the result

Four species, one identical protocol, same plates. Animal safety testing rests on the assumption that a rat or a dog predicts a human, and that assumption is almost never measured directly — no in-vivo programme can put four species through the same exposure. This design does, for the price of extra wells. On the study's strongest compound one of the four species disagreed with the other three.

One compound, one laboratory, no concordance statistic: this names a question rather than settling one. But it is the kind of question a closed loop structurally cannot ask, because a loop fixes its species on day one and then holds it constant forever — the same blind spot the vault recorded for cell-line identity (2026-08-12) and for cross-laboratory generalisability (2026-07-30). → four-species-in-one-protocol-and-the-dog-disagreed
