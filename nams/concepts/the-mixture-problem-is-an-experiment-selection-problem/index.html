Every other blocker in this vault is about reading the answer. This one is about choosing the question.

Eighty method maps, and every one of them grades a method that tests one chemical at a time. Real exposure is not one chemical at a time. This is the vault's first source about that, and the reason it earns its own map is not the endpoint — it is the shape of the blocker, which is new.

The two ends of a loop

A closed loop has two halves. The back half reads the result and decides what it means. The front half decides what to run next.

Nearly everything in this vault is a back-half problem. Twenty-plus maps say no agreed decision rule: the plate reader returns a clean number and nobody published what the number licenses. The fixes discussed — committee thresholds, quantitative adverse outcome pathways, benchmark comparisons — are all back-half fixes.

Mixtures are stuck at the front. Before you can ask what a result means, you have to have run something, and the number of candidate somethings is combinatorial. Two hundred chemicals in a plausible exposure scenario give you more pairs than the world has plates. The authors state it directly: comprehensive testing "is not feasible in practice."

And it is worse than a scale problem, because the thing to be selected is not even well defined. Real co-exposures are "incompletely characterized, chemically variable, and compositionally dynamic" — the mixture you would test is not a fixed object waiting to be sampled. You cannot enumerate the search space, so you cannot sample it uniformly, and there is no published rule for sampling it any other way.

Where the vault has seen this before, and from where

From the self-driving-lab literature, under the name experiment selection. The vault recorded the cleanest statement of it on 2026-08-01, from a Bayesian-optimisation paper in materials chemistry: "As execution is automated, the remaining bottleneck moves to experiment selection." That source also recorded the specific thing that stops the machinery transferring into toxicology — the policy needs a likelihood model saying how much a cheap measurement tells you about which hypothesis is right, and in toxicology that number does not exist.

This is the first time experiment selection appears as the PRIMARY blocker on a toxicological endpoint rather than as imported machinery. Mixture assessment is not a field waiting for a decision rule; it is a field whose central difficulty is the one the active-learning literature exists to solve.

That is a genuine, specific, checkable place where the two literatures this vault reads should be talking, and neither review cites the other.

The rule that does exist, and what it is not

The review reports mixture interactions being classified as synergistic, antagonistic or additive by comparing observed responses against a concentration-addition prediction — including inside an automated 384-well oestrogen-receptor reporter assay run under OECD Test Guideline 455 (the lab-grown cell test for whether a chemical switches on the oestrogen receptor).

Concentration addition is a published, computable, deterministic comparator. A machine can apply it unaided. It answers "is this mixture behaving as the sum of its parts?" and nothing else. No published rule converts a measured deviation from additivity into a hazard classification or an exposure limit — and the review is candid that non-additive responses are "often reported descriptively, without a clear understanding of where in the biological system those deviations emerge."

So the mixture field has a fixed rule for the question nobody regulates on, and none for the question they do. Both halves of the loop are open at once, which is why this map does not start higher than automatable-open-loop despite having machine-readable readouts and a computable comparator.

Limits

A structured literature review, not an experiment. One database, one search string, no second screener reported, and a small internal discrepancy (the abstract says 70 advanced studies; the tables sum to 71). It measures what was published, not what is possible.
