The same comparison gives two different answers depending on how far you zoom out

What was compared

Three systems, one drug (doxorubicin, a DNA-damaging chemotherapy whose gut toxicity is often what makes patients stop treatment), whole-transcriptome readout:

  • human colonoids — miniature gut-lining tissue grown from human stem cells,
  • mouse colonoids — the same thing from mouse,
  • mouse colon tissue — the actual animal.

A parallelogram: three measurable corners used to argue toward the fourth, human-in-a-person, which nobody can measure.

The result

All three converged on the same core biology. Cell-cycle regulation, DNA-damage response, DNA repair, apoptosis, and a shared set of p53-associated genes (BAX, INKA2, ZMAT3) — p53 being the master switch a cell throws when its DNA is broken. That is precisely what doxorubicin is supposed to do, and it happened everywhere. Reassuring, and almost entirely undiscriminating: it is the response nearly any DNA-damaging agent produces in nearly any dividing tissue, so it cannot tell two models apart.

Gene by gene, the mouse-in-a-dish looked more like the mouse-in-an-animal than the human-in-a-dish did. Group those same genes into pathways, and the two dish systems resembled each other — a shared in vitro signature the intact tissue lacked.

Why this is a finding and not an inconsistency

Both statements are true of the same data. They are not in tension; they measure different things. Genes are close to the machinery — orthologues differ, regulatory regions differ, baseline expression differs, so species leaves its fingerprint there. Pathways are an abstraction over sets of genes, and abstraction is exactly what absorbs species differences — while not absorbing the differences that come from being a piece of tissue in a gel rather than a piece of tissue in a body (no blood supply, no immune cells, no gradients, different oxygen, different mechanics).

So "does the non-animal model agree with the animal study?" is not a question with one answer. It has an answer per resolution, and the resolutions rank the models differently.

The consequence, which is the reason this is in the vault

A validation study that reports one concordance number has chosen a resolution, and usually does not say so. That choice is not neutral: here, scoring at the gene level would favour the mouse model, and scoring at the pathway level would favour both dish models over the animal. Same experiment, opposite conclusions about which system to trust, both defensible.

For a closed loop the problem sharpens, because a loop needs the choice made in advance and in writing. An automated system comparing a new model against a reference dataset has to be handed a resolution — genes, pathways, modules, an embedding — before it can compute agreement at all. Whoever picks that level picks the winner, and the pick is invisible in the output, which reports a single number that looks like a property of the models rather than of the comparison.

This joins a pattern the vault has been accumulating from several directions: agreement statistics between methods depend on how the comparison is set up, and none of the vault's own correlated_blind_spot rows carries one at all. It is also a live instance of a standing complaint — that the vault has repeatedly been told to measure agreement between paired methods and has never done it.

One more thing this source found, and it is a data-layer problem. Benchmarking against the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database — the public curated record of which chemicals change which genes — "supported DOX biology and highlighted underrepresented GI-relevant signals, indicating gaps in intestinal toxicogenomic annotations." The reference knowledge base is thinner on gut-specific responses than on the generic damage programme. Anything trained on it, or scored against it, inherits that thinness — and a loop that treats a curated database as ground truth will be most confident about the biology the database happens to cover.
