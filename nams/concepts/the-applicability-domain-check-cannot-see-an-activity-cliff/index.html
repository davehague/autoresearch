The guard rail passed everything, including the things it should have stopped

The applicability domain is this field's answer to "when should I not trust a computational prediction." It is one of the five OECD (Q)SAR validation principles, it is what a regulator asks for, and it is the thing an automated pipeline would call before accepting a model's output. The vault's glossary defines it as the boundary of chemical space a method is actually validated for; outside it, the prediction is not trustworthy.

In practice it is almost always implemented as a similarity check: compute how structurally close the query chemical is to the training set, and flag it if it is too far away. That is what was done here — Tanimoto similarity to the nearest training compounds against a threshold derived from the training set.

Result: 235 of 235 test chemicals inside the domain, with a comfortable margin (mean similarity 0.521 against a threshold of 0.153). By the standard check, every prediction in this study was trustworthy.

At the same time, in the same test set, the models were wrong in 11 of 12 structurally-near-identical clusters when using the MACCS fingerprint, and the single best configuration still sat at 74% balanced accuracy under cross-validation.

Why this is structural and not an implementation flaw

An activity cliff is a pair of chemicals that a structural representation says are nearly the same and that biology says are opposite — one causes allergy, the other does not. The definition contains the similarity.

So:

  • The domain check asks: is this chemical similar enough to things I have seen?
  • The failure mode asks: is this chemical dangerously similar to something with the opposite answer?

Passing the first is a precondition for the second. A chemical sitting on a cliff is not an outlier; it is maximally in-domain. The guard rail is not merely failing to catch cliffs — it is anticorrelated with them, because the deeper inside the training distribution a compound sits, the more analogues it has to disagree with.

What this means for an automated loop, specifically

The vault grades (Q)SAR and read-across as computational-connective — the model layer a closed loop would update — and repeatedly notes that this layer is the one choosing what the wet layer measures (in-silico-layer-chooses-what-the-wet-layer-measures). A loop that prioritises chemicals with a model and confirms them with an assay needs a reliable "I don't know" signal, and the applicability domain is the signal everyone reaches for.

This result says: that signal is silent on the errors that matter most. The wrongly-called chemicals do not arrive labelled uncertain. They arrive with a high confidence score and a passed domain check, and they are wrong in a way that a second model with the same representation will be wrong about identically. The vault has already recorded that models converge on what they propose and diverge on what they judge (multiple-frontier-models-are-one-vote-not-six); a cliff is where they converge and are wrong together.

Honest ceiling

One study, one group, 1,174 chemicals with 235 held out, one endpoint. The ≥70% Tanimoto cliff threshold is a stated compromise the authors flag — a stricter cut-off would have produced fewer, sharper cliffs and a smaller analysis. The applicability-domain implementation here is one of several in use, and a distance-to-model or conformal-prediction domain would behave differently (whether better on cliffs is untested here and is not claimed). And the labels being predicted are 95% animal-test outcomes, so the "errors" are disagreements with a mouse assay, not with a person.

Rebutted by: read-across-as-an-algorithm-fails-where-read-across-fails — same study, and it sharpens rather than opposes: the guard rail's blindness matters most for the neighbour-voting algorithms (k-nearest neighbours, i.e. automated read-across), whose errors on structurally discordant compounds were 100% false negatives.
