Robots fail at the joins, not at the motions

The interesting number in this benchmark is not the atomic-skill success rate. It is the gap between the atomic-skill rate and the workflow rate.

LevelBest policy
One motion (average across skill types)0.57
One motion (placement only — object already in hand)0.92
Sub-steps completed in a 7-step procedure0.62 of 7
Complete the 7-step procedure0 of 50 trials

A policy that succeeds at individual motions somewhere between half and nine-tenths of the time completes a seven-step procedure never.

Why the arithmetic is not the whole story

Part of this is just multiplication: seven steps at 0.6 each is 0.03, and at 0.4 each is 0.002. Reliability compounds along a procedure, which is why a per-step number that sounds tolerable in a demo is a workflow number of zero. Anyone who has shipped a pipeline knows this shape.

But the paper locates the failure more specifically than compounding would predict, and that is the finding. The best policy grasps the funnel with reasonable success and then "its success rate drops considerably when subsequently inserting the funnel into the round-bottom flask." ACT picks the funnel up 40% of the time and seats it on the flask zero times. The authors' summary: "the key limitations of existing methods lie not only in mastering individual atomic skills, but also in maintaining stable object states and reliably composing multiple skills into continuous execution."

So the difficulty concentrates at the transitions — where an object must be held in a known state while the next skill begins. Each skill was trained as a demonstration that starts from a tidy initial condition; a skill executed second starts from wherever the previous skill left things, which is not the distribution it was trained on.

What this says about the vault's premise

This vault's founding claim is that Tox21 already proved the robot half of the loop — ~10,000 chemicals, triplicate, in a week — and that the missing piece is the deciding half (automation-tox21-qhts-platform, automation-experiment-selection).

That claim survives, and this paper sharpens what it is a claim about. Tox21's robot moves standardised microplates along a fixed path with purpose-built fixturing and no improvisation. It is not doing dexterous manipulation and never needed to. So "the execution half is solved" is true for assays whose physical form is a plate on a track, which covers a large part of this vault's column: the reporter-gene assays, the cytotoxicity readouts, the high-throughput screens.

It is not true for anything that requires handling. automation-specimen-handling already carries the matching measurement from the biology side — a commercial zebrafish-embryo sorter that undercounts by 13.4% and returns 50.8% false negatives on fluorescence sorting, and whose quality classifications are statistically unrelated to whether the embryo survives. Two papers, two ends of the same limb: a generalist robot hand cannot chain seven bench steps, and a specialist biological sorter cannot be trusted about what it sorted.

The honest synthesis is that the execution leg is solved by format, not by capability. Standardise the physical form of the experiment and a machine runs it beautifully. Ask a machine to do what a technician does with glassware and tissue and it does not finish. That is a constraint on which assays can be looped, and it is not visible anywhere on the vault's three axes — the ladder grades readouts and decision rules, both of which sit at the end of an experiment.

Two limits on reading this too hard

A benchmark paper is incentivised to report a hard benchmark. The authors designed the tasks and then measured the failure. That makes the result good evidence that this difficulty exists and poor evidence about the field's ceiling.

And no human baseline was run. Adding NaCl through a funnel is a first-week task for a technician. The paper never says so with a number, so the comparison every reader makes silently is not actually in the paper.
