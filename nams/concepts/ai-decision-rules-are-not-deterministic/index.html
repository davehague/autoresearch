An AI decision rule is not a deterministic decision rule

shared/evidence-model.md defines the vault's top loop tier precisely:

closed-loop-ready — "Instrument-readable readout and a fixed, deterministic data-interpretation procedure. An AI could read the plate and make the call."

Twenty maps hold that tier. Every one of them earns it the same way — through arithmetic a committee wrote down:

  • ≤ 50% viability → irritant (TG 439, the lab-grown skin test)
  • OD at 405 nm against a fixed Irritection Draize Equivalent cut-off (TG 496)
  • time lag > 180 s AND intensity < 0.05 %/s AND plateau ≤ 5.0 % (TG 494)
  • 2 concordant results out of 3 assays (GL 497 Part I, skin sensitisation)
  • breakthrough time mapped to GHS sub-category (TG 435)

These are deterministic because they are fixed formulae. Same input, same output, forever, in any laboratory. That property is not incidental to the tier — it is the tier.

What the validation bodies say happens when you swap in a model

"Very different to other computer-based methods, AI does not necessarily deliver the exact same result for the same input every time, particularly when dealing with stochastic and/or probabilistic models. The same prompt in a large language model (LLM) can give divergent responses, even more so in different versions of the same LLM or different LLMs." — Hartung & Kleinstreuer, ALTEX 42(1), 2025

The vault has been reading its own third axis as a ladder whose top rung is "an AI could make the call." The rung is defined by a property AI does not have.

The precise shape of the problem — and its limits

This is not a claim that AI is unreliable, and it must not be flattened into one.

  • The vault's 20 closed-loop-ready maps are unaffected. Their decision rules are thresholds, not models. An AI executing if viability ≤ 50% is as deterministic as a spreadsheet. The tier is safe wherever the rule stays arithmetic. No tier moves on this source.
  • The exposure is at the frontier, in exactly the direction the field is heading: replacing a committee-written threshold with a learned one. The vault already holds the leading case — skin-sensitization-quantitative-pod-da (GL 497 Part III), whose data-interpretation procedure is a Bayesian model (SARA-ICE), not a threshold, and which is adopted. That is the vault's flagship evidence that a model can be a regulatory decision rule. It is also, now, the map where "deterministic" needs checking rather than assuming: a fixed, version-pinned Bayesian computation is deterministic; a retrained one is a different method. (The vault has not verified which SARA-ICE is; recorded as an open question, not a finding.)
  • Determinism is recoverable, and cheaply — by pinning the model version, fixing the seed, and setting temperature to zero. But that is a deployment discipline, not a property of the method, and nothing in the vault's grading currently records whether it was applied. A guideline threshold cannot be un-pinned by a careless operator. A model can.

The second half: validation as a moving target

"AI-based NAMs are envisioned to evolve rapidly, making static validation processes potentially outdated almost as soon as they are completed. As models grow and adapt, the traditional approach – anchored in version-controlled, static models – will struggle to keep pace."

Every validation this vault records — the JaCVAM ring trial behind TG 494, EU-NETVAL's thyroid programme, ECVAM's blinded CFU-GM trial — operates on a frozen artefact. A continuously-retrained model does not have one. See validation-as-continuous-service-not-event.

And the authorship cuts both ways.
