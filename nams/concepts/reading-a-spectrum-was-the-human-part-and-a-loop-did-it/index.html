Reading a spectrum was the human part, and a loop did it

Almost every human-in-the-loop grade in this vault comes down to the same sentence: the instrument produces a perfectly machine-readable output, and then a trained person has to decide what it means. A microscope image is pixels; a micronucleus is a judgement. A chromatogram is peaks; a metabolite is a judgement.

The vault has recorded machine vision chipping away at the image version of that problem — micronucleus scoring by imaging flow cytometry, embryo staging in a nematode. This source is the same move on the analytical chemistry version, and it is arguably the harder one, because the judgement is not perceptual but deductive: a spectroscopist reasons backwards from peaks to a structure that would explain every one of them.

MACROS does that by iterating — propose a structure, predict the spectrum it would give, compare to the real one, revise. The refinement loop is where the gain comes from, adding 0.05–0.09 fingerprint similarity over the first draft in every cohort tested. That is a small, specific, and encouraging result about closed-loop reasoning as an architecture: the model does not get better, the checking does.

Why this lands on the metabolism map

metabolism-biotransformation-metabolite-generation exists because the vault's single most repeated cause of correlated blind spots is chemical rather than interpretive: cells in a dish mostly cannot convert a chemical into what a body would actually produce, so a harmless parent with a dangerous metabolite reads clean. That map holds the proposed fix — run human enzymes as an upstream service and manufacture the metabolites so something else can test them.

That map's loop_blocker is stated precisely: "no rule states which metabolites matter, at what yield, or how the downstream assay's answer should be combined with the parent compound's."

This source does not touch that blocker. It touches the step before it, which the metabolism map had quietly assumed was free: once you have manufactured a mixture of metabolites, you have to know what you made. In practice that is mass spectrometry and NMR, followed by an expert working out which structures the peaks correspond to — and for anything not already in a database, that expert step is slow and often inconclusive. The paper names the standing state of that problem: known metabolites are "only a tiny fraction of possible structures in the mammalian metabolome, leaving extensive unidentified signals as metabolic 'dark matter'."

So the honest edge is: the identification step now has a candidate automation, and the interpretation step still has none. A loop that manufactures metabolites, names them automatically, and then has no rule for which of them matters has moved its bottleneck one step downstream without removing it. It has, in the metabolism map's own phrasing, "more compounds in it."

What must not be inferred

No axis on any map moves because of this. It is a preprint, it reports no toxicological work, and its strongest metabolite numbers require fine-tuning on metabolite spectra first — zero-shot on real metabolite data it sits at 0.52–0.59 fingerprint similarity, which is "structurally in the neighbourhood," not "identified." A fingerprint similarity is not an identification and the two must not be quoted interchangeably.

The three-expert human comparison is the paper's weakest evidence and its most quotable claim. A trained spectroscopist scoring 0.102 unaided on their own core task is a number that wants explaining, and the paper does not explain it.

And the loop-does-not-mean-endorsed rail applies here in an unusual form. If metabolite identification becomes cheap and automatic, the volume of identified metabolites entering a hazard assessment goes up sharply, and every one of them is a compound with no toxicological data and no rule for what to do with it. Automating the naming of things does not automate deciding about them, and a system that generates far more named unknowns than anyone can evaluate is not obviously an improvement over one that generated fewer.
