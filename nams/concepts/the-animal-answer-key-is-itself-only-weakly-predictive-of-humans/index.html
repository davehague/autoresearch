A method's "gold standard" comparator was never gold — the field's own largest concordance dataset says so

validation-paradigm-nam-confidence's strand (2) asks "whether anything exists to validate a method against" — this source answers it for the specific, commonly-assumed reference: animal data. Liu & Fan's 7,565-drug, five-species concordance analysis is the largest of its kind, and its own stated headline is that organ-system-level concordance between a preclinical animal finding and the matching human adverse event is "generally low to modest." Even the best-performing species (monkey) only reaches a median likelihood ratio of 3.74-4.18 across organ systems — below the conventional threshold (LR+ > 10) the authors themselves use to call a result "strong."

Why this pushes back on the validation paradigm rather than just adding a data point. Much of this vault's validation-tier ladder (regulatory-adopted > formally-validated > ...) implicitly treats "replicates the animal result" as a meaningful bar for a NAM to clear. This source is a reminder, with real numbers at real scale, that the animal result it is being compared against is itself a weak predictor of the thing that actually matters — the human outcome — for most organ systems most of the time. A NAM that disagrees with an animal study on a vague, pooled endpoint (like "hepatotoxicity," LR+ 2.2 here) may be disagreeing with a reference that was never trustworthy on that endpoint either.

The finding is not uniformly negative, and that nuance matters. Concordance is strong for specific, well-defined adverse events — immune-mediated hepatitis (LR+ 321.8, mice), hyperinsulinemia (217.7), drug-specific antibody detection and infection in biologics (116.0) — even where the organ-system-level average is weak. The authors' own reading: concordance is a property of how precisely the finding is defined, not of the organ system as a whole. This is a methodological lesson this vault should carry forward into its own endpoint grading: a method's apparent disagreement with "the literature" can be an artifact of comparing against a pooled, imprecise label rather than the specific finding that actually transfers.

Bias watch, stated plainly. This is a wholly Johnson & Johnson-authored and -funded study (funding_flag: industry), using J&J-licensed databases, promoting an internal J&J tool and Shiny app in its own conclusions. That does not make the statistics wrong — the result independently corroborates 97% of a prior, unrelated 2021 study's findings — but the framing (a refinement argument for smarter species selection, stopping well short of "stop testing species X") is also exactly the framing a pharma safety group has an institutional interest in landing on.
