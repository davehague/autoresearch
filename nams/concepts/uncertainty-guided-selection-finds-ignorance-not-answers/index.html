Uncertainty-guided selection finds the model's ignorance, not the world's answer

On 2026-08-12 this vault recorded a finding it flagged as awkward for anyone building a regulated closed loop: how well-calibrated a model's uncertainty is does not predict how good an answer it finds. In a materials-chemistry benchmark across fifteen surrogate models and nine problems, that correlation ran negative on every problem tested, and the best-calibrated model family was the weakest optimiser (calibrated-uncertainty-does-not-predict-optimisation-performance).

The awkwardness is that calibrated uncertainty is close to word-for-word what regulatory guidance on AI models asks for (regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility). One benchmark in one field is a finding worth watching, not a rule.

Tonight it happened again, in cancer pharmacology, from people who had never heard of the materials result.

The measurement

ScreenShot is a pretrained model that predicts how a tumour sample responds to drug combinations, and then uses its own representations to pick which combinations to measure next. In an ablation the authors swapped the selection criterion:

Selection weighted byGlobal prediction accuracyHit recall at budget 300
Monte-Carlo dropout uncertainty — "measure where I am least sure"best (MAE and Pearson)78.6%
Predicted effect size — "measure where I think the answer differs most"worse84.8%

Their reading, verbatim: "uncertainty-guided selection targets regions of model ignorance rather than regions of differential drug sensitivity."

Why two fields agreeing is the whole point

The materials result and this one share no model architecture, no uncertainty estimator, no objective, and no authors. Materials chemistry was optimising a physical property with Gaussian-process and ensemble surrogates. This is a transformer doing in-context prediction of cell viability with dropout-based uncertainty. The mechanism they name is the same, and it is not a quirk of either setup: a model's uncertainty is a map of the model, and the experiment you want to run is a question about the world. Those overlap only when the model's ignorance happens to sit where the interesting answer is, and there is no reason it should.

What this does and does not mean for the vault's question

It does not argue against calibration as a regulatory requirement. A regulator asking a model to know what it does not know is asking for something entirely reasonable, and "it makes a better experiment-picker" was never the justification. Both readings can be true: calibrated uncertainty is the right thing to demand of a model that is going to make a claim, and the wrong thing to steer a model that is going to choose an experiment.

What it does mean is that a closed tox loop cannot get its selection policy for free from its credibility dossier. The vault's structural analogue is tiered testing: run a cheap screen broadly, spend the expensive capacity where the screen pointed. Read through this finding, a tiered scheme that escalates where the screen was least certain is optimising for the model's comfort. A scheme that escalates where the predicted hazard is largest is optimising for the question. Regulatory guidance points at the first; both of these papers say the second finds more.

And the honest limit. This is a preprint, and it is efficacy screening in cancer cell lines — "hit" means a drug pair that works, not a chemical that harms. Nothing here was run in a real closed loop; the selection experiments replay datasets that were already fully measured. What transfers is the structure of the argument, not a number a toxicologist can use.
