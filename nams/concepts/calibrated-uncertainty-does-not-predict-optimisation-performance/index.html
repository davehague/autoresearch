The property regulators ask an AI model for is not the property that makes the loop work

Calibration is the property of a model whose stated confidence matches its actual hit rate: of the things it called 90% likely, about 90% happen. It is measured by expected calibration error — lower is better.

It is also, close to word for word, what regulatory guidance on AI models asks for when it asks a model to quantify and justify its own uncertainty. It is the reason Gaussian processes have been the default engine of self-driving labs for two decades: they hand you an honest error bar with every prediction.

Across fifteen surrogate models on nine optimisation problems, better calibration was associated with worse final answers — on every benchmark, without exception. The correlation between calibration error and regret (how far short of the true optimum the campaign ended) was negative throughout, from −0.88 at the extreme to −0.08 at the weakest. The entire Gaussian-process family, the best-calibrated group in the study, was the weakest optimiser group on the molecular-descriptor problems.

What this does and does not say

It does not say calibration is worthless. The authors' own reading is careful and this note adopts it: uncertainty acts as "a secondary, benchmark-dependent factor rather than a primary driver of performance." Calibration remains the right thing to want when you are being asked how much should I trust this specific prediction — a question that matters enormously for a regulatory dossier.

What it says is that calibration is not a proxy for whether the loop finds the right answer. Those are two different questions, and the field has been treating a model's honesty about its own uncertainty as evidence of its usefulness for choosing what to run next. On this evidence, it is not.

Why this lands on the vault's regulatory axis and not only its loop axis

The vault holds a framework map for how a regulator decides whether to accept an AI model at all (regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility), built around FDA's proportional model-risk framing: the more influence a model has on a decision, the more credibility evidence it must supply. Uncertainty quantification sits near the centre of what that evidence is expected to look like.

So there is a tension worth naming and not resolving. A model selected to satisfy an acceptance requirement for well-quantified uncertainty may, on this evidence, be a worse engine for the loop it is meant to drive. That is not an argument against the requirement — a regulator asking a model to know what it does not know is asking for something reasonable, and "it optimises better" was never the justification. It is an observation that the acceptance criterion and the performance criterion are measuring different things, and the field has been assuming they point the same way.

Honest limits

Computational only — no physical experiment anywhere in the source. A preprint, not peer reviewed. Nine benchmarks, two of them synthetic functions the authors constructed. No toxicology, no assay, no hazard endpoint appears in the paper; the transfer to a regulatory-tox loop is this vault's inference. And a correlation across fifteen models is not a causal account of why well-calibrated models rank candidates badly.
