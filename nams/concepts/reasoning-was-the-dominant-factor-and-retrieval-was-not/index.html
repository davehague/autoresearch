Reasoning was the dominant factor. Retrieval was not.

Why the ablation matters more than the system

The self-driving-lab literature in this vault is full of architectures and thin on controlled comparisons of architectural choices. This paper ran 20 agent configurations over five protocols of increasing complexity and asked which choices pay. That is the useful artefact, not the system.

And it measured the right thing. Three metrics were reported, but only one is an outcome: nRMSE on the final vial contents — how far the chemical amounts that actually end up in each vial are from the amounts that should. The other two (step F1, step-ordering correlation) are process metrics, and the paper says plainly that step-level scores "can mask critical errors." This vault has a standing note on exactly that confusion: the-metric-that-improved-is-not-the-one-a-decision-uses.

The split that should worry a hazard loop

What improved with reasoningBy how much
Numerical correctness (nRMSE — the amounts)0.25 → 0.10 single-agent; 0.027 best case; >85% error reduction on complex tasks
Procedural correctness (F1 — the steps)best ≈ 0.856; the effect of reasoning was "less pronounced"

Getting the steps roughly right is easy. Getting the quantities right is where reasoning earns its keep.

For a chemistry synthesis that is an inconvenience — the reaction fails and someone notices. For a hazard-testing loop it is the dangerous shape, and the vault has been building this case since 2026-07-26: a robot that drops a plate produces a blank and someone investigates; a robot that delivers the wrong concentration produces a plausible negative, which the loop believes, records, and selects its next experiment against. See a-robot-repeats-a-systematic-error-with-exceptional-precision and the-safety-net-fires-on-the-models-doubt-not-the-worlds-state. A configuration scoring 0.85 on step inclusion and dosing at the wrong concentration passes every check that only reads steps.

Retrieval did not help. Structure did. This is the second time.

They bolted a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline onto instrument documentation. It improved F1 for one architecture (+0.044 average absolute) and degraded it for the other (−0.024), and degraded the outcome metric, nRMSE, by 0.028 on average in both.

This is the second independent result in the vault saying the same thing, from a different task and a different group. retrieval-degraded-what-structure-repaired found that giving an experiment-designing model web search left five of seven models statistically unchanged, significantly degraded one, and nearly doubled another's fatal-flaw rate — while structural interventions (separating configuration from planning, tool-augmented atomic steps, explicit written norms) lifted scores across six models and cut the fatal-flaw rate from 14.00% to 2.67%.

Two studies, two domains, one answer: giving the agent more to read is not the lever. Giving it a better shape is. For a toxicology loop that is directly actionable, because "point it at the guideline text" is the first thing anyone proposes.

The self-correction result is the same shape and finer-grained: guided self-checks (sequential, task-specific) improve procedure; unguided self-checks (one holistic prompt, iterated by a reasoning model) improve numbers. Two different repairs for two different failures, and using the wrong one buys nothing.

Where this pushes back — an open disagreement, recorded as one

stance: pushes-back. The claim it contradicts is more-agents-and-more-retrieval-did-not-help, which found in a 105-configuration sweep of an instrument-controlling agent that one-agent systems used ~10,000 fewer tokens than two-agent equivalents at similar pass rates, that a three-agent topology showed no consistent improvement, and that a no-retrieval baseline performed about as well as the best retrieval configuration.

On retrieval the two agree completely. On agent count they disagree: that study found multi-agent bought nothing; this one reports multi-agent plus full reasoning as its best configuration, an 83% nRMSE improvement over the single-agent non-reasoning baseline.

Neither is decisive and the vault does not pick a winner. Both are single-lab. They differ in task (microscopy control vs. protocol authoring), in what they scored (benchmark pass rate vs. final vial contents), and the earlier study declares a confound — a model-implementation incompatibility inside its multi-agent graphs — that could account for its result on its own. What is recordable: "more agents" is contested and "more retrieval" is not. Anyone specifying a toxicology loop should treat agent count as an open empirical question and should stop treating retrieval as a free improvement.

And the tension with the reasoning dial that isn't a tension

more-reasoning-effort-produced-more-hallucinated-signal found the opposite-looking result: raising reasoning effort in an agent driving real quantum-sensing experiments made it dramatically more likely to report a measured signal that was not there — false positive rates rising from 1.4% to 16.7% in one model and 14.8% to 53.2% in another.

Read carefully, the two agree. That paper's own finding was that the same dial improves hypothesis formation while degrading data evaluation (its Ramsey checkpoint pass rates rose with effort; its resonance-detection false positives rose too). AutoLabs turned the dial up on the half where it helps and never touched the half where it hurts — it designs experiments and never once evaluates a measurement.

That is the synthesis, and it is a design rule for a toxicology loop rather than a paradox: turn reasoning up on planning; do not assume the same setting is safe on the plate reader. The loop has two halves and they want opposite settings on the same knob.

The honest limits

  • Only 2 of 5 protocols were physically run. All five loaded into the instrument software (100% for Experiments 1–4, occasional failures on the multi-plate fifth) and all five ran in simulation mode with the arms stationary. Everything above is measured on generated files and expert comparison, not on matter.
  • One instrument. The authors call it "a case study on this specific system" (Unchained Labs Big Kahuna).
  • Instrument constraints enforced by prompt text, not schema. The authors state this "does not guarantee compliance" — the same gap the-guardrail-was-a-schema-not-the-model records from the other direction.
  • Chemistry, not toxicology. Battery electrolytes. No endpoint, no assay, no Test Guideline appears.
  • funding_flag: none — four Pacific Northwest National Laboratory authors, code released openly, no instrument or platform vendor in the byline. Worth stating: automation claims in this vault usually come from the party selling the automation, and this one does not.
