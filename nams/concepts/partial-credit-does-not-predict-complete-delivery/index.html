A mean in the high eighties and a conjunction at zero

This is a statistics finding dressed as an AI benchmark, and it is the one an engineer standing up an automated pipeline is most likely to walk into.

Two ways to score an agent's scientific work:

  • Avg. Score — partial credit against a rubric. A mean over many sub-criteria.
  • Pass Rate — did the run satisfy the entire deliverable contract? Every required artifact present, every one correct, all of them mutually consistent. A conjunction over many conditions, checked by an executable grader.

Across twelve frontier models and three agent harnesses, Avg. Score ran 67.5 to 87.9. Pass Rate ran 3.1% to 20.6%.

And the gap is not constant. That is the part that turns a curiosity into a warning:

An entire scientific domain scored 94.9 out of 100 on average and completed nothing at all. A different domain, same agents, same week, finished six jobs in ten.

Why this happens, and why it is not a benchmark artifact

If a job needs n things to be independently right, and each is right with probability p, the job completes with probability p^n. A rubric averaging those n things reports p. Partial credit is linear in the number of correct components; completion is exponential in the number of incorrect ones. At p = 0.95 and n = 20, the rubric says 95 and the completion rate is 36%. At p = 0.90 it is 12%.

Which means the divergence is not evidence of a badly designed rubric. It is what a rubric mathematically does when the underlying task is a conjunction, and it gets worse the more deliverables a task has. The paper's own failure analysis is consistent with this: judge-assessed artifact shortfalls — one required deliverable missing, wrong, or inconsistent with the others — appeared in 97% of failing materials-characterization submissions and 95% of analytical chemistry, against 43% in quantum chemistry. The domains that decoupled worst are the ones whose contracts demand the most mutually consistent pieces.

The consequence for anyone instrumenting an automated lab

You will reach for the mean, because the mean is what moves. Pass Rate is a step function — it sits at zero for weeks, then jumps. Avg. Score rises smoothly, responds to every improvement, makes a nice dashboard, and can climb from 67 to 94 while the number of usable outputs stays at zero. It is the metric that will be chosen, and it is the metric that lies in exactly the regime you are operating in.

The second consequence is about transfer, and it is the one this vault cares about most. An aggregate leaderboard ranking is uninformative about any specific scientific setting. The same agents spanned 0% to 60% across six domains of chemistry and physics — fields with mature software, machine-readable data formats and decades of standardisation. Toxicology was not among them. Nothing here licenses a number for a toxicology workflow, in either direction, and the vault should refuse to interpolate one. What the domain spread establishes is that you cannot interpolate one — that a general capability figure tells you nothing about your endpoint until someone measures your endpoint.

The nearest neighbour is the life science slice: ten tasks, best Pass Rate 20%, and the domain where deterministic grader diagnostics fired most often (50%) — i.e. failures that a mechanical check could catch, rather than judgement calls about artifact quality.

What the benchmark deliberately did not test, which makes the numbers worse

From the paper: "The agent is not asked to set the research agenda or formulate the problem." Objective, inputs and required outputs were all fixed in advance. This is not autonomous science. It is executing a specified workflow — the easiest version of the task, and the version this vault would call the back half of a loop with the hard half removed. A closed toxicology loop needs the removed half too: deciding which chemical, which concentration, what to run next. That capability sits on top of a 20.6% execution base.
