The general-purpose model cannot see the assay

A recurring hope in the autonomous-lab literature is that a sufficiently capable general model can be pointed at an instrument's output and asked what it means. This is the first measurement in the vault of what happens when you point one at a toxicology-relevant image readout.

What was tested

Cell Painting stains cells with a fixed panel of fluorescent dyes marking different internal compartments — nucleus, mitochondria, cytoskeleton, membrane, endoplasmic reticulum — photographs them under an automated microscope, and reduces each picture to a numerical fingerprint of how the cell's shape and internal organisation changed. See phenotypic-profiling-cell-painting.

The authors took images from three large public Cell Painting archives and asked general-purpose multimodal models three retrieval questions of increasing difficulty:

  1. Which cell line is this? — F1 0.526.
  2. Which fluorescence channel am I looking at? — F1 0.628.
  3. Which compound was applied? — this is the one that matters.

(F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, on a 0-to-1 scale. What counts as chance depends on how many options the model is choosing between, which is why the paper reports against an explicit random baseline rather than a fixed threshold.)

The result on question three

"All models fell below random baseline, except for Grok-4, which slightly exceeded it. Confusion matrices … revealed near-zero F1 scores."

Worse than guessing. Not "poor", not "needs fine-tuning" — the models' answers were anti-correlated with the truth often enough to land under the coin-flip line. One model out of the set cleared random by a small margin.

The same task, with a model trained for it — CP-CLIP, fitted to 1,846,436 image-and-experimental-context pairs — reached F1 0.896.

Why this belongs in this vault and not just in a machine-learning one

Every agentic-science architecture the vault holds assumes a perception layer that works. The Lab Agent Protocol, ToxMCP, the trustworthy-autonomous-science roadmap, the protocol-validation systems — all of them specify how an agent talks to an instrument and none of them specifies that the agent can interpret what the instrument returns. See automation-self-driving-labs.

This is the perception layer being measured, on an assay type this vault tracks, and failing outright in its off-the-shelf form. The fix is known and unremarkable — train a model for the readout — but it is a real cost, and it has a consequence the vault already records: a trained scorer carries its own applicability domain, separate from the assay's (a-trained-scorer-has-its-own-applicability-domain). Swapping "the agent looks at the plate" for "a specialised model that was fitted to 1.8 million images from three specific archives looks at the plate" moves the loop's trust boundary somewhere the axis does not currently record.

What this does not say

  • It does not say general models are useless here. It says they cannot do compound identification from raw Cell Painting images without task-specific training. Those are different claims.
  • The performance gap is the paper's selling point and two of six authors are at a compute vendor (funding_flag: [industry]). Graded sceptically. It is also the expected result on priors — fluorescence micrographs of stained cells are not meaningfully represented in general training data — and it is reported against a stated random baseline, which is the honest way to report it.
  • Compound identification is not hazard classification. Knowing which chemical made this picture is a retrieval task with a known answer key. Deciding whether an unknown chemical is harmful is the thing nobody has a rule for, and no model in this paper was asked to do it. See an-agent-that-explains-the-answer-it-was-handed.
  • Single study, single readout type, strength: single-study.

Loop verdict

Nothing moves. phenotypic-profiling-cell-painting holds at automatable-open-loop. But the map's "no human scoring anywhere in the chain" description now needs a qualifier: the chain contains a trained model doing the seeing, and the untrained version of that model performs worse than chance.
