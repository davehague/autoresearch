The barrier this industry names is paperwork, and the second-largest one is that nothing is forcing it

Two facts from the same survey, and they should be read next to each other:

  • Only 10 of 34 companies (29%) named scientific gaps in the methods as a barrier to using them in a regulatory submission.
  • 17 of 34 (50%) said there is "no hindrance to development without NAMs."

Everything above those two rows is administrative: no harmonised international guidance (77%), no Japanese guidance (71%), not enough experience (71%), unclear which datasets and endpoints a regulator wants (68%).

The picture that assembles is not the one the field's own literature implies. The methods are not primarily being held back by doubts about whether they work. They are being held back because nobody has written down what an acceptable submission looks like, and because — for half of this industry — the animal-study route still works fine and there is no cost to staying on it.

Why this matters to a vault about automation

This vault grades every method on three axes and keeps them apart on principle: is the science sound, has anyone accepted it, and could a machine run it. The count that gets quoted from the loop axis is 26 maps at closed-loop-ready — an instrument produces the number, a published rule converts it into a call.

That count says what is possible. It says nothing about what is used, and this survey is a measurement of the second thing. An adopted, loop-ready assay that 85% of an industry has never submitted is automatable the way an empty motorway is fast. The vault has no axis for uptake, and every so often a source arrives that makes the absence conspicuous. This is one.

It also sharpens what "the regulatory axis" means. This vault's regulatory_status field records whether a method may be used — an OECD Test Guideline exists, a validation programme is running, a policy says a fit-for-purpose method is acceptable. All four of the top barriers here sit inside the OECD-TG-adopted and "CDER will accept a fit-for-purpose method" worlds and are not resolved by them. Permission is not instruction. A company that has been told it may file non-animal data, and has not been told what a complete filing contains, files an animal study.

The other side of the same number

regulatory-acceptance-japan-pmda-nams exists because Japan's medicines regulator audited its own 2020–2024 approval files and counted non-animal methods in 22 of 115 tests — about one in five — even where an adopted Test Guideline existed. The vault could measure that gap and had two competing readings of it: regulators not accepting, or industry not offering.

This is the industry answering, and it says: not offering, and here is why. The two measurements are independent — a regulator counting receipts, an association polling submitters — and they agree. That is the first time this vault has had both sides of an adoption number from the same jurisdiction.

Carefully, though: they agree, and they are not fully independent evidence about causes. The trade association has an obvious interest in the finding "we would use these if you wrote the guidance," and the map's own standing caution about a promoting body auditing its own programme applies in mirror image here. Two aligned measurements from two interested parties is better than one and is not corroboration in the ring- trial sense.

What the industry asked for

Ranked: an ICH topic proposal / international harmonisation (79%), Japanese qualification guidance (68%), a public database of qualified methods (65%), a formal qualification system (50%), an expert consultation desk (47%). One free-text request was blunter than any of those — a "clear indication of which in vivo tests can be replaced."

That last one is worth holding onto. It is the same question this vault asks in its own vocabulary, and neither the FDA roadmap, nor the EU phase-out plan, nor the UK strategy currently answers it as a list.

The brakes

  • Self-report, one snapshot, 34 companies, three months after the roadmap that prompted it; respondents likely skew toward the interested.
  • funding_flag: [industry] — the surveyors are the trade association of the surveyed, and the paper is explicitly written to support policy recommendations to Japan's ministry and regulator.
  • The category is unstable. The authors volunteer that respondents disagreed on what counts as a non-animal method, citing one of their own case studies (an eye test using cow corneas — a reduction, not a replacement) as an example. Every percentage above is a percentage of a soft denominator.
  • Japan, pharmaceuticals, non-clinical safety only. No claim is made or implied about chemicals, cosmetics or pesticides, or about any other market.
