A calibration chemical proves the assay works; it cannot prove the assay would have caught the chemical that matters

OECD Test Guideline 249 uses a single rainbow-trout gill cell line (RTgill-W1) as the non-animal stand-in for killing fish, and takes the lowest of three machine-read viability EC50s as the predicted 96-hour fish lethal concentration. That is a fully quantitative rule with an instrument readout — which is why this vault grades it closed-loop-ready. This study is not a test of that guideline (the guideline's own cell line was not among the six used), but it is a direct measurement of the assumption underneath it: that a fish cell line stands in for a fish.

On the calibration chemical, that assumption looks impeccable. 3,4-dichloroaniline — the guideline's own reference toxicant — killed cells at roughly the same concentration in coho salmon, chinook salmon, sockeye salmon, rainbow trout, bluegill and fathead minnow, and that concentration matched the value the guideline publishes. The authors use this correctly, as evidence their hands and protocol are sound.

On the chemical anyone would actually want to screen, the panel disintegrated. 6PPD-quinone is the tyre-rubber breakdown product identified in 2021 as the cause of coho salmon dying within hours of rainstorms in urban streams. Coho salmon cells detect it at 8.54 µg/L. Rainbow trout cells detect it at 37.8 µg/L — 4.4× less sensitive. Chinook salmon, sockeye salmon, bluegill and fathead minnow cells show nothing at all, at concentrations up to 500 µg/L, roughly 59× the coho response level. Two of those four silent lines are salmon.

The generalisable statement, which is not about fish. A reference chemical certifies that an assay is being performed correctly. It says nothing about whether the assay's biology contains the mechanism the next chemical will act through. Here the reference chemical is a general cytotoxicant that kills any cell, so every line agrees; 6PPD-quinone acts through something species-specific — prior work points at mitochondrial dysfunction and interspecies differences in metabolic processing — so the answer depends entirely on which line was in the plate. An automated loop cannot tell these two situations apart from inside the assay. Both produce a clean dose-response curve or a clean flat line, both pass every quality control the guideline specifies, and only one of them is an answer.

Three further limits the authors state. All concentrations are nominal — not analytically confirmed — while Test Guideline 249 asks for measured concentrations, and 6PPD-quinone can adsorb to plastic and hydrolyse, so true potency may be higher than reported. The lines are not equivalent tissue (embryonic vs epithelial vs gill), and fish absorb chemicals mainly through the gill, so gill-derived cells may simply be the more sensitive choice. And the RTG-2 result contradicts an independent laboratory that reported no effect in the same cell line with the same viability assay up to 100 µg/L — the authors attribute it to culture conditions and close by calling harmonised protocols "an urgent need." That is an inter-laboratory reproducibility problem inside the vault's cleanest commercially-unencumbered adopted method's own assay family, and it is recorded as one.

Bias watch. The study was partly funded by Bridgestone Corporation, a tyre manufacturer, on the toxicity of a tyre-derived chemical. The disclosure is upfront, the performing institution is a Japanese national research institute, and the result confirming extreme coho sensitivity is not convenient to the funder. The finding that four of six fish cell lines see nothing is, and it is graded with that in view.
