A potency method whose decision rule is already software

Nineteen of this vault's method maps sit at automatable-open-loop for one reason: the assay produces a clean machine-readable number and no published procedure converts that number into a call. Four routes out have now been seen — a committee writes the rule, everyone waits for more potency data, somebody fits a rule to the already-published literature, or a regulator writes a waiver flowchart. All four are institutional acts.

This method sidesteps the question by not producing a call at all. It produces a dose.

What that changes

A transcriptomic point of departure is the concentration at which a cell's gene expression starts moving. The pipeline is: dose across a range, sequence the whole transcriptome at each dose, fit a curve to every responding gene, take each gene's benchmark dose — the concentration producing a defined small change — and summarise the most sensitive end of that distribution. Then convert the dish concentration to a swallowed dose by mass-balance modelling and in-vitro-to-in-vivo extrapolation.

Every one of those steps is an algorithm with published parameters. Curve fitting, model selection, gene filtering, summarisation, extrapolation — software, end to end. Nothing is scored by eye and nothing waits for a committee.

That makes this method structurally different from almost everything else in the vault, and it lands the same way the vault's Cell Painting map did, from a different direction: a method that computes a quantity without naming an endpoint. The comparison is worth being precise about, because the two are mirror images — Cell Painting produces a point of departure with no endpoint attached; this produces a point of departure with the endpoint left as "whatever the transcriptome noticed first." In both cases what is missing is not the arithmetic. It is the statement of what the number licenses you to do.

The step that is still missing, and it is the same step

Here is the honest limit. The tPOD pipeline computes a number without human judgement. What it does not supply is the criterion. Nothing published says how far below a transcriptomic point of departure a human exposure must sit before the chemical is acceptable, or what agreement with a chronic animal study a tPOD must reach before it may replace one. The authors position the output for "chemical screening and prioritisation" — ranking what to look at next — and that is precisely the honest scope.

So the blocker moves rather than disappearing: from "no rule turns the measurement into a call" to "the measurement is computed cleanly and nobody has said what it licenses." For a closed loop this is a genuine improvement — a continuous, ordered quantity is something an optimiser can pursue, where a hazard class is not — and it is not a solution. A loop that optimises a number nobody has attached a threshold to will produce a great deal of well-ranked evidence about a decision it cannot make.
