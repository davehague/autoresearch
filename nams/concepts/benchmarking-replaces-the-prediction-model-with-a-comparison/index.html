When you cannot say "is it safe," ask "is it worse than this"

The single most common finding in this vault is no agreed decision rule. An assay produces a clean, machine-readable number and nothing published states what number means hazardous. It sits on more than twenty maps and it is the reason the largest block of methods stops at automatable-open-loop instead of reaching closed-loop-ready.

The vault has been treating that as a research gap — somebody has to do the work of writing the rule, and until they do, the method is stuck.

Industry did not wait. This review documents, across 32 published sources, what the cosmetics and personal-care sector does instead: it swaps the absolute question for a relative one.

Why this is genuinely interesting for a loop, and genuinely not a free lunch

A side-by-side comparison is a decision procedure a machine can execute. Measure A. Measure B on the same plate, the same day, in the same laboratory. Compare. It requires no consensus threshold, no committee, and no reference database — which makes it, on its face, the cheapest imaginable route past the vault's dominant blocker.

The catch is the-reference-answer-is-a-marketed-products-safety-record, and it is structural rather than incidental: the comparison only means anything because the benchmark product carries extensive market experience data — years of consumer exposure and complaint history — which is not something an assay produces and not something a loop can generate.

So the trade is real but it is a trade. Benchmarking converts an interpretation problem into a provenance problem. The interpretation problem has no known solution; the provenance problem demonstrably does. But a loop treating a benchmark comparison as self-contained has quietly relocated its ground truth from the bench to a company's records.

Grading discipline, stated

This note is graded peer-reviewed-method because the claim it carries is descriptive — this practice exists, in these product classes, with these named reference sets, across 32 published sources. The review's prescriptive claim, that benchmarks can be used to evaluate the predictive capacity of test systems lacking a prediction model, is a proposal with no validation study behind it and is graded separately at in-silico-mechanistic. Collapsing the two would let a documented practice lend its weight to an untested recommendation.

regulatory_status: none is correct and worth stating plainly: no Test Guideline, defined approach or validated framework specifies benchmarking. The authors say so themselves.

Bias watch. Both authors work for a contract testing laboratory whose service catalogue is precisely the assay list above. A testing laboratory arguing for a second comparator arm in every study is an interested party, notwithstanding the paper's "no competing interests" declaration.
