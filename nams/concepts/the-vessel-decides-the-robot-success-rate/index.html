The vessel decides the robot success rate

Five wet-lab manipulation tasks, 100 episodes each, two general robot-learning policies. The results do not sort by how complicated the biology is. They sort by what shape the container is:

Taskπ0SmolVLA
Centrifuge lid closing96%98%
Centrifuge lid opening90%96%
Tube pickup59%95%
Petri dish placement52%96%
Petri dish take-out34%66%

A centrifuge lid is rigid, hinged, approached from a fixed pose, and it works. A Petri dish is round, flat, low-friction and transparent, and it is where the numbers fall apart.

This independently reproduces, in success rates, what transparent-labware-perception-fails-on-tubes-and-flasks-not-plates found in pose-estimation error. That source measured a perception benchmark — how accurately a model locates transparent plasticware — and found the failure was shaped: plate-like and rack-like objects solved, tube-like and flask-like objects not. This is a different group, a different task, a different metric, and the same ordering. Two independent measurements agreeing on the shape of a failure is the strongest support this vault recognises.

And reliability compounds, which is where this becomes a statement about loops rather than about robots. At the best rate here (98%) a 20-step protocol runs to completion 67% of the time. At the Petri-dish rate (66%) it completes 0.03% of the time. A closed loop is a long chain of steps, and the chain is priced by its worst link, repeatedly.

⚠ Read the honest ceiling before using these numbers. They are simulation — the Pipette platform, which has no surface tension, no condensation and no liquid film — and the simulator was built by the same group that built the system under test. Three tasks were run on a real robot arm and are reported qualitatively, with no per-task real-robot success rates in the body. Real screening laboratories engineer around all of this with fixed nests, barcoded racks and known slot coordinates, so this bounds the general case — a robot handed an unfamiliar bench — not a deployed plate handler.

The consequence for this vault's ladder is the uncomfortable part. Its closed-loop-ready population is overwhelmingly well-plate-based, and the ladder was built on readout objectivity and decision rules — never on whether a machine can pick the vessel up. The two rankings keep agreeing for a reason the ladder does not contain. That is not confirmation; it is a second, independent reason the same methods look easy, and the methods with the strongest claim to human physiological relevance — liver chips, spheroids, anything in a flask or a perfusion reservoir — sit in the worst part of both curves.
