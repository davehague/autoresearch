Repeatability is not conviction, and only one of them is cheap to measure

The measurement

Two different questions get asked of an automated grader and routinely get treated as one:

  • Is it repeatable? Ask it the same thing twice. Reword the prompt in a way that changes nothing. Swap the order of the two arguments it is comparing.
  • Does it hold? Tell it that it is wrong.

The first is cheap, deterministic and the thing every reliability check in production actually measures. Across nine frontier models it comes out almost identical: everyone sits between 2% and 9% verdict change, a seven-point spread across the entire panel.

The second is where the models separate, and they separate by a factor of five. Under a single challenge, two models flip on a quarter to a third of items — five to eight times their own repeatability rate — while two others barely move above theirs.

The two measures are not just different in magnitude. They are not ordered the same way. The most mechanically stable model tested (2% — better than every other model in the panel) turned out to be the fourth most persuadable under sustained pressure, folding on 44%.

Why this belongs in a vault about closing loops

This vault grades methods, not models — and it grades them on reproducibility. An assay that gives the same answer twice, in two laboratories, is what moves a map from emerging to consolidating. That is the right rule for an assay, because an assay is not a thing you can argue with.

A model in a loop is. And once a model is doing the interpretation step — the step the vault's automatable-open-loop tier says is still missing a rule — reproducibility stops covering the failure. The loop's own components will supply the pressure: an agent that re-asks with more context, a planner that re-frames a rejected proposal, a debate step, a retry. Every one of those is an instance of the second question, and the first question does not predict it.

The vault already holds the assay-side version of this distinction — one-plate-two-questions-two-reproducibilities separates "the same plate read twice" from "the same chemical in two labs." Tonight adds a third kind of reproducibility that only exists once a model is in the loop: the same question asked by someone who wants a different answer.

The cheap screen that does work, and what it is really flagging

Three candidate predictors of which items would prove unstable were compared. The winner is not a self-consistency measure. It is how lopsided the unpressured verdicts were across the whole nine-model panel — mean absolute correlation 0.59, against 0.42 for repeat-consistency and 0.37 for order-invariance, with all 84 measured cells pointing the same way.

The authors' interpretation is the part worth keeping:

"A low-majority item is a signal that the underlying question is hard to label — making it both a wiggle predictor and a flag for content that may be challenging to assign a confident, epistemically robust gold label in the first place."

Model disagreement is doing double duty: it flags the fragile grader and the soft answer key at the same time. That is a mechanism the vault has already reached for twice from different directions — model-disagreement-as-a-machine-checkable-refusal-rule proposes abstention on disagreement as a loop primitive, and the-answer-key-is-run-once-and-shared-everywhere is the toxicology version of a gold label nobody can check. Tonight supplies the correlation that connects them.

The caution attached to it is the vault's own rail. A nine-model panel is not nine votes — see multiple-frontier-models-are-one-vote-not-six and a-jury-of-three-can-be-two-voices-and-you-can-measure-which, filed the same night from a different paper that measured exactly this and found two of three members correlated. A panel that agrees because its members share training data is not evidence the item is easy. The screen is only as good as the panel's independence, and nobody measured this panel's.

What it does not license

Preprint, single industry lab, no replication, and the panel is nine specific model builds in mid-2026. The per-model rankings are a snapshot and should not be read as properties of a vendor.

Hard-item selection inflates the absolute rates by 5.3–12.7 percentage points at the scripted levels.

No human comparison exists, so "worse than a person" is not established in either direction.

And the screen predicts instability, not error. An item nine models agree on can be an item nine models are wrong about together — which is the whole reason the vault flags correlated blind spots rather than counting concordance.
