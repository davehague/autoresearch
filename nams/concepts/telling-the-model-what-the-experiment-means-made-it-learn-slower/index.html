The knowledge is not being misunderstood. It is being obeyed.

The experiment is a clean one and its result is the opposite of what everyone deploying these systems assumes. Run 55 iterative design tasks twice, changing exactly one thing.

  • Domain-aware: the prompt says what this is — "CHO antibody expression titer" — and the parameters keep their real names, units and categories (cell_line: HEK293T, HEK293, …).
  • Domain-agnostic: "optimize a black-box function." Parameters renamed X1, X2, C1: A, B. Same ranges, same cardinalities, all meaning stripped.

Identical oracle, identical parameter space, identical feedback. The only variable is whether the model knows what it is looking at.

Telling it made it worse. On the 16 biology tasks where the feedback can in principle identify the best answer, domain-aware prompting matched the published-best design 10.4 percentage points less often by round 30 (p = 0.004). Seven of eight models. Not one or two.

The three controls that make this a finding rather than an artifact

1. It is not a worse starting guess — it is a worse response to evidence. Round-1 match rates are statistically indistinguishable between conditions (+0.01, p = 0.60). Domain knowledge does raise the round-1 oracle score by 3.78 percentage points, so the priors genuinely help you start well. What differs is the climb: the round-1-to-round-30 improvement is 11.3 percentage points smaller with domain knowledge (p = 0.004). The paper's phrasing: "a feedback-responsiveness gap, not an initial-quality one."

2. It is not under-exploration. Parameter-space diversity is higher under domain-aware prompting. The model explores more and still lands on the field's usual answer.

3. And the sign flips on the control set. On the 10 education tasks — built from randomised controlled trials where the literature consensus generally is correct — domain-aware prompting wins (73% pair-win against 42% on biology). That is what rules out "domain text confuses the model." The model is not degraded by the semantics. It is anchored by them, and anchoring only costs you when the anchor is in the wrong place.

The worked case, which makes it concrete

A microalgae biomass task. The published optimum is a raceway pond at 1.13 g/L. The reactor type the literature usually uses is a photobioreactor.

  • Domain-aware: sat on photobioreactor for 57.5% of rounds; reached raceway pond on 6.7%.
  • Domain-agnostic, given the same numbers with the labels removed: explored its way to raceway pond on 84.2% of rounds.

Narrow to the 6 tasks where the literature-typical answer differs from the published best, and the effect is 6 of 6 reversed — 36.1% of iterations on the right answer without domain context, 20.7% with it (p = 0.031). On 4 of those 6, the oracle actively rewards the published best above every more-common literature value, so the feedback telling it to move was there and was not taken.

The authors close every obvious escape: search was disabled (and forcing it on for one model does not rescue the condition); the effect persists on papers published before the likely training cutoff; three prompt-template perturbations do not change the direction.

⚠ Why this is the sharpest thing this vault has read about a toxicology loop

automation-experiment-selection already records that the missing decision rule blocking the back of the loop on twenty-plus method maps also blocks the front — a selection policy needs to know how much a cheap measurement tells you about an expensive outcome, and toxicology has never published that number.

Tonight adds a second obstacle at the front, and it is the opposite of a missing input. It is an input that hurts.

Regulatory toxicology is the most literature-anchored field this vault touches. Thirty years of standard test systems, standard concentrations, standard reference chemicals, standard positive controls, standard thresholds — and a validation culture that explicitly rewards agreement with what has been done before. A language model primed with all of that is, on this evidence, more likely to reproduce the field's habitual answer and less able to be moved off it by its own data.

And the check that caught it here does not transfer. The audit works because a published-best datapoint exists to score against. In regulatory toxicology the correct answer is the object being sought; there is no column to compare the proposal to. This is the same structural problem the vault recorded on 2026-08-13 — a discovery system's mechanistically-wrong-but-numerically-perfect law was detectable only because the benchmark was synthetic and the truth was known (numerically-right-and-mechanistically-wrong-with-a-number-on-it). Second instance in two weeks: a real failure mode of AI-driven science, measured, with a detector that regulatory toxicology cannot run.

Practical reading, and it is cheap. The obvious deployment choice — give the model everything it knows about the domain — is on this evidence a testable liability, not a free win. Running both prompt conditions costs one extra pass.

Brakes

Not peer reviewed. Industry-authored (Pareto.ai), with the mitigation that the finding is bad for the product category. And no experiment is run anywhere: every "outcome" comes from a regression model fitted to the same published literature the domain-aware prompt is anchored to. The published-best audit is the authors' own defence against that circularity and it is a good one — but it is not a wet lab, and the vault's standing brake applies: a rule fitted to a corpus will agree with that corpus (quantitative-aop-probabilistic-modelling).
