Nothing is scoring the thing that makes a loop a loop

The vault's definition of a closed loop has four legs: a robot runs the assay, an instrument reads it, a rule turns the reading into a call, and something decides what to run next — then the cycle repeats and a model updates. The vault grades the third leg exhaustively (that is loop_readiness), has one source on the first leg's reliability, and has repeatedly logged that it has no way to grade the fourth.

Bisht et al.'s fourth argument is that neither does anybody else. Most scientific benchmarks measure single-turn prediction accuracy and lack feedback from physical experiments back to the computational model. A benchmark that presents a question and scores the answer cannot distinguish a system that would have revised its approach after seeing a result from one that would not — because it never shows it a result.

Which means the field's headline capability numbers are silent on autonomy. A model can top a scientific-QA leaderboard and have no demonstrated ability to run the only operation a self-driving lab performs: take an outcome, update, choose differently. Nothing in the evaluation penalises a system for treating each turn as independent, and Bisht et al.'s companion argument about reward design shows how that bites in practice — "A system optimizing a fixed scalar reward such as agreement between simulation and experiment might discard a candidate material that performs significantly worse in experiments than simulation instead of using it to improve their simulation methodology." The disagreement between prediction and reality is the most informative thing that can happen in a loop, and a scalar-reward optimiser throws it away.

This is the third distinct hole the vault has found in its own axis, and they are stacking in a pattern.

Three ungraded dimensions, and the vault's table shows none of them. The loop-readiness view reads as a ladder with 24 maps at the top and "loop infrastructure" as the only thing missing. On the evidence now accumulated the honest statement is: 24 methods have a machine-executable decision rule, and the vault has no measurement of whether a machine can run the assay reliably, no measurement of whether a machine can choose the next one well, and — per FDA — a rising evidentiary burden for every rung it climbs (regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility).

Tonight's small counterexample, worth holding beside this. OECD TG 492B's decision rule contains a mechanically evaluable retest trigger — repeat the test when viability lands within ±5% of the cut-off (tg492b-decision-rule-contains-its-own-retest-trigger). That is a one-step experiment-selection rule inside a regulatory guideline: uncertainty sampling with a budget of three. It is the smallest possible version of the fourth leg, and it is the only instance the vault holds of the fourth leg appearing in a regulatory document rather than in an AI paper.
