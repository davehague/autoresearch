The cells are alive and the rhythm is wrong

The finding

Twelve cancer drugs were run on human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived heart muscle cells, over 120 hours, with two independent readouts:

  • impedance-based viability — are the cells still there and still attached? (a survival measure)
  • multielectrode array — what does the electrical activity of each beat look like? (a function measure)

Four of the twelve "exhibited minimal to no significant reduction in impedance signals". The cells were fine.

For sunitinib and erlotinib, the electrical recording was not fine: significant FPDcF prolongation — the field potential duration, rate-corrected, is the dish equivalent of a prolonged QT interval on an ECG, and QT prolongation is the classic precursor to the fatal rhythm called Torsades de Pointes. The classifier scored both above its risk threshold at exposures of 72 hours and longer.

The authors' conclusion: "These findings highlight a critical limitation of relying solely on viability-based screening… their cardiotoxicity may manifest as functional impairments rather than immediate structural cell death."

Why this matters beyond cardiotoxicity

Most of this vault's cell-based assays measure survival. MTT absorbance is a viability readout — it is the readout behind OECD TG 439 (skin irritation), TG 431 (skin corrosion), TG 492 and TG 492B (eye), TG 498 (phototoxicity in reconstructed skin). Neutral red uptake in TG 432 is a viability readout. CellTiter-Blue in the population-variability panel is a viability readout. The whole MTT column of the vault's closed-loop-ready tier answers one question very well: did the cells die?

This source is a clean demonstration, in a different organ, that "did the cells die?" and "is the tissue working?" are different questions with different answers. A drug can leave every cell alive and destroy the coordination between them.

That is not a criticism of the MTT methods — dead skin cells are precisely what skin irritation means, and those assays are measuring the right thing for their endpoint. It is a boundary condition on a whole class of readouts, and it explains why cardiotoxicity needed a functional assay before it could have a NAM at all: a survival readout has no access to the endpoint.

The loop angle

This is a readout a machine reads better than a person. Electrical traces at 9.6 kHz across a grid of electrodes, reduced to four numbers per condition, tracked over 120 hours. No morphology, no scoring, no microscope. In readout terms it is among the most loop-native measurements in the vault — closer to TG 494's electrical resistance than to anything scored by eye.

And it is a time series, which is new here. Every other closed-loop-ready method in the vault reads an endpoint — one measurement, or a fixed set of exposure durations as in TG 492B. This one produced its finding by watching a signal drift over five days: the risk score only crossed the threshold at ≥72 hours. A loop running this assay is not choosing which chemicals to test next; it is choosing when to look, which is a different experiment-selection problem and one the vault has not previously encountered.

Honesty

  • One laboratory. No independent replication. strength: single-study.
  • The vendor of the cells performed and published the evaluation and declared no conflict of interest.
  • Sunitinib's cardiotoxicity is not news. The paper's own reference list includes a 2007 Lancet paper on sunitinib cardiotoxicity. The claim here is that this platform detects a known liability that a viability screen misses — a plausible-direction result on drugs whose answer was already in the literature, which is weaker evidence than a prospective finding.
  • Four drugs showed no viability loss; two of those four were flagged. What the other two (idarubicin, cyclophosphamide) should have shown is not resolved in the accessible text.
