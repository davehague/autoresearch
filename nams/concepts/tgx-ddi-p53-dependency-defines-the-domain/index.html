When the applicability domain is a pathway, not a chemical class

Most domain_gate entries in this vault are chemical: OECD TG 435 screens out much of pH 4.5–8.5, OECD TG 249 is bounded to log KOW −4 to 7. A loop checks a property of the molecule and decides whether the decision rule applies. TGx-DDI's gate is a different shape — it is biological, and it is a property of the cells, not the compound.

The mechanism. The 64-gene signature is enriched for transcripts regulated by p53, the tumour suppressor that senses DNA damage and switches on a stress-response programme. TGx-DDI does not detect DNA damage directly; it detects the cell's p53-mediated reaction to DNA damage. Everything else follows from that:

  • The cells must have working p53. Many immortalised and cancer cell lines do not. The authors list the lines validated so far — TK6, HepaRG, MCF-7, KGN — and say functional p53 signalling must be verified before using any other model. They recommend a concrete QC check: confirm the stress-response genes ATF3, CDKN1A, GADD45A actually respond.
  • Aneugens are out of scope. A chemical that misdirects whole chromosomes during cell division — by hitting the spindle rather than the DNA — is genuinely genotoxic and genuinely dangerous, and TGx-DDI is not designed to see it. This is a structural exclusion, not a performance shortfall.
  • False positives are mechanistically predictable. Anything that perturbs the p53 pathway without damaging DNA trips the signature. The paper names kinase inhibitors (sunitinib) and protein synthesis inhibitors (cycloheximide). These are not random errors; they are the assay working exactly as designed on the wrong stimulus.
  • False negatives have a named cause too: transcription inhibitors (which suppress the very readout), and antimetabolites, whose "genotoxic effects may manifest at later time points" than the assay's window.

Why the vault likes this and why it is still a gate. A mechanistically-defined domain is better than a chemically-defined one in one important respect: it is explainable and testable in advance. You can ask "does this compound have a plausible p53-independent mechanism?" and "does my cell line have functional p53?" before you run anything, and both questions have answers. Compare that with an empirical applicability domain derived from a training set, where the honest answer to "is this chemical in domain?" is often "I don't know, it doesn't look like anything I've seen."

But an autonomous loop has to be told. closed-loop-ready never means "trust the decision rule on any chemical" — it means trust it inside the domain. Here the domain check has two halves and they live in different places: a compound-side check (is this an aneugen? a kinase inhibitor? a transcription inhibitor?) that needs structural or mechanistic annotation the assay itself does not supply, and a cell-side check (does this batch of cells still have working p53?) that is an ongoing QC obligation, not a one-time setting. A loop that skips either gets confidently wrong answers with high inter-lab reproducibility — the worst combination, because the reproducibility looks like validation.

The complementarity is the practical resolution and the authors state it: combined use with the micronucleus assay "enhances classification accuracy while retaining the ability to detect aneugens" (genotoxicity-in-vitro-micronucleus). TGx-DDI's blind spot is precisely the micronucleus assay's strength — a genuinely anti-correlated pairing, and the opposite of the failure the vault's blind-spot rail usually catches. Which makes it all the more important that the other half of that pairing is correlated: see tgx-ddi-micronucleus-share-the-s9-metabolism-gap.
