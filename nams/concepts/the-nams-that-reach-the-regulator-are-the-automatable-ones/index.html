The methods that get filed are the methods a machine could run

⚠️ This is a vault inference, flagged as one. Dao & Sadrieh do not mention automation, loop-readiness, robotics or AI anywhere. They classify NAM submissions by method type. The observation below is this vault's, made by laying their taxonomy against the loop_readiness axis, and it is offered as an open question rather than a finding.

The ordering is the same in both columns. The methods with the highest submission volume are the ones a machine could run at scale today. The methods with the lowest are the ones that need a human to maintain a perfused multi-tissue chip for weeks.

The obvious confound, stated first. Cheapness explains most of this without any reference to automation: an in-silico prediction costs a licence fee, a 96-well plate costs reagents, and an organ chip costs a specialist and a month. Automatability and cheapness are largely the same property here, and this note does not pretend to separate them. Regulatory familiarity is a second confound — sponsors submit what they believe reviewers will accept, and (Q)SAR has an ICH M7 precedent that liver chips do not.

Why it is still worth recording. The vault's working assumption has been that loop_readiness is an engineering property — a description of what a robot could do, orthogonal to whether anyone wants it. This suggests it may also be a selection pressure already operating on the evidence base. If the methods that reach regulators are systematically the automatable ones, then the regulatory record itself is being shaped by automatability, and the endpoints that need the hardest wet biology accumulate the least regulatory experience — which is the mechanism automation-self-driving-labs already carries under a different name.

That mechanism has a name in this vault, from Hartung 2025: agenda drift. Automation deprioritises the endpoints hardest to automate, which are exactly the ones where the animals still are. Hartung argued it prospectively, about where research effort would go. This is a fifteen-year retrospective of regulatory filings whose shape is consistent with it having already happened. Consistent is not the same as confirming, and the confounds above are not controlled for. Logged as a watch item, not a conclusion.

What would test it. A breakdown of submissions by toxicological endpoint rather than by method type — if the endpoints with the thinnest NAM submission record are the ones this vault grades bench-bound or human-in-the-loop (reproductive toxicity, repeat-dose systemic, carcinogenicity — the three the UK strategy also left out of every basket), the inference gets much stronger. That breakdown is not in this paper.
