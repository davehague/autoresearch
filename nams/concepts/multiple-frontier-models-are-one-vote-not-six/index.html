One vote, not six — the rail moves up a layer

The vault's rail, as written for assays. Two tests in one battery that fail on the same chemicals for the same structural reason cast one vote, not two. The danger is specifically automated: a human may discount a concordant battery, while a loop reads concordance as confirmation, trusts its rule, and iterates fast and confidently into the shared error.

Every one of the vault's eight blind-spot rows is about the ASSAY half of the loop. Two fish assays with no nervous system. Two phototoxicity assays with no biotransformation. Three endocrine reporters sharing the MMTV promoter. Two eye methods on the same corneal tissue. Nothing has ever been recorded about the half of the loop that decides what to run next.

The experiment. Bisht et al. posed two tasks to six frontier models — three Anthropic, three OpenAI — over 50 NeurIPS 2025 AI-for-materials papers, ten samples each:

  • Task A, convergence expected: given a summary of a paper's experiments, recover the underlying hypothesis. A determinate answer; high agreement is correct.
  • Task B, divergence wanted: given the full paper, propose novel hypotheses extending it. Here agreement is a defect.

The result: "inter-model similarities remain high despite the desired diversity in task outputs." The authors ran the obvious control — checking that the embedding model can distinguish outputs derived from different papers — and report "what we have discovered is true lack of semantic diversity." Their conclusion in full: "A research community that queries multiple AI systems for novel hypotheses is, from an epistemic standpoint, effectively sampling from a single model … The effective epistemic sample size, for generating directions the field has not already taken, is close to one regardless of how many systems are consulted."

The shared cause, and it is structural in exactly the way the rail requires. Two of them:

  1. A shared training corpus. All six models learned what a plausible hypothesis looks like from largely the same published literature — which records what worked, not what failed. Bisht et al.'s second argument is that tacit procedural and failure knowledge of laboratory practice is absent from that corpus for every model equally.
  2. Convergent preference optimisation. Post-training alignment pulls outputs toward annotator consensus in both provider families, and distillation propagates it: a "post-training optimised teacher inherits both the diversity compression and any additional biases" into its students.

Different companies, different weights, the same upstream cause of the same failure. That is the rail's exact criterion, and it is the reason two assays lacking metabolism are one vote rather than two.

The practical consequence for a closed loop in this vault's domain. The vault's loop is: robot runs the assay → instrument reads it → rule makes the call → something picks the next experiment. If the picker is an LLM, or an ensemble of LLMs, then querying more of them does not widen the search — it re-samples the region the literature already emphasises. The authors' own worked example: consulting multiple frontier models on which electrolytes to synthesise "would produce recommendations concentrated in the same families from each system independently," while "underrepresented chemistries and unconventional transport mechanisms receive no additional attention from any model queried."

Translate that to toxicology and it is a familiar shape. A loop asked which chemicals or endpoints to investigate next will propose what the literature is already dense in — the plate-reader assays, the well-studied receptor panels — and not the endpoints with no method at all. See mcnamara-fallacy-selects-the-endpoints-that-already-have-assays.

And the remedy is the one the vault already has. From Tox21, 2026-07-13: the fix for a correlated blind spot is a measurement that fails differently, not a bigger battery. Applied here: an ensemble of frontier LLMs is a bigger battery. What fails differently is a mechanistic simulator, an explicit coverage or novelty constraint over what has already been proposed, or a human with a reason to look somewhere unfashionable. Bisht et al. reach the same place from the other direction, proposing scientific simulations as verifiers and a centralized preregistration repository for all AI-generated hypotheses — the second being, in effect, a what-have-we-already-proposed index, i.e. exactly the diversity constraint an ensemble lacks.

Honest brakes, and they are substantial. A position paper, not peer-reviewed. One experiment: six models, 50 papers, one embedding model, materials science, not toxicology. Cosine similarity of embeddings is a proxy for epistemic independence — two genuinely different hypotheses can embed closely, and the paper does not show that embedding-similar hypotheses lead to similar experiments. evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory, strength: suggestive. The transfer to toxicology is a vault inference; the paper never mentions it. The finding earns its place here for landing on the vault's own rail one layer up, not for the weight of its evidence.

Rebutted by: the-instrument-that-audits-the-benchmarks-is-itself-unreliable — when two frontier models from two providers were used to judge the same transcripts rather than to propose hypotheses, they disagreed with each other substantially (sensitivity 0.74 vs 0.17 on one criterion, 0.93 vs 0.44 on another). This holds the claim for generation and pushes back on extending it to judgement: convergence on what models propose is not convergence on what they conclude, and the strong reading — "N models are always one vote" — does not survive the distinction.
