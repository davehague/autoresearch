The platform with the best human-relevance argument is the one nobody routinely runs

Ask which non-animal method most deserves to displace an animal study and the technical answer is straightforward: organ-on-chip — living human cells grown in microfluidic channels under flow, engineered to reproduce an organ's structure and mechanical environment. This vault maps the liver version of it and records something unusual for a non-adopted method: a real apical predictivity figure on a blinded panel of 27 drugs, catching 87% of the genuinely liver-toxic ones while wrongly flagging none of the safe ones.

Seventeen international food companies were asked what they actually use. Organ-on-chip was "not routinely used." What they run is conventional cell-based assays, computer models of what a chemical does, the Threshold of Toxicological Concern (the agreed rule for when no testing is required at all), and physiologically based pharmacokinetic modelling — the oldest and the cheapest options in the toolbox.

Two readings, and the vault should carry both

The deflationary reading, which is probably most of it. Food safety is a low-margin sector answering narrow questions about ingredients, not a pharmaceutical company deciding whether a candidate drug will destroy a liver. A microfluidic device is expensive, slow, low-throughput and needs a specialist to run — three of the four barriers this same survey names. Nobody adopts an organ chip to screen a flavouring.

The reading this vault should not let slip past. The methods in routine use are precisely the ones this vault grades highest on automatability — plate-reader assays and computational models — and the one that is not used is precisely the one it grades worst. The vault has already recorded, from an entirely different direction, that transparent-labware perception fails hardest on tubes, flasks and perfusion vessels while being effectively solved for flat well plates, and drew the conclusion that the push toward more realistic biology and the push toward automatability point in opposite directions.

Here is that same divergence measured in purchasing behaviour rather than in robot vision. The advanced platform is not being held back only by a robot's inability to grip a flask; it is not being bought. Two independent mechanisms, same direction, and this is the vault's first evidence that the effect is visible in what laboratories actually do.

Why the stance is pushes-back

Because the field's own advocacy — and parts of this vault's own framing — treat physiological realism as the axis along which non-animal methods improve, with adoption assumed to follow. This says adoption is running the other way, toward the simplest available option, in a sector that has already decided to use non-animal methods. That is a limit on how much a method's biological quality predicts its use, and it belongs next to the claim it complicates.
