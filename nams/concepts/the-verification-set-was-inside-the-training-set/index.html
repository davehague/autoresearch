The 52 compounds it "verified" on were its own training data

One sentence, in an otherwise careful paper, in the case study rather than the results:

"The screened dataset contained 52 compounds from the modeling set - 35 BBB+ and 17 BBB- chemicals; the model classified all of them correctly. Verified accuracy on known compounds illustrates the high reliability of the consensus model's predictions."

A model reproducing the labels it was trained on tells you it fitted. It tells you nothing about a chemical it has not seen, which is the only thing anyone wants to know. Reported as 52 out of 52 with the word verified attached, it reads exactly like an external test result.

Why this is worth a note rather than an eye-roll

Because the rest of the paper is the opposite of sloppy, and that is the point. The same authors:

  • de-duplicated the public dataset down from 7,807 compounds to 3,968, specifically because two-dimensional descriptors treat stereoisomers as the same molecule;
  • ran scaffold splitting — forcing molecules that share a core skeleton onto one side of the split — so the test set contains genuinely unfamiliar chemistry, and led with those numbers rather than the flattering cross-validation ones;
  • reported that class imbalance was making their models good at saying yes and near coin-flip at saying no, then fixed it and showed both sides of the trade;
  • cited a 2026 analysis finding that near-identical compounds in blood-brain-barrier datasets inflate reported AUC by an average of 13.3%, and named removing duplicates as "a critical step for building reliable and trustworthy models."

Two paragraphs after that last point, they treat 52 training compounds as verification. The failure is not ignorance of the principle — they wrote the principle down. It is that the sentence lives in an application section where the frame has shifted from "how good is this model" to "here is the model being used," and in that frame recovering known answers feels like a sanity check rather than a claim.

What it means for a machine reading the literature

This vault's central question is what an AI could run end to end, and reading papers is the cheapest part of that loop and the part most often assumed solved. A model extracting performance claims from this paper — for a meta-analysis, a training corpus, a model card, an agent deciding whether to trust this predictor — has two sentences to choose between:

  • balanced accuracy 0.81 on a scaffold split, which is a real out-of-domain result with a stated protocol; and
  • 52/52, verified accuracy, high reliability, which is a rounder number, appears next to a concrete application, and is wrong.

The second one is more quotable. There is no syntactic feature distinguishing them; telling them apart requires knowing that the 52 compounds came from the modelling set, a fact stated in the same sentence and easy to read past. Provenance-checking a claim is not a formatting problem, and it is the sort of thing this vault's stages/02-ingest does by hand every night.

What it does not do

It does not undermine the paper's result. The scaffold-split numbers stand on their own protocol, the applicability-domain rule (model-disagreement-as-a-machine-checkable-refusal-rule) is a genuine contribution, and the 27%-of-screening-actives-never-reach-the-brain finding is useful triage. The map's Status does not move on this note. It is recorded because the vault's job is to keep the graded claim and the overstated one apart, and because a single well-formed sentence is all it takes for the second to survive a citation chain intact.

The brakes

  • This is a reading of one sentence, not a re-analysis; no data was re-run, and the 52 compounds' effect on any headline number is nil, because they are not in any headline number.
  • Single study, computational only, no regulatory standing.
  • stance: pushes-back is against the specific reliability claim in that sentence, not against the paper and not against the method.
