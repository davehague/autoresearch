The third arm: a correct negative that is not a safety statement

Most genotoxicity method papers test two things: chemicals that damage DNA, and chemicals that do not. This panel tests three, and the extra arm is the one worth writing down.

ArmChemicalsWhat the assay should sayWhat that answer means
A — genotoxiclasiocarpine, cyclophosphamide, ethyl methanesulfonate, aflatoxin B1, benzo[a]pyrenepositivecorrectly caught
B — non-genotoxic, non-carcinogenicaflatoxin B2, 2-deoxy-D-glucose, D-mannitol, caffeine, ampicillinnegativecorrectly cleared
C — non-genotoxic carcinogenPFOA, diethanolamine, thioacetamide, phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate, DEHPnegativecorrectly answered, and the chemical still causes cancer

Why arm C is a different kind of test

A non-genotoxic carcinogen causes cancer without breaking DNA — by activating a receptor, by chronic tissue irritation, by promoting cells that were already damaged. Phorbol esters are the textbook tumour promoters. PFOA and DEHP work largely through receptor-mediated proliferation.

A genotoxicity assay that calls these five negative is right. It was asked whether the chemical damages DNA and the answer is no. But the output of that correct answer — "negative" — is the same string a chemical in arm B produces, and those two negatives mean completely different things. One says "this chemical does not appear to be a carcinogenic hazard by this route." The other says "this chemical is a carcinogen and this test was not the one that would find it."

Why this belongs in a vault about closed loops rather than in a methods appendix

A decision rule returns a label. It does not return which question it answered. An automated system reading a negative call off a genotoxicity assay has, in the arm-C case, a true result that is uninformative about the thing it was ultimately trying to learn — and nothing in the data distinguishes it from the arm-B case where the same negative is genuinely reassuring.

This is a different failure from the one the rail usually tracks. A tgx-ddi-micronucleus-share-the-s9-metabolism-gap-style correlated blind spot is two assays being wrong together. This is one assay being right about the wrong question, and it will never show up as an accuracy loss, because the assay's answer is correct. It shows up only if somebody asks what the battery as a whole is supposed to cover.

That is exactly what the vault's carcinogenicity coverage is for: carcinogenicity-cell-transformation-assay exists because the genotoxic/non-genotoxic split is the axis carcinogenicity testing turns on, and a battery composed only of genotoxicity assays has a hole in it the shape of arm C. The honest reading of a clean negative across a genotoxicity battery is "not a DNA-reactive carcinogen" — a narrower claim than the one a decision rule's output invites.

The rest of the panel design is also deliberate

Aflatoxin B2 in arm B, one bond away from aflatoxin B1 in arm A. Two mould toxins differing by a single double bond, opposite calls. That is an activity cliff — near-identical structures with opposite outcomes — and it is a direct wet-lab instance of the failure that in-silico-qsar-read-across carries a domain_gate for: a similarity-based applicability-domain check cannot flag a cliff, because a cliff is defined as two similar chemicals disagreeing. Here a wet assay is being asked to resolve what a structure-based model provably cannot.

Caffeine and D-mannitol in arm B are the compounds that make the adopted in-vitro genotoxicity battery's specificity as bad as it is — this vault records the in-vitro micronucleus assay wrongly flagging between a third and two-thirds of harmless chemicals (genotoxicity-in-vitro-micronucleus). Getting them right is the point of a transcriptomic biomarker existing at all.

None of this is scored. The panel is well designed and the per-chemical results are behind a paywall. What is recorded here is the design, which is public and is itself the finding.
