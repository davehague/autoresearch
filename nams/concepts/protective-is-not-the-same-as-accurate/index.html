The 89% and the 95.20% — what a one-sided criterion can and cannot establish

A point of departure is the anchor of a chemical safety limit: the dose or concentration at the bottom edge of where an effect starts to show. Regulators take it and divide down by uncertainty factors to get an allowable exposure. Lower point of departure → stricter limit.

Two widely cited analyses ask what would have happened if non-animal methods had set that anchor instead of animal studies:

  • 448 chemicals screened in EPA's ToxCast high-throughput programme: 89% got a lower point of departure from the non-animal data than from the animal study.
  • 5,801 chemicals on Canada's Domestic Substances List: 95.20% got a protective value.

This is the strongest empirical argument the field has, and it is a genuinely good one. It answers the regulator's actual fear — if I accept this instead of the rat study, will someone get hurt? — with: on this evidence, almost never, because the non-animal answer is nearly always the more cautious one.

And it is a one-sided test. Consider a method that returns "hazardous at any measurable dose" for every chemical it sees. It scores 100% protective. It has also banned drinking water, and it has told you nothing. Protectiveness measures the direction of the error. It places no bound whatsoever on the size of it.

That distinction is not academic here. The vault holds an independent measurement of the size, from a different 2026 study on 35 priority chemicals (ivive-reproduces-the-animal-answer-for-liver-and-not-for-development): non-animal-derived doses landed within 15-fold of the published guidance value for the liver, and missed by up to 17,042-fold for developmental effects. Every one of those developmental misses was in the protective direction. Every one of them would count toward an 89%. And none of them is usable — a limit set 17,000-fold below the real one does not protect anyone better than a correct limit; it prohibits the chemical, which is a different decision made by accident.

Why this matters specifically for an automated loop. A closed-loop system needs a scalar objective, and "is it protective" is available, cheap, and computable at scale — the animal point of departure exists in a database, the model's prediction exists, one comparison gives you a label. A loop optimising for protectiveness will converge on conservatism, because conservatism is free under that objective. It will learn that predicting low is never punished. Nothing in the criterion pushes back, and the resulting system will be, in the paper's terms, a complete success and, in a risk assessor's terms, unusable.

The honest framing of these two numbers is: they establish that non-animal methods are safe to adopt from a public-health standpoint, and they do not establish that non-animal methods are correct. Those are the two different questions, and this vault's rule about never inferring one axis from another applies to criteria as much as to axes.
