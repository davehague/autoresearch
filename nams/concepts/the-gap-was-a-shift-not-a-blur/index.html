The gap was a shift, not a blur

Two ways a model trained on simulations can fail, and they need opposite fixes

A model trained on computed data and deployed on measured data is the standard bargain of AI for science: you can generate unlimited perfectly-labelled simulations and you cannot generate unlimited labelled measurements. When it degrades on real data, there are two explanations and the field's reflex is the wrong one.

  • Additive — real data is noisier. Fix: train on noise, or denoise the input. This is what everybody tries.
  • Structural — real data is systematically displaced. The peaks are in slightly the wrong place, every time, in the same direction, because of sample height in the instrument and temperature and which machine it was. Fix: align to the instrument.

This paper measured both, on the same data, and the result is unusually clean.

The measurement

The denoiser did nothing. A one-dimensional U-Net trained on synthetic degradation improved synthetic performance by +0.30 correlation — it worked, in the world it was trained for. On real spectra it moved the median from 0.318 to 0.315, Wilcoxon p = 0.49: "statistically indistinguishable from zero."

The shift search did everything. Searching ±1.0° for the peak-position offset raised median peak-aligned correlation from 0.146 to 0.385 across 306 held-out spectra — more than double. On the subset where the chemical system matched exactly, 0.205 → 0.482. On the weakest retrieval path, from 0.001 to 0.259, a path that had essentially no signal at all before alignment.

And real data beat more simulated data. A crystal-system classifier trained only on simulations scored 27.5% on measured spectra. Fine-tuned on real ones: 49.4% held-out. The authors' own read of the ceiling is worth quoting because it is the opposite of the usual claim — "the ceiling is set by the size of the real fine-tuning pool rather than by the training objective." Not a better model. More real data.

Why this belongs in a toxicology vault

Change the nouns and this is a description of the vault's own model layer.

A toxicology model trained on curated database records — hit-calls, harmonised assay outputs, cleaned dose-response summaries — is trained on something processed, standardised and slightly idealised relative to what the instrument in a specific laboratory on a specific day actually produces. The vault already holds the additive version of this worry: that models learn assay artifacts rather than hazard. This source supplies the structural version and, more usefully, a method for telling them apart — measure the obvious fix, and if it lands at p = 0.49, the problem is calibration and not noise.

The transfer is an analogy with a stated mechanism, not evidence about any assay. The vault should say that plainly. But the mechanism is general: a model deployed on instrument output inherits the instrument's systematic offsets, and no amount of synthetic augmentation removes an offset it does not know about.

The loop consequence, which is the sharpest part

The vault's Tox21 finding is that the robots arrived in 2013 and the loop never closed, because interpretation was never automated. This paper's opening sentence is the same finding in materials science: "its analysis is the rate-limiting step as laboratories automate acquisition." Two fields, no shared citations, one bottleneck.

And there is a specific warning for a closed loop. A loop is a single laboratory running fast, on one instrument. Its systematic offset is constant across every experiment it ever runs, so it never appears as variance — it appears as a stable bias that looks like a property of the world. There is nothing inside the loop's own data that can reveal it. The correction here required real measured spectra with known answers, which is exactly the external anchor a self-contained loop does not have.

That is the same structure as the vault's finding that an experienced laboratory silently modified an adopted Test Guideline protocol and only a second laboratory caught it. Different mechanism, identical epistemology: a single fast runner cannot audit its own systematic error.

Evidence weight

arXiv preprint, not peer-reviewed — the vault's tier ladder still has no rung for that document type, and this is the nth time it has been logged. Materials characterisation, not toxicology. One group. Against that, the execution is better than most: a frozen held-out partition of 534 spectra from a 2,696-spectrum corpus, a negative ablation reported honestly, and a limitations section that names its own failure rates ("roughly 50% of single-spectrum predictions remain incorrect"). Under the vault's automation-hype rail a system paper's claims about its own system are graded as sceptically as a method developer's claims about their own assay; this one earns some of that back by reporting what did not work. evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory, strength: single-study.
