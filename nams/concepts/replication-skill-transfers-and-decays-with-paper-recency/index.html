Something transferable is being learned — and every agent gets worse the more recent the paper is

Two findings from the same experiment, both relevant to whether an AI could ever run a toxicology experiment end to end, and they cut in opposite directions.

The transfer result. Faraday is a 27-billion-parameter model post-trained by reinforcement learning to replicate machine-learning papers. Evaluated on held-out scientific domains it never trained on — computational protein and structural biology, climate and earth science, materials and chemistry — it still beats both frontier baselines on 60% of tasks. The authors' reading: "the behaviour it acquired is not memorisation of a specialised procedure but a transferable way of approaching the underspecified task of paper replication." The uplift is a shift of the whole score distribution, not a few wins, and it survives prompt-optimising the baseline for 24 generations.

The scaling detail is worth noting on its own: the 27B model directs GPT-5.5 (roughly 5 trillion parameters, two orders of magnitude larger) as a tool, and the pair outperforms the large model working alone. What was trained is the outer layer — deciding what to investigate, scoping an experiment to a budget, judging when a replication is done — which is the layer a laboratory loop would need and the layer this vault files under automation-experiment-selection.

The recency result, which nobody set out to find. Papers published more recently are harder to replicate, for every agent tested. Scores decline by roughly 2.7 to 8.4 rubric points per decade depending on the model — the untrained base model degrades fastest (−8.4), Faraday slowest (−2.7). The authors offer two speculations: less information about recent papers in pre-training data, and higher compute requirements making the scale-down judgement harder.

Why that second finding is the one to keep. If capability on scientific work partly reflects how thoroughly the work has been absorbed into training data, then measured performance on published, discussed, replicated science systematically overstates capability on the thing an autonomous lab is for: work nobody has done yet. A regulatory toxicology loop is, by construction, at the recency end of that curve — new chemical, new data, no prior literature. The slope is a warning about extrapolating any benchmark number forward, and it applies to the win rates in the same paper.

Two limits, both stated by the authors. Every score in this study comes from their own rubric judge — see a-judge-consistent-with-itself-and-not-with-experts for what that judge is and is not. And: "Faraday did not have access to any physical lab equipment." The held-out "AI-for-science" split is other people's code in scientific domains, not other people's benches. This is a loop-move claim about the deciding half of the loop only; nothing here touches an instrument.
