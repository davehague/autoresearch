0.97 for the plan, 0.71 for the world, 0.47 for the small pipette

AEGIS measures two things on one robot, and the gap between the scores is more informative than either.

Checking the plan before it runs: F1 0.97. Nearly every genuine violation caught, almost no false alarms. Software reasoning about software, with a written-down rule set.

Watching the robot actually do it: F1 0.71, average precision 0.89, area under the ROC curve 0.80 — under a leave-one-plate-out evaluation, meaning the monitor never saw the plate it was judged on. In English: roughly seven in ten physical failures caught, with a real rate of good dispenses wrongly flagged. And on the p20 — the pipette that handles the smallest volumes — F1 falls to 0.47, which is close to useless.

Why the ordering is the wrong way round for a loop

The small-volume case is the one automated assays care about most. Miniaturisation is the entire economic argument for high-throughput screening: 1536-well plates exist because small volumes are cheap. The monitoring works worst precisely where the assays are densest, for the plainest possible reason — at microlitre and sub-microlitre scale there is less to see.

So the assurance available to an automated laboratory is inversely proportional to its throughput ambition.

What this fixes in the vault's account of the loop

automation-self-driving-labs has held, since 2026-07-22, that this vault "has been grading the decision half of the loop while taking the execution half on trust." The Pipette benchmark showed general wet-lab manipulation at 37–72% task success in a simulator. The GPT-5 cell-free-synthesis campaign supplied the first production error rate — 2 of 480 AI-designed plates with fundamental design flaws, both caught after the fact because the failure was loud.

AEGIS is the first source that measures catching them before and during, rather than after. That closes part of the schema gap logged on 2026-07-22. It does not close it comfortably: the number that comes back says the catching is mediocre.

And the loud-versus-silent distinction from the GPT-5 result applies here directly. A missing tip is loud — AEGIS catches it deterministically. A partial dispense is quieter and needs a second model to vote. Transparent water is silent and cannot be seen at all. In a hazard-testing loop, the silent failure is the one that matters, because the result it produces is a plausible negative — a chemical that looks safe because half the dose never left the tip.
