Closing a loop and validating one are different things — and only one system in the surveyed corpus does the second

The vault's loop_readiness ladder asks two questions of an assay: is the readout machine-readable, and is there a fixed, deterministic data-interpretation procedure? This survey supplies the missing third question from the other literature's side, and it is sharper than either: when the loop acts on the answer, what outside the loop said the answer was right?

Ding et al. split closed-loop autonomy in two, and the split is the contribution:

  • L4-m (mechanical) — the loop feeds results back, but the trigger is "a metric or constant-fit, not demonstrated revision of a scientific hypothesis." The system consulted itself.
  • L4-v (validated) — the loop "revises a scientific hypothesis against an outside oracle (a physical measurement or independent checker)."

Nine of 24 systems reach L4. Seven are mechanical. One is author-claimed with no external check. One is validated — CAMEO (Kusne et al. 2020), a Bayesian active-learning materials platform in which the physical measurement is the in-loop signal selecting the next experiment — and it predates large-language-model agents.

The two near-misses are the instructive part, because both look closed from outside:

  • MASTER optimises a computational success metric and adds a human expert read after the run. "The physical world never gates its next hypothesis," so it is coded mechanical.
  • ChatBattery does close against a wet-lab synthesis — but the experiment "confirms a final nominated candidate rather than driving each iteration," so the wet lab is "a terminal check, not an in-loop oracle," and its overall autonomy is coded L3.

Why this matters to a toxicology vault whose subject is not in the corpus. The corpus is computational AI/ML research only — no assay, no regulated endpoint, no bench. But the distinction transfers exactly, and it re-describes what this vault has been grading all along. An adopted OECD Test Guideline with a published decision rule and an instrument readout is a physically anchored in-loop oracle. OECD Test Guideline 439's rule — is lab-grown human skin tissue less than 50% viable after exposure — is a check the generating system cannot author: the tissue either died or it did not. That is precisely the property the survey finds missing from every language-model-era autonomous science system it coded.

So the vault's closed-loop-ready tier and this survey's L4-v are the same property arrived at from opposite directions — and the two literatures reach opposite states. Toxicology has 26 assays with an external anchor and no autonomous system running them; autonomous science has the systems and, in this sample, one anchor, from 2020.

The rebuttal it carries. Any claim in this vault that an autonomous-science platform "closes the loop" is now graded against a published coding rule that says most such claims mean re-runs on its own number. Applied to the vault's own infrastructure maps: Tox21's robotic screening (automation-tox21-qhts-platform) is not even L4 — it is a fixed library against a fixed panel with no feedback at all — which makes it, unhelpfully, more honest than a system that feeds its own metric back and calls the result discovery.
