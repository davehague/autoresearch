Which model is better depends on which validation you believe

The four tests, in order of how easy they are to pass.

  1. Stratified cross-validation. Shuffle every record, hold some out, predict them. Records from the same source paper end up on both sides of the split — so a model can succeed partly by recognising which study a record came from, since one lab's records share a protocol, a cell batch, a dispersion method and a technician.
  2. Article-grouped cross-validation. All records from one paper stay together on one side.
  3. Leave-one-article-out. Train on every paper but one; predict that one. Simulates meeting a new study.
  4. Leave-one-family-out. Hold out a whole class of nanotube. Simulates meeting a new material.

The result. "Random Forest showed higher point estimates under conventional stratified validation, whereas Logistic Regression provided comparatively stable discrimination under stricter grouped-validation settings." The flexible model wins when it can leak study identity across the split; the simple one wins when it cannot. The authors name the mechanism in their own methods: they used the stricter schemes specifically "to mitigate overoptimistic estimates arising from within-study similarity."

Why this is the vault's own argument, measured

The vault's standing position is that reproducibility is not correctness. Its sharpest statement is a cell transformation assay where four independent laboratories agreed with each other and the animal test they were replacing disagreed with all four — reproducible and wrong. Here that shape appears in software: a model reproduces its training distribution beautifully and the reproduction is the artefact.

ml-model-reporting-reproducibility grades the toxicology machine-learning literature's reporting practice and records its blocker as accountability, not accuracy — the models may work and the papers frequently do not let you check. This source is a small, unglamorous instance of checking, and the check changed the answer. A paper reporting only the stratified number would have reported a better model and a worse one.

The ablation is the more actionable half

"Ablation-based stress testing identified high-dose non-toxic A549/MTT observations as a major source of false-positive toxicity predictions, particularly for Random Forest."

Translated: records where a lot of nanotube was applied to a common human lung cell line, measured with the most common viability dye, and the cells lived anyway are the cases the model calls toxic and should not. The model learned the reasonable rule "more dose, more death," and this combination is where the rule fails.

That is a checkable query, not a caveat. An automated system training on literature-derived nanomaterial data will over-call toxicity in that specific region — confidently, reproducibly, and in the same direction every time, because the bias lives in the training distribution rather than in any individual run. A loop cannot detect a bias its own training data shares, which is the model-layer form of the same objection this vault raises about batteries whose assays share a blind spot.

Caveat on the caveat. With 11–13 source articles, leave-one-article-out means each fold trains on about twelve papers. The instability the strict schemes reveal is partly the honest signal and partly small-n; the source says as much by calling itself exploratory, and no per-scheme numbers are retrievable.
