FDA's seven-step AI credibility framework — and the two words that connect it to this vault

Read the grading before the finding

evidence_tier: regulatory-adopted records the standing of the sourcing instrument (the regulator's own primary document), not the standing of the framework. The framework is a DRAFT — nonbinding, "Not for Implementation," comments closed 7 April 2025, no confirmed final version. regulatory_status: proposed, strength: suggestive.

The framework

Credibility is defined as "trust, established through the collection of credibility evidence, in the performance of an AI model for a particular context of use (COU)." The COU "defines the specific role and scope of the AI model used to address a question of interest."

  1. Define the question of interest.
  2. Define the context of use.
  3. Assess the model risk (see fda-model-influence-penalises-autonomy — this is the step that prices autonomy).
  4. Plan the credibility evidence you will produce within that COU.
  5. Execute the plan.
  6. Document results and deviations from the plan.
  7. Determine adequacy for the COU.

Steps 4–7 scale with step 3. Nothing in the framework is a fixed bar — there are no numeric thresholds anywhere in it. It is a proportionality rule: "commensurate with the AI model risk and tailored to the specific COU."

The two words: "toxicologic studies"

FDA's list of AI uses that produce data supporting regulatory decisions opens with:

"(1) reducing the number of animal-based pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and toxicologic studies"

So the AI-acceptance question is not adjacent to the NAM-acceptance question. They are the same question, at the same agency. regulatory-acceptance-fda-nam-framework (CDER, March 2026) names AI models as a NAM category; this document (January 2025) is how a NAM that happens to be an AI model gets judged. The vault now holds both halves of FDA's position, and they were written fourteen months apart by the same centre.

The same unit of acceptance, confirmed from a second document

CDER's NAM guidance said acceptance attaches to (method × context of use × submission), not to the method — the finding that forced regulatory-acceptance-fda-nam-framework into existence. This guidance, on a different topic, independently uses the identical unit: the COU, defined as the model's "specific role and scope … to address a question of interest."

Two FDA documents, two subject-matters, one unit. That is no longer an idiosyncrasy of the NAM guidance — it is how the agency thinks. The vault's regulatory_status ladder, whose every rung is a property of the method alone, is measuring something FDA does not use.

Data drift — the requirement a closed loop cannot dodge

"the potential for the model's performance to change over time or across deployment environments when new data inputs are introduced and these inputs differ from the data on which the model was trained (i.e., data drift) requiring life cycle maintenance of these models."

Read that against the vault's definition of a closed loop: a robot runs the assay, an AI picks the next experiment, a model updates.

An active-learning loop changes its own input distribution every single cycle. That is not a side effect — it is the mechanism. The loop deliberately selects the next chemicals to be informative, which means not drawn from the distribution the model was trained on. A self-driving lab is, in FDA's vocabulary, a data-drift generator by design.

The obligation this creates — scheduled monitoring and periodic re-evaluation — is not an objection to loops. It is a concrete, buildable requirement, and it is one of the few places where the regulator's demand and good engineering practice point the same way. It is also the requirement most likely to be skipped by whoever builds the first tox loop, because it is the one that produces no results.

One caveat about the framework's provenance

It is adapted from ASME V&V 40, an FDA-recognised standard for the credibility of physics-based computational models in medical devices — models whose behaviour derives from known equations. The guidance lifts its "high-level key concepts" (question of interest, COU, model risk) onto AI models, which are fitted from data and have no such derivation. FDA says so openly. Whether a credibility apparatus built for finite-element analysis transfers to a neural network is an open question the guidance does not answer, and it is one of the substantive criticisms in the published commentary on this draft.
