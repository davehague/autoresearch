The vault's carried open question gets a fifth voice, arguing the other way

The question the vault has been carrying

Logged 2026-07-22: "The ring trial is being demoted by four actors and nobody has named the replacement. Interlaboratory reproducibility is the only rung in the vault's ladder that is hard to fake. Open question, carried."

The four were FDA CDER (will accept a fit-for-purpose NAM with no formal validation), UKCVAM (ring trials "or innovative ways of agreeing regulatory readiness"), Hartung & Kleinstreuer (a model that retrains cannot be validated by a one-time event), and Kopańska & Hartung's next-generation validation framework (ring trials too slow, wrong reference).

On 2026-07-25 validation-paradigm-nam-confidence added the sharpest version: the ICCVAM Validation Workgroup (March 2024, 17 US federal agencies) ends its five-step confidence framework with "interlaboratory evaluation (if needed)", and footnote 1 names the exemptions — "large scale roboticized high-throughput screening assays and machine learning-based in silico approaches." Those are the robot half and the model half of a closed loop. The vault recorded the consequence: the more automatable a method, the more likely it is exempt from the evidence this vault grades highest.

The fifth voice

Published in the same year, in Archives of Toxicology:

"Ring trials are indispensable to demonstrate the robustness and reproducibility of a new method." "Reproducibility requires a robust, standardised, and well-described laboratory protocol which is transferable to other laboratories. This must be proven via ring trials."

On the proposal to make them optional: "We explain why this is simply not an option."

And the silence that matters: the paper grants no exemption to automated, high-throughput or machine-learning methods. High-throughput screening appears in its abbreviation list and nowhere in its argument. It proposes no alternative pathway for them.

Who is arguing which side, and why that is the interesting part

Every previous bias flag in this vault records industry arguing for a lighter standard, or for its own method. This one points the other way, and the byline is worth reading in full:

Five of seven work for companies that pay for validation studies and would save money if ring trials became optional. They are arguing to keep the expensive requirement. The vault records that as a directional signal — against short-term commercial interest — and not as neutrality, because the same companies also benefit from a high barrier to entry for competitors' methods, and from regulatory decisions that are hard to challenge. The paper says as much in its own framing: "A lack of confidence … will hinder both regulators and industry. This can delay legal decisions … as they are likely to/may be challenged, on the basis of the robustness of the NAMs."

What the vault concludes, carefully

The question is not resolved. It is now properly contested, which is a better state than it was in.

  • Both documents are 2024. Neither cites the other.
  • ICCVAM's exemption is non-binding, US-only, hedged ("not needed or not feasible"), and ICCVAM still commissions ring trials for wet assays it cares about — it coordinated the thyroid microtissue interlaboratory study this vault holds.
  • Jacobs et al. is an opinion piece, explicitly labelled as one, with no standing beyond its argument and its evidence table.
  • They concede a real point in common: reliability and relevance "could potentially be separated", and ring trial design should modernise from binary agreement to continuous-data comparison. That is a change to how reproducibility is measured, not whether.

The vault's position: the automation exemption in ICCVAM's footnote is now a contested position rather than a settled trend, and the vault should stop describing it as the direction the field is going. What nobody on either side has done is address the specific case the exemption was written for — a closed loop, which is one laboratory running fast, and therefore has no between-laboratory variance to measure at all. See eight-methods-that-broke-when-a-second-lab-ran-them for why that is the awkward case.
