Zero toxicology, but a hard number on how far current AI is from reading a real lab

This vault's standing observation about the self-driving-lab literature is that it is overwhelmingly chemistry/materials, not toxicology, and that the crossover is the rarest and most valuable thing the sweep can find. BEAKER is squarely in the chemistry-only majority — its 1,000 expert-curated questions, built from real (not simulated) lab images and video, contain zero toxicology content — but it puts a concrete, large-n number on exactly the capability gap this vault's loop_readiness ladder is implicitly betting will close.

The number. A three-chemist human baseline scores 93.3/100 across four capability dimensions (perceiving affordances/planning action, identifying apparatus/judging safety, recognizing ongoing operations, reasoning over procedural footage). The best of 28 tested AI models — a general-purpose model, not a chemistry- or robotics-specialized one — scores 34.1 points lower. Chemistry-specialized fine-tuned models score lowest of all 28, and models purpose-built as "embodied brains" for robotic execution do only marginally better than general models and still trail humans by 48 points.

Where the gap concentrates. Not in recognizing things — multiple-choice knowledge tasks (naming apparatus, judging whether a setup looks safe) average 41.6/100, well above a 30.4% random-guess floor. The gap is in turning that recognition into a trustworthy physical action: locating a precise operable point (a stopcock tip, a pipette nozzle) and planning a feasible motion path averages just 9.7/100 on a 0-50 scale, by far the weakest of all 11 tested task types. This is a direct, independently-measured instance of this vault's own distinction between a machine-readable readout and a machine that can act on it — the same distinction that separates automatable-open-loop from closed-loop-ready on the toxicology side of this vault, now measured on the physical-execution side instead.

The authors' own caveat, worth carrying forward. BEAKER is offline, single-turn question-answering — no model acted on a real lab in this study, by explicit design, "to avoid executing chemical operations with unvalidated models." The paper states its own scores "should not be read as evidence of real SDCL [self-driving chemical laboratory] deployment capability." This map's standing finding that the loop layer itself remains unbuilt for any regulated toxicology endpoint is unchanged; this source adds an independently-measured capability ceiling for the AI side of that loop, in a domain adjacent to but not inside toxicology.
