The model asked out loud scored below a coin flip

The prerequisite nobody measures

A closed loop needs something to choose what to run next. Increasingly that something is a language model asked, in words, which of several options is most promising — the pattern the field calls LLM-as-judge. This paper checks whether the pattern can do the easier version of its job: not invent a good hypothesis, just recognise one, given fifteen plausible wrong ones for company.

"before a model can be trusted to propose new scientific ideas, it should first be able to discriminate correct hypotheses from superficially plausible but incorrect ones."

The number

Of every pairing of (the real hypothesis, one wrong hypothesis), how often does the method rank the real one higher? A method that shuffles the list at random scores 50%.

MethodScore
GPT-5, prompted to rank the sixteen candidates37.4%
Reading a model's internal confidence (softmax NLL), seven models68.8% – 74.3%
Reading raw-logit energy, best model (Llama 3.2, 1 billion parameters)82.2%

The authors state it without softening: "Listwise prompting of GPT-5 performs worst at 37.4%, falling below what random ordering would achieve."

Two things about that row are worth separating. On Hit@1 — is the right one first out of sixteen — GPT-5 scores 16.6%, which is well above the 6.25% of pure chance. So the model is not answering noise. But its ordering is systematically wrong: it is worse than chance at the pairwise comparison, which means whatever signal it is following actively pushes real hypotheses down the list more often than it pushes them up. The authors' own explanation for why this is possible is the bias they set out to route around: verbal judges "favor conventional ideas … while discarding genuinely novel hypotheses." A published hypothesis is, by selection, the one that turned out to be interesting.

This one is sharper than any of them, because it crosses zero. The others report a language model losing to a better method. This reports a widely-deployed configuration losing to no method at all — and the losing configuration is the one people actually ship.

The confound that could explain the winner, which the authors volunteer

Do not read this as "use logits instead." The authors state the alternative explanation themselves:

"gold hypotheses were written by domain experts and may simply be more fluent than the distractors. This raises the possibility that likelihood-based methods capture writing quality as well as scientific plausibility, which our current design cannot fully separate."

The winning method scores text by how unsurprising it is. The right answers in this benchmark are the published, edited, peer-reviewed ones. The whole result is consistent with the models detecting good prose. That is a competing explanation the authors could not rule out, said out loud, in a paper proposing the method — the vault's bias watch usually has to find this kind of thing, and here it did not have to.

The loop reading

The direct consequence for an autonomous lab: the cheap, obvious way to pick the next experiment — ask the best model, in words — is not merely unreliable in this setting; it is anti-correlated with being right. And because the vault's own standing worry is that automation compounds error rather than catching it, an ordering that is worse than random is worse than an ordering that is noisy. A random selector wanders. A systematically-inverted selector walks steadily away from the answer, and every step increases its confidence that the direction is good.

What it does not license

  • Preprint, not peer reviewed. One proprietary model, one prompting strategy, zero-shot, listwise. The authors say plainly that "different prompting methods or models could produce different results." The vault records a measured failure of one widely-used configuration, not a claim about LLM judges as a class.
  • Recognition, not discovery. "the task is closer to identifying a known hypothesis than evaluating truly open-ended scientific reasoning." The wrong answers were written after the fact by the authors.
  • The winner is post-hoc. 53.1% Hit@1 is "the maximum across 14 model-by-scorer combinations selected post hoc" — their words.
  • No chemical, no assay, no toxicology anywhere in this paper. Twelve academic disciplines and their published hypotheses. The transfer is the vault's inference.

Rebutted by: the-confidence-interval-that-was-wrong-quietly — a scope condition on how far an internal-confidence signal can be trusted, not a contradiction of the ranking result. That note records a system whose conformal prediction intervals, calibrated on simulated data and deployed on real measured instrument output, covered the truth 54.6% of the time against a nominal 90% — and raised no error while doing it. A model's own confidence being a better signal than its verbalised judgement (this note) is compatible with that confidence being badly calibrated in absolute terms under distribution shift. The distinction matters exactly where these signals are proposed to be used: ranking candidates by confidence survives miscalibration; gating an autonomous decision on a confidence threshold does not.
