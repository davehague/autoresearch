The model that reads the figure scores below a coin flip

What was being measured, and why it is not the usual benchmark

Most benchmarks that a model scores well on are exam-shaped: the question is written in words, the answer is recallable, and a model can succeed by having read enough. This one deliberately is not. Every question here was contributed by a working scientist and is anchored to a real experimental figure — a protein blot, a nuclear-magnetic-resonance spectrum, an electron micrograph, an X-ray diffraction pattern, a stress–strain curve — and the construction pipeline includes a step that deletes or rewrites any question a model could answer without seeing the image.

So what is being scored is the specific competence a laboratory loop needs and which almost nothing else measures: can the machine look at what the instrument produced and state what it supports?

The scores

Nobody passed. Best model 48.7%. Range across nineteen models, 15.9% to 48.7%. Flat across disciplines — chemistry 32.5%, biology 31.2%, materials science 29.0% — so this is not one weak subject pulling an average down. Refusals, non-answers and unparseable outputs were all scored wrong, which is the strict choice and the right one for a laboratory setting.

Specialising a model for science made it worse on average. Four science-adapted models: 22.2%. Fifteen general-purpose models: 34.9%. The best specialised model reached 29.0% and beat several mid-tier general models, so it is a tendency and not a law — and the authors note the same inversion has been reported in clinical medicine. Their reading is worth keeping: domain adaptation buys terminology and task format, and this benchmark is not asking for terminology.

Coordination is where it breaks. One sub-field label on a question: 36.2%. Two: 35.6% — essentially no cost. Three: 29.5%. Four: 28.2%. The cliff is between two and three. The authors' explanation is multiplicative compounding: a task requiring precise visual reading and domain knowledge and multi-step logic fails at the product of three individual weaknesses. Nuclear-magnetic-resonance spectra — which need exactly that combination — are the worst category across every architecture tested.

Tools helped a little and hurt in a new way. Web search plus a code interpreter, six top models: everyone improved, mean +4.6 points, best rising 48.7% → 52.7%. But the trajectory analysis found 145 errors caused by tool use against 411 corrections, and the largest error class — 40% of them — was evidence-integration failure. The worked example is the one to remember. Asked to read a peak off a spectrum, the plain model read 138 ppm and got it right; the tool-using agent searched the literature, found a value for a different compound, and answered 143. It preferred what it retrieved over what it was shown.

What the failures look like underneath

The authors' taxonomy, in their words and mine:

  • Perception. "The visual encoders of current MLLMs are pattern recognizers rather than measurement instruments." Good at "what kind of thing is this," bad at "what number is this." For anything where quantitative precision is non-negotiable, the authors call this "a fundamental bottleneck that cannot be resolved by improvements to reasoning alone."
  • Prior knowledge overriding the image. When the picture disagrees with what is common in training data, models defer to training — reverse-mapping from frequent concepts rather than parsing the structure in front of them. This fails precisely on atypical cases.
  • Over-extrapolation. "Models frequently interpolate 'missing' experimental steps and invent evidence to justify their final claims."

And the summary line, which is a general statement about where these systems break: "while the essence of scientific inquiry lies in the ability to make rigorous judgments regarding out-of-distribution phenomena, current models systematically regress to high-frequency patterns when confronted with unfamiliar evidence."

Where it lands in this vault

Nothing on any method map moves. No assay, no endpoint, no Test Guideline. This is a loop-layer measurement of the deciding leg, and it belongs beside the vault's other measurements of it — the agentic benchmark recorded 2026-07-29 whose weakest skill was the one a toxicology loop would use most, and the 2026-08-06 result that a right answer can arrive with wrong reasoning.

The specific thing it adds is that the previous measurements were of tasks — write this protocol, control this instrument, plan this campaign. This one measures reading the result, and the number is below half on questions that a domain postgraduate wrote and two other domain experts checked.

Two honest limits on how far that travels. It measures question-answering about published figures, which is a proxy for reading your own instrument's raw output rather than the same task; a plate reader hands you a number, not a picture of one. And the leaderboard will be obsolete within months — nineteen named models in August 2026 is a snapshot. The durable findings are the shapes: specialisation underperforming, the three-label cliff, and the tool-induced preference for retrieved facts over present data.

Bias watch. Built and published by Alibaba and the Qwen team; Alibaba's own Qwen3.8-Max is one of the nineteen models ranked, at fourth (41.8%). A vendor publishing the yardstick that grades its own model is flagged as a matter of course — and it is worth saying that this one places its own model mid-pack behind two competitors and concludes that everything, including its own, fails.
