1,533 papers, nine model systems, and four chemicals everyone agrees about

If h295r-cannot-receive-the-signal-that-drives-a-testicle establishes that the adopted assay is reading the wrong organ for male reproductive steroidogenesis, the obvious next question is what to use instead. This is the answer, and it is a well-evidenced negative.

What exists

Řehůřková et al. screened over 1,500 studies (1,533 met inclusion criteria) covering primary Leydig cells — the testosterone-making cells of the testis — plus immortalised Leydig cell lines, stem-cell-derived Leydig-like cells, 3D testicular systems and whole testis explants. Their targeted comparison ran across H295R plus eight testicular systems: primary Leydig cells, MA-10, TM3, mLTC-1, R2C, BLTK-1, and explants.

What most of them actually are, in one sentence from the paper:

"Most models rely on rodent-derived, cancerous cell lines cultured in two-dimensional monolayers, with limited representation of human and immature Leydig cells."

That is three independent domain problems stacked, and each one is the kind of thing this vault records as a domain_gate:

  • Rodent-derived — a species-extrapolation step, reintroduced by the replacement for the animal test.
  • Cancerous — an immortalised tumour line does not necessarily regulate hormones the way a healthy cell does. It is the same criticism levelled at H295R, and most of the alternatives share it.
  • 2D monolayers, few human, few immature — and immature is the one that matters most for the endpoint. Male reproductive tract programming happens in a developmental window; a model built from adult cells cannot see disruption of it.

And nobody agrees on what the models should say

23 reference chemicals were traced across four hormone outputs. Four behaved consistently:

"Forskolin, genistein, prochloraz, and ketoconazole showed consistent effects and may serve as promising reference compounds."

The other nineteen did not:

"data for most chemicals in testicular models are scarce or inconsistent—particularly for androstenedione and progesterone—underscoring the need for improved model standardization."

This is the pre-condition for validation, and it is missing. A ring trial needs an agreed reference-chemical set with agreed expected answers, because the trial measures whether independent labs reproduce those answers. Four compounds is not a reference set. So the field cannot yet run the study that would move it up this vault's evidence axis, and the authors say the regulatory consequence outright:

"most available models are from rodent origin and lack formal validation and OECD regulatory acceptance"

What this means for the loop

The readout is instrument-readable. Hormones are quantified by immunoassay in 85% of studies — an antibody-based plate read, a number out of a machine — with mass spectrometry (more specific, more definitive) in only a few. So the measurement leg of a loop is fine, and in a technical sense this assay family could be run robotically tomorrow.

What is missing is the rule and the referent. There is no agreed data-interpretation procedure, no standardised model, and no reference-chemical panel to calibrate against. That combination is exactly automatable-open-loop, the vault's most common tier, and the specific blocker here is unusually clean: an automated system could produce thousands of hormone numbers and would have nothing to compare them against. Nineteen of twenty-three reference chemicals do not have a stable expected answer.

This is the same shape as the vault's finding on the DART screens — machinery is not the constraint; agreement is. And it confirms rather than complicates reproductive-fertility-multigenerational-nams: the endpoint's most-studied corner is 1,533 papers deep and still has no validated method.
