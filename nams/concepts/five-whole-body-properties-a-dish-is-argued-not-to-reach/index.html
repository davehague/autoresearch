Five whole-body properties a dish is argued not to reach

The list, and why it is one list

Almost every method in this vault answers a local, fast question: does this chemical burn skin, damage an eye, break DNA in a dish, block a channel. Conde names five questions of a different kind:

PropertyPlain EnglishWhy a dish struggles
Whole-body biodistributionwhere the drug actually ends upthere is no body; a dish is one compartment
Immune handlinghow the immune system finds, coats and clears itthe immune system is systemic, not local
Organ clearancehow fast liver and kidney remove itneeds organs wired to each other
Chronic toxicitywhat months of exposure doesculture lifetimes are short and do not adapt
Therapeutic indexthe gap between an effective and a harmful doseneeds efficacy and toxicity in one organism

They are one list because each is a property of the whole system rather than of any part of it. An in-vitro method measures what a chemical does to the cells in front of it. None of these five is a statement about cells — they are statements about routing, timing and trade-off across a body. Isolate any organ and the quantity being asked about stops existing, which is what an in-vitro method does by construction.

The proposed remedy is not "keep the animals." It is context of use — decide per question, not per technology. That is the same principle FDA CDER's framework rests on (fda-fit-for-purpose-acceptance-without-validation) and the same principle context-of-use-first-is-argued-to-be-the-constraint-not-the-fix already records an argument about.

Two unrelated clinical fields, three weeks apart, drawing the same line

This is the part worth recording. On 2026-08-01 the vault ingested seven critical-care investigators saying non-animal methods "excel at interrogating isolated subsystems" but "cannot currently replicate integrated physiological responses" — naming "dynamic organ–organ interactions, immune–microbiome crosstalk, and adaptive systemic feedback loops" (a-clinical-field-says-the-whole-organism-question-has-no-non-animal-answer).

Now oncology drug development, single author, different continent, different question, and the boundary lands in almost exactly the same place: system-level properties in, part-level properties out.

Neither measured it. Both are arguments. Two arguments are not evidence, and the vault does not upgrade a claim for being repeated. What is recordable is the pattern of who is making it: in both cases clinicians and drug developers, not toxicologists — and in both cases about the endpoint this vault's own repeat-dose-systemic-toxicity-nam map has flagged as the hardest since 2026-07-06.

What it pushes back against, and what it does not

stance: pushes-back. The claim it contradicts is clinical-attrition-has-not-improved-in-six-decades — a registered systematic review pooling 32 publications from 1963 to 2017, which found that 80–91% of drugs entering human trials never reach approval, that the rate was highest in the most recent decades, and that roughly 77% of stated failures were safety, efficacy or pharmacokinetics: the three things preclinical testing exists to establish. That note is the strongest evidence in the vault that the animal-based comparator has a measured and unimproving failure rate.

The tension is real and it is not a contradiction of fact. Conde's argument is that the replacement cannot supply five specific properties; Filipova's finding is that the incumbent has been failing at roughly those same properties for sixty years. Both can be true, and if both are, the honest conclusion is not "keep the animal study" but "nothing currently answers these five questions well" — which is a coverage gap, not a choice between two methods. That framing is the vault's, not either paper's, and it is the one that matters for the loop: an autonomous system cannot be pointed at a question that has no reliable answer key, whichever side of the animal line the answer key would sit on. See the-reference-answer-was-built-by-committee-because-no-animal-test-could-supply-it.

What this note does NOT say. It does not say organoids are bad, it does not downgrade hepatotoxicity-liver-chip-mps or any other method map, and it does not move anything's Status. evidence_tier: in-silico-mechanistic, strength: suggestive — argued, not measured.

The conflict, and why it is not the usual one

The author is a co-founder and shareholder of a therapeutics company and sits on another company's scientific advisory board. funding_flag: [industry].

This vault's usual bias-watch case is a method developer talking up their own assay (shared/evidence-model.md, bias watch). This is the mirror image: an interested party arguing that the replacements are not ready, in a field — nanomedicine — where the value proposition of the product is a whole-body biodistribution claim, and where an accepted non-animal route would change the evidence a competitor needs.

Both readings are available and neither is provable from the text. Someone who works on biodistribution daily is genuinely the person best placed to know its limits; someone who sells a biodistribution-dependent product also benefits if the whole-body evidence requirement stays. The vault records the conflict and declines to choose — the same discipline applied to the developer-funded validations elsewhere in the vault, run in the opposite direction.

The loop consequence

If these five properties really have no in-vitro answer, then a closed loop can be built for the local questions and cannot be built for the systemic ones — not because the robot cannot run the assay, but because there is no assay to run and no reference answer to score against. That is bench-bound / no-nam-exists territory, the rung the vault reserves for a missing method rather than a missing rule (no-standalone-fertility-nam-replacement). Nothing here is graded onto that rung — the claim is too thin, from one page with no data — but it is the direction this note points, and the next source that measures rather than argues it will matter a great deal.
