The screening robot ran, and four chemicals in five came back with no number

This vault's standing account of the robotic screening platform is that its robotics were finished in 2013 and the thing missing is a rule for turning an activity concentration into a hazard call (automation-tox21-qhts-platform). Twenty-four maps in this vault carry a version of that blocker.

This is the first source to test what happens when you supply the rule.

The setup

In-vitro-to-in-vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) is the published, quantitative, machine-runnable procedure for converting "active at 3 micromolar in a dish" into "equivalent to a human daily dose of X milligrams per kilogram" — see pbk-qivive-toxicokinetics. It is not a weight-of-evidence judgement. It is arithmetic on a kinetic model. If the missing decision rule were the only thing standing between robotic screening data and a usable safety number, IVIVE is the rule, and the pipeline should run end to end.

Chiu, Roe and Rusyn ran exactly that pipeline on 41 substances the US EPA had already worked up through its Provisional Peer-Reviewed Toxicity Value programme, alongside three other routes to the same number.

The result

It produced a number for 9 of the 41. Four in five chemicals went in and nothing came out.

The comparison, on the identical chemical set:

Why this pushes back, and on what exactly

It does not refute the missing-decision-rule finding. It relocates part of the problem upstream of it.

The vault has been treating automatable-open-loop as a state where the generation half is solved and only the interpretation half is missing. On this evidence that is too generous for at least one downstream use. For 32 of 41 chemicals the generation half did not deliver an input the interpretation half could consume. A decision rule cannot rescue a missing measurement, and an autonomous loop that assumes its screening layer will always return something to reason about is assuming a thing that was false four times in five here.

The honest caveats, and one of them is load-bearing

  • The full text is gated (Elsevier, HTTP 403 at the browser rung; no open deposit). Every number here is from the verbatim abstract. Why the 32 failed is not stated — inactive across the panel, outside the solubility domain, and missing kinetic parameters are all plausible and have completely different fixes. The vault records the number and refuses to attribute it.
  • The comparison may be tilted. The 41 substances are ones EPA selected for quantitative read-across. If EPA picked them because read-across was tractable on them, then 83% is an upper bound on the expert route and the head-to-head is not a fair one. The abstract's phrasing points this way and the methods section would settle it. Recorded as an open question, not a defect.
  • One study, one chemical set, one endpoint (chronic oral toxicity). strength: single-study.
  • Coverage is not accuracy. A method that returns a number every time and is wrong is worse than one that refuses. The structure-only models span 46% to 100% coverage across three implementations, and the abstract reports no per-model accuracy — so the 100% end cannot be read as the best performer.

What it means for the loop

A closed loop needs its measurement step to return something the next step can use, and this is the first measurement in the vault of how often that holds. It held 22% of the time for the most automated route on this task.

That is not a reason to demote the platform — it does not touch how well the assays work, and the map's loop_readiness is a statement about the assay, not about this downstream application. It is a reason to stop reading "runs at 10,000-chemical scale" as "produces an answer for 10,000 chemicals."

The other half of the result cuts the opposite way and belongs in the same breath: the-cheapest-routes-cover-the-most-chemicals — stacking the purely computational routes reached over 90% coverage, within a factor of ten of the expert. The layer that scaled was the model layer, not the robot layer.
