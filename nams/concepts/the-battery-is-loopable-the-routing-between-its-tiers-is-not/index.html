Both assays could be run by a machine. The step between them could not.

The vault grades OECD Test Guideline 455 (estrogen-receptor activation, read as luminescence against a PC10 rule) and OECD Test Guideline 456 (the H295R hormone-synthesis assay, read as testosterone and estradiol immunoassay against a ≥1.5-fold rule) as closed-loop-ready, and correctly: instrument readout, published arithmetic, no human in the call. They are among the strongest cases in the vault for "an AI could run this end to end."

This study runs both, in sequence, and the sequence exposes something neither map records.

Tier 1 fired on 13 of 14 chemicals. The PC10 rule did its job deterministically and returned a nearly uniform answer.

Four chemicals advanced to tier 2, and the stated criterion is "strong ERα responses."

There is no published threshold for strong. Not in TG 455, whose rule tells you positive-or-negative and stops. Not in TG 456, whose rule tells you what to do with a sample once it is in the assay, not which samples to put there. The IATA is where the two are joined, and an IATA is by definition the expert weight-of-evidence structure — it documents a strategy, it does not publish a recipe. That is the whole distinction between an IATA and a defined approach like Guideline 497.

Why this is a new location for a blocker the vault already tracks

Nineteen maps sit at automatable-open-loop because no agreed decision rule exists — the vault's single largest blocker category. Every one of those is a rule missing inside one method: the readout is machine-obtainable and nobody has written down what number means what.

This is the same gap in a different place. Both methods have their rules. The composition has none.

That matters for a loop in a way the per-assay gap does not, because tier routing is where a testing strategy spends its money. A machine handed these fourteen chemicals and both guidelines would produce thirteen positives and then stop — not for lack of an instrument or a readout, but because the next question ("which of these thirteen is worth the more expensive assay?") has no procedure attached to it. A cheerful implementation would invent one, rank by potency, take the top four, and the resulting number would look exactly like this paper's while resting on a threshold nobody agreed to.

And the tier-1 result is what makes the routing step load-bearing rather than incidental. If tier 1 had separated 3 positives from 11 negatives, the routing rule would be nearly redundant — the assay would have done the discriminating. At 13 of 14, the screen has told you almost nothing about which chemicals differ, so essentially all of the selectivity in this assessment lives in the undocumented step. The loopable part carried little information; the unloopable part carried most of it.

The connection to the loop layer, which neither literature makes

experiment-selection-under-known-cost-is-the-loops-other-half recorded that cost-aware experiment selection needs a likelihood model — how much does a cheap measurement tell me about the expensive question — and that in toxicology that number does not exist. This is the concrete instance, in a real study, with adopted guidelines: nobody has published how much a PC10-positive receptor result predicts a ≥1.5-fold steroidogenesis result, so the transition between the vault's two most loopable endocrine assays is made by a person looking at a curve.

No status or tier moves on this note. Neither guideline changed; both assays remain closed-loop-ready, deservedly, and TG 455's access_gate (the VM7Luc4E2 reference cell line is unobtainable from its source) and the H295R domain_gate are unaffected. What changes is the vault's account of where the remaining blocker lives.
