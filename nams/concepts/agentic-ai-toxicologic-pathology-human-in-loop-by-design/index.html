Toxicologic pathology's own AI roadmap keeps the pathologist in the loop — and that is a costed choice, not just a caution

Why in-silico-mechanistic, not peer-reviewed-method

Hartung 2025 (self-driving-labs-chemistry-yes-toxicology-not-yet) is a single-author perspective in a peer-reviewed journal; the vault graded it peer-reviewed-method as the closest honest fit for a no-data reasoning document, recording the standing of the sourcing instrument. This source is one rung below that: a closed-door industry roundtable's white paper — not peer-reviewed, not an individual expert's argued synthesis, but a room of pharma, CRO, academic and regulatory representatives converging on a proposal. There is no wet validation, no data, and no single accountable author whose track record the reader can weigh. in-silico-mechanistic — reasoning only, no experimental grounding — is the honest floor. The vault's tier ladder has no rung for a roundtable white paper, which is a third instance of the missing "perspective/review" rung already logged for Hartung 2025 and FDA's draft guidance, and arguably a distinct gap of its own: those two were individually- or agency-authored; this one is a convened industry position, a source type the ladder has not encountered before.

What toxicologic pathology is, and why it is a strange place for "automation" to show up

A toxicologic pathologist reads tissue slides from an animal study under a microscope and writes the report that says what a chemical did to the organs. There is no plate reader, no absorbance value — the readout is a trained human eye recognising injury patterns, which is exactly the profile shared/evidence-model.md's loop-readiness ladder assigns to human-in-the-loop: "trained human judgment is load-bearing in the call." And it is a step that exists because there was an animal study — automating it does not touch whether the animal study happened.

The crossover, from a third direction

The sweep contract calls a source that sits in both the self-driving-lab literature and the regulatory toxicology literature the highest-value find available. The vault now holds three, each arriving from a different angle: Hartung 2025 is a validation-body insider addressing AI researchers; robin-semi-autonomous-mind-not-hands is AI builders running a biology discovery loop and reporting into toxicology's orbit; this source is the toxicologic pathology profession convening itself to ask what agentic AI should do to its own workflow. It is the first of the three where the tox community is the party calling the meeting, not the subject of somebody else's paper or the recipient of a vendor's pitch.

The architecture is human-in-the-loop by design — and that is the finding

The exact proposed target is "pathologist-in-the-loop report generation." Read literally, the field's own design for its AI future keeps the human load-bearing in the call, exactly where shared/evidence-model.md already places this endpoint on the loop-readiness ladder.

Read through regulatory-acceptance-ai-model-credibility, this stops being a soft caution and becomes a specific, priced design choice. FDA's draft guidance defines model risk as model influence × decision consequence, where model influence measures how alone the model is in the decision — and FDA's own worked example states plainly that a human independently cross-checking a result "would reduce the AI model influence." A "pathologist-in-the-loop" architecture is, by construction, a choice to keep model influence off its maximum.

This is the second system in the vault to land on the cheap side of that pricing rule by its own design, not the vault's argument. robin-semi-autonomous-mind-not-hands kept humans in the loop by leaving physical execution to bench scientists while the AI ran cognition. This roundtable proposes keeping humans in the loop by leaving the diagnostic call to the pathologist while the AI runs data orchestration. Two different fields, two different points in the pipeline, the same structural move. Neither source frames its choice as a response to FDA's pricing — Robin's builders describe it as a practical division of labor; this roundtable describes it as a governance precondition. Stating the convergence as a pattern is therefore a vault inference about convergent design, not a claim either source makes for itself, and it should be read with that brake attached. Two data points is early evidence of a pattern, not a trend.

The blocker list is the vault's own list, arriving from a new field

"Validation standards do not exist" — named as the second precondition — is precisely the vault's dominant, recurring blocker (no agreed decision rule) restated for the AI-orchestration layer instead of the assay layer. The vault has watched this exact gap block loop-readiness upgrades across the genotoxicity and carcinogenicity maps; here it shows up one level up the stack, in the software that would organize a pathologist's report rather than in the assay itself. Same shape, new layer.

The honest counterpoint — this is not progress on the vault's question

This source is AI applied to a step downstream of an animal study: making the reading of animal-study slides faster and more organized does not reduce or replace the animal study that produced the slides. Set against the vault's premise — closing loops that let a non-animal method stand in for an animal test — this is a different kind of progress, and a real one for the people running that step, but not a move toward the vault's central question. It is also a clean instance of the agenda-drift finding already recorded on automation-self-driving-labs: effort flows to what is automatable, and here the automatable target turned out to be a workflow-orchestration step inside the animal-testing pipeline rather than a step that displaces it. funding_flag: [industry] is carried on this note deliberately — the roundtable's room is stocked with the CROs and pharma sponsors who run and pay for toxicologic pathology today, and a proposal to make that step more efficient is also a proposal that keeps the underlying animal study commercially viable for longer, which is a tension worth naming rather than smoothing over.

Rebutted by: the-non-expert-in-the-loop-made-it-worse — the first measurement in this vault of what an in-the-loop human actually buys, and it is not monotonic: non-expert collaboration made an experiment-designing agent's output worse than full autonomy, and the expert arm, while clearly better than both, still missed omitted stir-rate commands that the machine's own self-check caught. The tension resolves into a scope condition, not a contradiction: "pathologist-in-the-loop" is a coherent safeguard because the loop participant here is a domain expert reading their own domain — which is exactly the arm that worked. What it cannot claim is generic protection. The measured expert failure mode was low-salience procedural detail rather than headline judgment, and the honest reading of both notes together is that expert oversight protects the call and not the parameters.
