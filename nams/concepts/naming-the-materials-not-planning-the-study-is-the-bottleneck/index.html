The models can say what to test. They cannot say what to test it with.

The headline of the-design-leg-scores-half-marks-on-its-first-benchmark is the total score. The interesting result is where the points are missing from, and it is the opposite of the intuitive answer.

Split experiment design into two tiers:

  • High level — which experiments does this claim require? Main experiments, ablation studies (strip out one component at a time to prove it was doing something), analysis experiments.
  • Low level — with what, exactly? Which datasets, with which splits. Which existing methods to compare against. Which measurements to report.

The high tier looks like the hard, creative, scientific-judgement one. It is the tier the models are better at. Across all seven models the gap runs 2.78 points the other way, and the ceiling is absolute:

"No Low-Level sub-dimension exceeds 3 out of 5 for any model, whereas High-Level dimensions frequently surpass 3.5."

Inside a single model: Claude 4.5 Sonnet scores 4.62/5 on designing analysis experiments and 2.01/5 on naming datasets. The authors' conclusion is a design instruction, not an observation:

"configuration accuracy—rather than planning logic—constitutes the core bottleneck … Low-Level configuration must be treated as a distinct, externally grounded process rather than an appendage to High-Level planning."

Why this is the same finding this vault already has, in a third medium

The vault has now recorded three independent versions of the specifics are the problem, not the plan:

  1. the-thinking-half-of-the-loop-is-worst-at-looking-things-up (LABBench2) — the weakest task family for frontier models is querying a specialised scientific database and extracting an exact record. Not reasoning about biology. Looking up a specific fact correctly.
  2. a-written-protocol-hides-twelve-watch-fors-and-forty-one-steps (ProtoAct) — written protocols omit roughly twelve unwritten watch-fors and forty-one unwritten physical steps each. The plan is on paper; the particulars are in someone's hands.
  3. This. Given a research claim, models describe the right shape of study and then cannot name the right materials.

For a toxicology loop, low-level configuration is not a detail — it is nearly the entire regulatory content of an assay. "Run a skin-sensitisation battery" is high-level and easy. Which reference chemicals, at which concentrations, which cell line at which passage, which positive control, which viability threshold, which of the five bacterial strains — that is what a Test Guideline is, and it is the tier where every model tested here sits below 3 out of 5.

The optimistic reading, kept honest. Low-level configuration is also the tier most amenable to being looked up rather than reasoned out, because for a regulatory assay the right answer is written down in a guideline. That is a genuine asymmetry in toxicology's favour compared with ML research, where the right dataset is a judgement call. It is also exactly the hopeful inference already logged in docs/open-gaps-log.md (item 6) as untested — and the same source that motivates it shows retrieval alone does not deliver it (see retrieval-degraded-what-structure-repaired).
