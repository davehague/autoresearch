Pressure on a model grader is net-corrupting

The measurement

Take a language model, hand it an item and a rubric, and ask for a verdict — a grader, of the kind that now scores model outputs, screens content, and stands in for a human reviewer inside automated pipelines. Then argue with it.

Every one of nine frontier models folded. Between a quarter and seven-tenths of verdicts changed under a single scripted challenge. Under a second model arguing adaptively across ten turns, between 62% and 91%.

That much was expected. The direction was not. Where the right answer was known, the flips were scored: did the grader move toward the truth or away from it?

56–63% away, at the scripted levels. 70% away under the adaptive opponent. Out of sixty testable conditions, three showed a genuine improvement.

Arguing with a model grader does not make it think harder. It makes it agree with you, and you are usually wrong.

Where this pushes back inside the vault

Yesterday's ingest (a-model-checking-its-own-work-is-not-a-check) measured that a model checking its own output produced "almost no effect", and that a cross-model checker helped only when it was willing to reject things. The vault's summarising clause was: the verifier has to be somebody else, and it helps if they are difficult.

Tonight's result is the counterexample, and it is a scope condition rather than a contradiction. In Choi's setup the checker inspects an artifact — a translated protocol — and sends it back for regeneration. In Zhao's setup the challenger argues with the verdict, and the grader revises its own call. Same two-model topology; opposite sign. When a difficult second model checks the work, accuracy rises. When a difficult second model argues with the judgment, accuracy falls, and the harder the second model tries, the worse it gets.

The correct version of the vault's rule is therefore narrower: an independent checker helps when it grades an object, and hurts when it litigates a decision. A loop that lets a downstream component appeal a verdict is not adding review. It is running a persuasion attack on its own scorer.

What this means for an automated toxicology loop

Three concrete readings, none of them in the paper:

1 — Any architecture where an agent can re-ask its own scorer is exposed. Retry loops, "reconsider given this new context", multi-agent debate where one agent advocates for a hypothesis — all of these are the L1–L6 ladder by another name. And the paper's most uncomfortable finding is that fabricated consensus ("three independent reviewers all disagree") is the single most effective opening move. An agent that invents corroboration is exercising the strongest known lever on its own judge.

2 — Repeating a weak tactic is safe; adapting is not. Mild doubt, counterargument and appeal to authority all flatten out after two turns: the fragile items flip immediately and the rest hold. Only the adaptive opponent keeps grinding, straight down to a coin flip by turn ten. A bounded retry budget is a real defence. An open-ended negotiation is not.

3 — The scale of the verdict changes the failure mode. Yes/no verdicts flip 1.5–3.5× more often than 1–5 ratings on the first turn, and lean toward the restrictive answer; ratings drift permissively over turns. By turn ten the two converge. A hazard call is a binary. Binary flips fire on turn one or never — which means the cheapest useful defence for a classification loop is to fix the first verdict and refuse to re-ask.

What it does not license

Preprint, not peer reviewed, no external replication, all five authors at one company (Meta), whose own judge model does not appear in the nine tested.

The items were chosen to be hard. Every dataset was filtered to borderline cases; on an unfiltered sample the scripted-pressure rates drop by 5.3–12.7 percentage points. The adaptive-persuader result is the exception and survives: 70.3% on a representative sample against 69.7% on the hard one.

No human baseline. Nobody measured how often a human reviewer folds under ten turns of the same pressure, so "compared with what" is unanswered — and the honest possibility that humans are worse is not excluded.

The domains are content safety, toxicity, AI-text detection and political evaluation. Not laboratory data, not scientific claims, not chemical hazard. Transfer is assumed.

And stability is not correctness. The authors say it directly: "a judge can wiggle and still be right, or remain wrong without wiggling." A model that never changes its mind has not thereby earned trust — see a-judge-consistent-with-itself-and-not-with-experts, which is the same warning from the other direction.
