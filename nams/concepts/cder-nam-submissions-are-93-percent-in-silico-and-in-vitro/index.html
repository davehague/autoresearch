What industry actually sends the FDA

The first behavioural measurement on the acceptance axis. This vault holds four maps about regulatory acceptance and every one of them is about rules — what FDA's draft guidance says it would accept, what the EU roadmap aspires to, what the UK aims for, what the joint FDA/EMA AI principles require. None of them says what anybody actually did. This is FDA's drug-review centre searching its own archive and counting.

The count. Fifteen years of Module 4 (the nonclinical study reports section of the standard drug dossier), sorted into five categories:

CategoryShare
In silico (computer prediction — QSAR, read-across, PBK)49%
In vitro (cells in a dish)44%
In chemico + non-human in vivo + other7% combined

Within the in-vitro group, "stem cell-derived and sandwich culture models, showed higher prevalence compared to 3D models and organ chip or MPS models."

Two readings, both worth keeping.

(1) The computational layer is not a frontier — it is already the largest category. The vault has treated in-silico-qsar-read-across and pbk-qivive-toxicokinetics as the "model half" of the loop, the connective tissue a closed loop would update. In FDA submission volume they are the single biggest slice. The caveat the authors supply matters and shrinks this somewhat: an application often contains many in-silico reports (one per metabolite, one per prediction platform), so 49% of submissions is not 49% of applications. It is still the largest share by a wide margin.

(2) The organ chips are barely present. Microphysiological systems carry a large share of the field's attention and funding. Fifteen years of real filings put them among the rarest categories. That is a hard number against a soft narrative, and it aligns with what the vault's own hepatotoxicity map already showed: hepatotoxicity-liver-chip-mps sits at emerging / FDA-ISTAND under-validation, developer-run, adopted nowhere.

The authors' own conclusion is about supply, not demand. CDER has been receiving NAM data for years, but industry submits only a fraction of what it generates, and the paper closes by asking for more so the regulatory-confidence base can be built. The bottleneck the regulator names is not its willingness to look at non-animal data. It is that the data isn't being shown to it. That sits directly on top of fda-cder-2026-nam-validation-draft-guidance's statement that a fit-for-purpose NAM "does not necessarily need to be validated": the door has been open, and it is under-used.

What this does NOT establish, and it is the important limit. The study counts arrivals, not outcomes. Nothing here says how many of these NAM reports were relied on in a decision, how many were supporting colour, and how many were disregarded. The vault's only evidence that a NAM actually carried a regulatory decision remains yao-2025-fda-cder-nam-experience's named drugs (Kalydeco, Galafold, Veopoz, Kimmtrak). Volume is not acceptance, and this note does not let the two blur. A keyword search of a document archive also has unmeasured recall — a NAM described in unmatched language is invisible, and no sensitivity check is reported.
