The robot can see the plate. It cannot reliably see the tube.

Everything this vault holds about autonomous laboratories argues about the parts above the bench: which experiment to pick, whether the readout can be interpreted without a person, whether a regulator will accept the answer. The hands are assumed to work.

This is a measurement of the hands, and specifically of the eyes that guide them.

Laboratory plasticware is transparent, which breaks machine vision in two ways at once. Colour cameras see straight through to the background. Depth cameras — which bounce infrared light off surfaces and time the return — get nothing back, or get a return from the wrong surface. Put several such objects in front of each other, which is the normal state of a bench, and the object is both see-through and half-hidden.

Handed a perfect outline of the object and a CAD model in advance — generous inputs a real system would have to earn — the strongest general-purpose pose model tested got the object's position and orientation right often enough to grasp it 67.9% of the time on laboratory consumables, against 89.5% on the established household-transparent-object benchmark. Average positional error went from about 10 mm to about 23 mm. A gripper aiming 2 cm off does not pick up a 15 mL tube. The second-best model managed 34%.

Why that matters to this vault, and nobody planned the coincidence

The methods this vault grades closed-loop-ready are overwhelmingly run in well plates. Tox21's entire platform — 10,000 chemicals in a week, the vault's reference example of automation at scale (automation-tox21-qhts-platform) — is 1536-well plates moving on a track. The loop-readiness ladder was built on readout objectivity and decision rules, and it never considered whether a machine could physically handle the vessel. Set the two rankings side by side and they agree, for a reason the ladder does not contain: the well plate is the one piece of labware a robot can reliably see.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the one the vault did not have:

The correlation is not evidence. Plate-based assays were already at the top of the ladder because their readouts are instrument-friendly. This adds a second, independent reason they are easy to automate. It does not upgrade anything, and it does not mean the ladder was secretly measuring perception all along.

The physical rung is shaped, not uniform. The vault has been treating "a robot could run it" as a property that improves for everything as robotics improves. It does not. The methods that need a flask, a tube, a perfusion reservoir or a bioreactor — organ-on-chip work like hepatotoxicity-liver-chip-mps, spheroid and organoid culture, anything at volume — sit in the worst part of this curve, and they are also the methods with the strongest claim to human physiological relevance. The push toward more realistic biology and the push toward automatability point in opposite directions on this axis, and neither literature says so because they are not the same literature.

The honest counterweight, and it is large

This is a computer-vision benchmark, not a laboratory result. It measures general-purpose models with no laboratory-specific training, on a hard new dataset, and new benchmarks are introduced precisely by leaving headroom. The authors decline the upgrade themselves: "a dataset for studying unresolved boundary cases rather than a solved benchmark."

Real automated laboratories mostly do not solve this problem — they engineer around it: barcoded racks, fixed nests, known slot coordinates, gantries that move to positions rather than to recognised objects. Under those conditions the perception problem largely disappears. Do not read this as "deployed plate handlers drop one tube in three." Read it as a bound on the general case: a robot walking into an unfamiliar bench and working out what is on it. That general case is what a genuinely autonomous laboratory needs, and it is the case this vault's question is about.

The brakes

  • One dataset, one paper, no replication. strength: single-study.
  • A benchmark's job is to be hard. Fine-tuned, laboratory-specific systems will do better and nothing here measures by how much.
  • No affiliations, no funding statement and no competing-interests statement appear in the retrieved preprint. Recorded as missing information, not as a clean bill. That said, the structural posture is the opposite of the automation-hype variant the vault watches for: this is a benchmark that makes existing methods look bad, published by people with no platform whose capability it inflates.
