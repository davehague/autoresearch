The screen can say a well is different. It still cannot say what different means.

A primary high-throughput screen measures each test substance once. That single measurement is what buys the throughput — ~22,000 chemicals or siRNAs in a campaign — and it is also why the usual scorecard for a classifier cannot be filled in. Sensitivity ("of the things that really were active, what fraction did we catch?") is a count over a set whose true answers are known. A screen has no such set.

This paper's move is to stop counting and start deriving. Assume the test response and the negative-control response are both normally distributed with the same variance within a plate. Then the whole scorecard falls out of one number — SSMD, the distance between the two response distributions measured in units of the noise:

QuantityIdentity
Area under the ROC curveΦ(β)
Sensitivity and specificity at the optimal cutΦ(β/√2)
Sensitivity at a chosen specificityΦ(√2·β − Φ⁻¹(specificity))

with exact confidence intervals from the noncentral t-distribution, valid at one replicate. It is neat, and for a machine that has to place a threshold and defend it, it is genuinely useful.

The thing to hold onto

Those sensitivities were never measured against anything. They describe separation between two modelled distributions. If the assay is measuring the wrong biology, if the siRNA hits an off-target gene, if the readout is confounded by cytotoxicity — the derived "sensitivity" is unaffected and still reports 0.917. It is a signal-to-noise ratio wearing a validation result's clothes.

The vocabulary is the hazard here, not the mathematics. Sensitivity, specificity and AUROC are, in every other context this vault records, measurements against reference chemicals with known answers — that is what a ring trial produces, and what an OECD Test Guideline's performance table reports. A number carrying those names and meaning something else is exactly the kind of thing that survives a hop through a literature summary intact and wrong.

Why it does not move the Tox21 map

automation-tox21-qhts-platform has recorded one blocker since 2026-07-13: the robotics were finished in 2013, the platform emits an AC50 and an efficacy value for every chemical in every assay, and nothing converts that pair of numbers into a hazard call by a fixed published procedure. That is the automatable-open-loop tier's definition, and it is a toxicological gap, not a statistical one.

Two separable questions:

  1. Is this well's signal distinguishable from noise? Statistical. Answered — here, and in the SSMD literature since 2007.
  2. Does that signal mean this chemical harms a person, and at what dose? Toxicological. Unanswered, on this platform and on roughly two dozen other maps in this vault.

A closed loop needs both, and confusing them is the specific error this note exists to prevent. The screening field has spent nineteen years getting very good at question one. Getting good at question one does not chip away at question two; it makes the absence of an answer to question two harder to notice, because the output looks decisive.

The brakes

  • Preprint (arXiv, not peer reviewed), single author, no new experimental data.
  • The Gaussian equal-variance assumption carries every number and is not tested against the dataset it is then applied to. Screening distributions are commonly skewed.
  • The demonstration is siRNA gene knockdown for hepatitis C host factors, not chemical hazard. The statistics transfer; the biology in the worked example is a different problem.
  • 14% of plates were discarded by a human-set quality threshold before the "automated" rule ran.
  • funding_flag: [method-developer] — the author introduced SSMD, and the paper's conclusion is that SSMD already supplies what a competing vocabulary asks for. Publicly funded (NIH P20 GM156679), no product for sale; the directional interest is recorded rather than laundered.
