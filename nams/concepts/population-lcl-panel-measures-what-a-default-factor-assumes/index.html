Measuring the safety factor nobody measured

The convention being tested. Every chemical safety limit in the world is built the same way: find a dose that produced no meaningful effect, then divide it by a stack of uncertainty factors to cover the things the test could not see. One of those factors covers inter-individual variability — the fact that people are not identical, and the person setting the limit does not know who is at the sensitive end. The conventional value for the toxicodynamic half of that factor (how differently people respond to a chemical that has reached their tissue, as opposed to how differently they absorb and clear it) is 10^0.5 = 3.16. It is a default. It was chosen; it was not measured per chemical.

What this study did. Ran 53 substances against 131 human lymphoblastoid cell lines — immortalised white blood cells from named donors in the 1000 Genomes reference panel, spanning Utah/European (CEU), British (GBR), Tuscan (TSI) and Yoruba/Nigerian (YRI) ancestry, sex-balanced. For each donor, each batch and each chemical, fit a concentration–response curve and estimate the EC10 (the concentration cutting cell viability by 10%). Then compute TDVF05 = the ratio of the population median EC10 to the 5th-percentile EC10. In plain terms: how much lower a dose does it take to affect a sensitive person than a typical one?

The median is reassuring and the distribution is not. 3.8 against a default of 3.16 reads as "the convention was about right." But the paper's own results section states "About half of these chemicals exceeded the default TDVF05 of 10^1/2" — so for roughly one substance in two, the assumed margin is too small, and for doxorubicin it is short by more than an order of magnitude (46-fold spread between a sensitive individual and a typical one). Two chemicals exceeded even the factor of 10, which is supposed to cover variability in both how the body handles the chemical and how it responds.

The abstract describes the finding as "largely consistent with default assumptions." Both readings come from the same data. For anyone actually setting a limit, "the median is fine" and "half the chemicals exceed it" have opposite operational consequences, and this vault records the second.

Why the variation happens, per the paper. A genome-wide association analysis across the 131 donors found the loci associated with differential cytotoxicity "primarily containing transporter and metabolism genes" — i.e. differences in how cells move a chemical in and out and chemically process it, which is a mechanistic explanation rather than unexplained noise. That matters for whether the number is transferable.

A new claim type, not a new endpoint. Every other method in this vault answers is this chemical hazardous (a class), how hazardous (a potency band), or at what dose (a point of departure). This one answers how much do people differ — a spread. It is the third distinct kind of output the vault holds, after the hazard/potency/dose triad of OECD GL 497, and it exists only because you can run 131 cell lines where a conventional assay runs one.

Grading, stated plainly. evidence_tier: peer-reviewed-method — a well-designed single research programme with a defined method and a stated applicability domain, not an interlaboratory validation; no second laboratory has reproduced it. regulatory_status: none — no Test Guideline, no guidance, and no regulatory programme has yet used a TDVF05 to set a real limit. strength: single-study. funding_flag: [industry] — four co-authors are Unilever employees and the work was part-funded by a Unilever sponsored research agreement, on a number that next-generation risk assessment needs in order to work without animals.
