A battery of three that fails as one

This vault's sharpest standing rule: when two assays in a battery fail on the same chemicals for the same structural reason, that battery casts one vote, not two — and an automated system that trusts the battery's decision rule will read concordance as corroboration and iterate confidently into a systematic error.

Geci et al.'s liver-injury battery is a clean instance, and the authors supply the evidence for it themselves without framing it that way.

The three assays. Cytotoxicity (does the drug kill liver cells), mitochondrial toxicity (does it poison their energy machinery), and BSEP inhibition (does it block the pump that clears bile acids out of the cell). Each is a single-cell-type, short-exposure, dish-based measurement of a drug acting directly on a liver cell.

What all three structurally cannot see:

  • Immune-mediated liver injury. A large share of severe clinical DILI is idiosyncratic — the drug or a metabolite of it provokes an immune attack on the liver, in a small minority of patients, often weeks into treatment. There are no immune cells in any of these three assays. There is no mechanism by which this class of injury could register.
  • Slow, cumulative and adaptive processes. All three read acute effects. The authors state the analysis "did not account for different dosing schedules of drugs". A drug whose harm accrues over weeks of repeated exposure looks benign at every timepoint measured.
  • Anything requiring a second cell type or an intact tissue. No stellate cells, no cholangiocytes, no architecture. Fibrotic and bile-duct injury run through cells the battery does not contain.
  • Transporters other than BSEP, and mechanism synergy. Both stated as unmodelled by the authors.

Why this reads as one blind spot rather than three separate limitations. Adding an assay to a battery buys you an independent chance to catch a hazard only if it can fail independently. These three fail on the same drugs — the ones whose mechanism runs through something absent from all of them — and they fail for the same reason: they are all the same experimental object with a different endpoint measured. The battery's diversity is in what it reads, not in what it contains.

That is a specific, mechanistic account of why the sensitivity is 51%. The missing half is not noise and it is not a coverage accident that more data would fix. It has a shape.

What it means for a loop. The battery's decision rule is fully deterministic and every input is machine-obtainable — the map is closed-loop-ready, and deservedly. But the rule's silence carries no signal. An automated system running it would see a clean result for an immune-mediated hepatotoxicant and have nothing to distinguish that from a clean result for a genuinely safe drug. Because the failure is structural rather than stochastic, running the battery again, or on more compounds, does not surface it; it reproduces perfectly, which an active-learning loop will read as reliability. This is the rail's exact mechanism: high confidence, high throughput, one direction of error.

Cross-battery note. The bile-salt-export-pump measurement is also a component of the vault's cholestasis battery (hepatotoxicity-cholestasis-battery), by a different group with different funding. Any hepatic assessment composing both inherits the same BSEP reading twice while appearing to consult two independent sources.

Honesty about what is sourced. The authors state the coverage limits, the unmodelled mechanisms and the uneven data. The specific immune-mediated framing above is the vault's reading of why the missing half has a structure, not a claim the paper makes in those words — it is flagged as an inference and needs a citation before it is treated as established.
