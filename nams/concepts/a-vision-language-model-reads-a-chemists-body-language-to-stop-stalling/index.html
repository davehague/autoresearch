The LiDAR stop was never the hard part — reading what the human is about to do was

automation-self-driving-labs's execution strand already carries fifteen architectures that act on hardware, written protocols, or raw sensor streams to decide whether a physical step ran correctly. This one answers a narrower but load-bearing question none of the others touch: in a lab a robot shares with a human, when should it stop, and can it say why?

What the baseline actually was

Before fine-tuning, the vision-language model's read on what a detected human was doing was worse than a coin flip on two of three scenarios (20% and 29% accuracy against a binary-ish classification problem) — the paper is explicit that the prior art here is "simple LiDAR-based obstruction detection," i.e. presence/absence only, with no attempt at intent. Fine-tuning on 3,270 real-world-collected examples closed that gap to 82-94%.

The counter-intuitive result worth keeping

Adding depth/distance information — which should, naively, be strictly more information — made the model worse on two of the three scenarios (down 18 and 8 percentage points). The likely mechanism: the extra numeric channel diluted a signal the fine-tuned vision-language model was already extracting cleanly from the RGB image and language reasoning alone. This is a small, concrete instance of a pattern this map already watches for elsewhere — more input is not automatically more signal.

Why this doesn't touch toxicology

Zero chemical, biological, or regulatory content. The lab in question is a synthetic-chemistry self-driving lab (University of Liverpool's mobile-robot-chemist platform family); the contribution is domain-general human-robot-interaction perception that happens to be demonstrated in a chemistry setting.

What's still missing

Single lab, single hardware platform, in-domain cross-validation only (no test on a different lab's layout, lighting, or instrument set). A short HRI companion-track paper, not a full journal study. evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory; UK academic/public funding only, no industry sponsor or COI.
