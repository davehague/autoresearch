The comparator's authority comes from people, not from a plate

benchmarking-replaces-the-prediction-model-with-a-comparison describes a decision procedure that looks, from a distance, like the cheapest possible fix for this vault's dominant blocker: no published threshold needed, just run the candidate against a benchmark and compare.

This note is the reason it is not that.

Read the authors' own list of what makes something a valid benchmark, and one entry is doing far more work than the others: extensive market experience data. Product class, application site, composition, batch stability — those are all statements about the material. Market experience is a statement about the world: this product has been on shelves, used by a large number of people, and the complaints did not come.

That is an epidemiological observation. It accrues over years, it is partly held in proprietary consumer-complaint databases, and no assay generates it.

What that does to a closed loop

The vault's ambition is a cycle: a robot runs the assay, an AI reads the result and decides, a model updates, the AI picks the next experiment. For that cycle to close, everything the decision consumes has to be producible inside it.

A benchmark comparison fails that test in a specific way, and it is worth being precise about how. The comparison itself is fully mechanisable. What is not mechanisable is the selection of the comparator — and the selection is where the safety judgement actually lives. Choose a benchmark with a thin market history and the comparison is meaningless; choose one with a rich history and you have imported a human-exposure conclusion that the loop did not derive and cannot check.

So the loop can run the comparison and cannot own the standard. Somebody outside keeps feeding in market history, and that somebody is exercising the judgement.

Why this is filed as an open question rather than a refutation

Because it might be the right answer anyway.

The alternatives on the table for the vault's missing-decision-rule problem are, as recorded across the maps: (a) a committee writes a threshold, which is slow and has not happened for most endpoints; (b) wait for potency data that does not exist; (c) fit a quantitative adverse outcome pathway that maps a measurement to a probability of disease, which is the only machine-executable route and is barely started. Benchmarking is a fourth: anchor to a product the world has already tested on itself.

Compared with a committee threshold, that is not obviously worse grounded — arguably it is better grounded, because the evidence is human exposure rather than expert consensus. What it is not is autonomous. A vault whose question is "what could a machine run end to end" has to record the difference between a rule a machine can execute and a rule a machine can own, and this is the cleanest instance of that distinction the vault holds.

Honest limits. This is the vault's reading of a criterion list in a narrative review by a contract testing laboratory. The authors do not frame market experience as a loop constraint; they frame it as good practice. No study measures how much of a benchmark's validity comes from market history versus from the other criteria, and one cited study did anchor to direct human patch-test data on 40 chemicals instead. The inference is labelled.
