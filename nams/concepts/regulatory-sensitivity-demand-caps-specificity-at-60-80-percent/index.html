The specificity ceiling is set by the regulator, not by the biology

The vault's 2026-07-11 correction was that specificity must be read against battery position, not as standalone method quality — derived from the phototoxicity ROS assay's 100% negative predictive value, where a 42–82% specificity is fine because a positive only routes onward. GD 263 states the same thing as a general constraint with a number attached:

"it is clear that due to the very high sensitivity required by regulatory authorities for accepting the use of in vitro test methods to identify chemicals not requiring hazard classification and labelling for serious eye damage/eye irritation (UN GHS No Cat.), their specificity will never go beyond 60-80% (the highest the specificity, the more limited the applicability)."

Why the ceiling exists. A method licensed to declare a chemical harmless has an asymmetric cost structure. A false positive over-labels a safe product — expensive, embarrassing, harmless. A false negative sends an irritant to market unlabelled. Regulators therefore insist on sensitivity in the high nineties, and every threshold that buys sensitivity spends specificity. Push a viability cut-off up until you catch 97% of real irritants and you will necessarily flag a large slice of the harmless ones too.

The parenthesis is the sharper half: "the highest the specificity, the more limited the applicability." The other way to improve specificity is to narrow the applicability domain — exclude the chemistry the method gets wrong. So a method can report a better specificity number by answering fewer questions. Two methods with different specificities may not be differently good; one may just have a smaller domain. This is a direct warning against the comparison the vault makes routinely.

GD 263's own worked numbers: ICE (TG 438) and STE (TG 491), "those showing the best accuracy for identifying UN GHS No Cat. chemicals," achieve "specificity … only 76-81% with already a few false negatives being obtained (sensitivity around 97%)." Best in class, at the stated ceiling.

And the prescribed fix is composition, not a better assay: "several methods capable of identifying UN GHS No Cat. chemicals with very high sensitivity will need to be combined to increase the overall specificity of the testing strategy to acceptable values."

Which collides with the same document's other instruction. Composing high-sensitivity rule-outs only raises specificity if their false positives are drawn from different populations — i.e. if the methods are conditionally independent (oecd-names-conditional-independence-as-a-battery-design-rule). GD 263 says to combine them and, twenty pages earlier, says to check independence, and never connects the two instructions to a specific pair. It contains at least one pair for which combination cannot work: tg492-and-tg492b-are-one-measurement-read-twice.

What this changes across the vault. Numbers the vault has recorded with mild alarm should be re-read as in-family, not as weakness:

  • TG 492 (RhCE eye rule-out): 95–99% sensitivity, 63–72% specificity — at the ceiling.
  • TG 494 (Vitrigel eye rule-out): 96% sensitivity, 67% specificity — at the ceiling.
  • TG 495 (ROS phototoxicity screen): 100% sensitivity, 42–82% specificity — below it, and the guideline is content because the NPV is 100%.
  • h-CLAT (TG 442E, skin sensitisation): 93% sensitivity, 66% specificity — same shape, different endpoint.

Four adopted methods, four specificities in the sixties, and it is a design consequence rather than four independent disappointments. The vault should note the ceiling where it quotes these numbers.

The loop consequence, and it is not comfortable. If specificity has a regulatory floor on cost and the only remedy is composition, then an autonomous system optimising for throughput has an incentive to run one high-sensitivity rule-out and accept its over-flagging — which is cheap and safe — while the thing that actually improves the answer is running several mechanistically different methods, which is expensive and looks redundant to an optimiser. The efficient choice and the correct choice diverge, and only the correct one requires understanding conditional independence.
