FDA: a NAM does not have to be validated to be used

"It is important to note that for a NAM to be considered for review in drug development, the test does not necessarily need to be validated. However, as FDA gains confidence in these tools, they could be formally adopted to reduce or replace specific animal tests. A fit-for-purpose NAM, even if not validated, may adequately address specific toxicological concerns. When a NAM is used it is always in the context of the weight of evidence (WOE)…" — FDA CDER, General Considerations for the Use of New Approach Methodologies in Drug Development (draft, March 2026)

Read the grading before the finding

evidence_tier: regulatory-adopted here does not mean "this framework is adopted." It means the claim is sourced from the regulator's own primary document — the same convention the vault used for photoallergy-photogenotoxicity-no-validated-method, where an adopted OECD guidance is the source for a claim that something does not exist. The framework itself is regulatory_status: proposed — it is a draft, it is nonbinding ("distributed for comment purposes only"; "does not establish any rights for any person"), and comments closed 18 May 2026 with criticism from both the Physicians Committee and (per RAPS reporting) industry. strength: suggestive, deliberately. The vault has been burned before by reading a draft as a final text (TG 454A, 2026-07-10) and is not repeating it. The schema has no tier for "draft regulatory instrument" — that gap is logged in docs/open-gaps-log.md.

The finding: the acceptance axis is measuring the wrong unit

The vault's regulatory_status ladder —

OECD-TG-adopted > under-validation > proposed > none

— silently encodes a method-centric theory of acceptance: a method gets validated, earns a Test Guideline, and thereby becomes usable. Every rung is a property of the method.

CDER describes a parallel route in which none of that is required. A sponsor may bring a NAM with no formal validation and no Test Guideline and have it carry regulatory weight, provided they can show it is fit for purpose for a defined context of use, with demonstrated human biological relevance and technical characterization, inside a weight-of-evidence argument about a particular drug.

On that route, acceptance is not a property of the method. It is a property of the triple:

(method × context of use × submission)

The same assay can be sufficient for one dossier and inadequate for the next, because the question being asked is different. That is not a rung on the vault's ladder. It is a different ladder, and it is not attached to the method at all.

What this does to three of the vault's maps

The vault holds three maps at regulatory_status: none with genuinely interesting science, and has been implicitly narrating none as "not usable yet":

  • hepatotoxicity-liver-chip-mps — 87% sensitivity / 100% specificity on a blinded 27-drug panel, but developer-run, and in FDA's own ISTAND pipeline. This is precisely the class CDER's fit-for-purpose route exists to admit — and CDER's "human biological relevance" criterion ("hepatocytes, stellate cells, Kupffer cells… albumin production, metabolic competence") reads almost like a specification of an MPS liver model.
  • inhalation-airway-epithelium-ali — CDER's own respiratory example is "a tissue reconstructed from primary human lung cells… (e.g., beating cilia)", which is a description of this map's method.
  • repeat-dose-systemic-toxicity-nam — a weight-of-evidence framework, which is the mode CDER says NAMs are "always" used in.

None of their statuses change. They have no Test Guidelines, and regulatory_status correctly records that. What changes is the reading: the axis is measuring guideline existence, and the vault has been narrating usability. Those are not the same thing, and until this source the vault had no reason to notice the difference.

The tension worth holding

The Physicians Committee — an organisation whose interest runs toward replacing animal tests — welcomed this provision and simultaneously warned that "the lengthy validation process can sideline promising, human-centric methods while drug developers continue to rely on historical animal testing." That is the strongest argument for the fit-for-purpose route: formal validation is slow, and slowness has a body count in animals and in bad predictions. The vault has the number — TG 445A was ECVAM-validated in 2014 and adopted in 2026: twelve years (metabolism-cyp-induction-heparg).

The argument against is equally real and this vault is built on it: validation is what makes a performance claim trustworthy, and "fit-for-purpose, judged by the sponsor, assessed case-by-case" is exactly the regime in which a developer-run 87%/100% panel starts doing regulatory work it has not earned. The vault's funding_flag exists because that failure mode is common.

Both are true. The honest reading is that FDA is trading throughput for rigour, deliberately, with a credibility framework (COU, biological relevance, technical characterization) meant to stop the trade from becoming a giveaway — and that whether the framework holds is an empirical question nobody can answer yet, because the guidance is four months old and not final. That is the state of the evidence, and it is why this is suggestive, not strong.
