The agent ran the right experiments and not all of them

What was measured. Six agents on GPT-4o — one reading user intent into requirements, one planning, one writing simulation code, one rule-based executor running it, one vision model reading the resulting plots, one writing the answer — pointed at a proprietary AstraZeneca crystallisation simulator and asked to improve a pharmaceutical process. The system does the thing this vault's central question is about: pick a variable, perturb only that one, re-run, compare against the baseline, recommend.

Two numbers describe how well it chose:

  • Precision 94% — of the simulations it ran, nearly all were appropriate.
  • Recall 64% — of the simulations a human annotator judged necessary for the task, it ran about two in three.

A third of the required comparisons were never proposed.

Why this is a different failure from the one this map already holds

automation-experiment-selection declares one argument: can anything choose the next experiment well enough to be trusted with the choice, and its recurring finding is that the uncertainty estimate the selection rests on is the part that has not been shown to hold. Every prior entry is about a selector whose confidence is miscalibrated — it thinks it knows more than it does, and picks badly as a result.

This is not that. Nothing here says the agent's chosen experiments were poor; 94% of them were judged appropriate. The defect is that the search never covered the space. A miscalibrated selector runs the wrong experiment and you can see the wrong experiment. An incomplete selector runs a subset of the right experiments and the output is indistinguishable from a complete run — every trajectory in it is sound, every conclusion is supported by a simulation, and the third that was never asked leaves no trace.

In a toxicology loop the analogue is direct and uncomfortable. A system asked to characterise a chemical's hazard runs eight assays out of the twelve a battery calls for, all eight cleanly, and reports a coherent negative. The missing four are not in the result table. This vault already knows what happens when a battery's votes are not independent; this is the prior problem of a battery whose votes were never cast.

The two other numbers, and what they are worth

Hypothesis confirmation: 76%. Roughly a quarter of the agent's own hypotheses were not supported by the simulator it then queried. The paper reads this positively, as "a form of virtual empirical validation" — and that is fair, because being wrong and finding out is the point of running the experiment. It is also a stated error rate on the generative step, and it is the only metric in this paper with a machine-checkable ground truth.

Correctness 4.1 and helpfulness 4.2, on a 1–5 scale, from two people across five tasks. The authors deliberately rejected LLM-as-judge on the grounds that "LLMs lack real-world experience with the specific scenario knowledge" required — an honest and correct call. What replaces it is two senior domain specialists, unnamed, with no reported inter-rater agreement, no blinding, in a study three of whose authors work for the company that owns the simulator. The headline correctness figure is an expert opinion, and should be read as one.

The Requirement Analyzer ablation is the cleanest structural result in the paper. Delete it and recall rises to 69% while precision falls to 65% and confirmation collapses from 76% to 45%. The system "tends to overuse available simulation functions." So the component that makes the agent selective is the same component that makes it incomplete — precision and recall on experiment selection trade against each other through one design choice, and the paper found no configuration that got both.
