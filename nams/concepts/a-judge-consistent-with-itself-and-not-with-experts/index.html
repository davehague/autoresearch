The grader is more reliable than the experts and agrees with them less — and it is the reward signal

Falck et al. built a task space where an AI agent must replicate a published result — read the paper, rerun the experiments, regenerate a figure that was removed. There is no correct answer to check against, so the reward is a language model reading the agent's work against an auto-generated per-task rubric. They then report the judge's reliability, and the numbers deserve to be read side by side rather than one at a time:

ComparisonAgreement
Rubric judge vs. another draw of the rubric judge0.66
Simpler baseline judge vs. itself0.46
Human expert vs. human expert0.30
Rubric judge vs. human0.19
Baseline judge vs. human0.15

Both readings are true and they point in opposite directions. The judge beats the baseline on human agreement (0.19 vs 0.15) — that is the authors' claim and it holds. And the judge's agreement with human experts is lower than the agreement humans manage with each other, while its agreement with itself is more than double the human-human figure. The authors say so themselves: "there are several tasks on which our rubric judge disagrees with humans, suggesting room for improvement."

Why the self-consistency is the point, not a bonus. A reinforcement-learning reward has to be low-noise or training does not converge — the paper reports that eight baseline-judge samples are needed to reach the noise level three rubric-judge samples give. So the property being optimised for is stability, and stability is exactly what a systematically-wrong measuring stick also has. Human disagreement at 0.30 is not obviously noise, either: replication quality may genuinely be a contested judgement, in which case a grader that returns the same answer every time has resolved a real disagreement by fiat.

The circularity, stated plainly and without exaggeration. The judge is a coding agent built on GPT-5.5. GPT-5.5 (in the Codex harness) is one of the two baselines the trained agent is reported to beat. The trained agent uses GPT-5.5 as its own tool. Everything — task space, rubric, judge, agent, evaluation — is built and reported by one commercial laboratory. The authors take the visible mitigations (three-sample averaging, human study, a held-out split, prompt-optimised baseline) and disclose the residual honestly, including that the judge "was never validated on imagined tasks" before it was used to support an innovation claim on 20 counterfactual variants.

The vault has this shape once already, at the audit layer. the-instrument-that-audits-the-benchmarks-is-itself-unreliable records automated benchmark-validity scanners agreeing with human graders at F1 between 0.74 and 0.14 depending on flaw class and model. Same structure, one layer down: when the measurement is contested, the proposed replacement is another language-model measurement, and its agreement with people is where the trouble shows.

This is calibration-audits-the-estimator-not-the-oracle with a number attached. That concept argues no in-family fix turns a model judge into an independent oracle. This paper is a careful, well-executed instance of building the best in-family judge one can — and its own reliability table shows what that buys: consistency with itself, not agreement with the experts whose judgement it stands in for.

The transfer to this vault's question. A toxicology loop does not have this problem where an adopted assay supplies the verdict — cell viability under 50% is not a rubric score. It has exactly this problem everywhere the verdict is a weight-of-evidence judgement, which is most of the vault's automatable-open-loop column. Substituting a model judge for the missing decision rule would produce a loop that trains beautifully and is anchored to nothing.
