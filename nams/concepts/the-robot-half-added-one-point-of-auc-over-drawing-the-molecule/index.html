The robot half added about one point of AUC over drawing the molecule

Tox21 is this vault's exhibit A for the claim that the robot half of an autonomous lab already exists: ~10,000 chemicals against a fixed assay panel, in weeks, since 2013. This is the first source the vault holds that asks what all that wet work is worth against a cheap alternative — and answers it with a controlled comparison.

Thirteen targets were added to the panel, including the cardiac hERG channel and five drug-metabolising enzymes. Machine-learning models were then trained three ways: on assay data alone, on chemical structure alone, and on both.

InputLiver injuryCardiotoxicity
structure + assays0.74 ± 0.030.82 ± 0.02
structure only0.73 ± 0.030.80 ± 0.04
assays only (49-target panel)0.65 ± 0.030.69 ± 0.02

AUC-ROC of 0.5 is a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect. A model that never touched a cell scored within one to two points of the model that consumed 262 robotic assay readouts, and the gap sits inside the error bars. Run the other way round: the assays on their own are markedly worse than the structure model.

Why this is a limit, not a debunking

The paper is not saying biology is useless. It is saying something narrower and more useful, and the authors say it themselves: a structure model "relies exclusively on chemical structure information, making it less susceptible to such variability" — where the variability is "changes in experimental conditions, technical noise, and batch effects." The wet measurement carries real information and real noise, and on this benchmark the two roughly cancel.

What it means for a loop

This is the sharpest empirical pressure the vault holds on its own framing. The loop story assumes the robot generates the evidence and the model consumes it. Here, on two commercially important endpoints, the model was already most of the way there without the robot — and the residual value of running the plates is small enough to be hard to detect against experimental noise.

Two consequences worth carrying:

  • An autonomous loop that trains on its own screening output and then uses that model to choose its next screen is, on this evidence, largely closing a loop on chemical structure. Its apparent agreement between model and assay is not two independent votes. This is the correlated-blind-spot rail applied to a model–assay pair rather than an assay–assay pair, and the schema has no row shape for it (logged).
  • It sharpens rather than contradicts models-trained-on-hit-calls-learn-the-assay: if a model trained on high-throughput screening inherits that screening's artefacts, and a structure-only model matches it, the question of what the biology is contributing is now measured, not rhetorical.

Also see a-better-panel-buys-fewer-experiments-not-a-better-answer — the same paper's result that does favour the expansion, and in the currency a loop actually spends.

Do not over-read this. It is one modelling study, on two endpoints, inside one chemical library, with reference labels compiled by an automated join over adverse-event databases. It is a strong hint, not a finding about biology in general.

Rebutted by: the-metric-that-improved-is-not-the-one-a-decision-uses — a scope condition rather than a contradiction: the AUC-ROC scores compared above measure ranking (does the model score a toxic chemical above a safe one), and a 2026 study on 37 Tox21 assays showed a model can gain significantly on that metric while losing to a plain random forest on the precision–recall and Matthews-correlation measures a screening decision actually runs on. Nothing here is wrong; read "0.73 vs 0.74" as a statement about ordering chemicals, not about how a loop would act on either model.
