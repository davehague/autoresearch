The chip is a manufactured part, and the lot is an uncontrolled variable

The thing a plate reader cannot see

An assay run in a 96-well plate has a consumable too, and nobody worries much about it: a well is a well. An organ-on-chip is a fabricated fluidic device — channels of a specific geometry, made of a specific polymer, with a specific surface treatment, offering a specific resistance to flow. The review states plainly that all four of those change the answer:

"Changes in device geometry, material composition, surface properties or fluidic resistance can alter cellular behavior and assay outcomes."

So the consequence for regulatory use is a manufacturing consequence: controlled material specifications, validated production processes, lot-release criteria, traceability, formal change control — the vocabulary of a manufactured product, not of an experiment. And the open question is one the field has not settled: which manufacturing changes require a comparability study, and which require partial or complete revalidation?

This is a supply-chain property masquerading as an assay property, and the vault has now met it twice in three weeks from different directions — here as device manufacture, and on 2026-08-15 as the robot's own calendar, where the scheduling of an automated workflow turned out to be a chemical variable. Both are the same category of finding: something outside the biology and outside the protocol is moving the result, and nothing in the readout reports it.

Why a loop makes this worse rather than better

The instinctive argument runs the other way — automate the assay and you remove operator variability, which is the biggest source of noise. That is true and it is not the whole ledger.

A loop can standardise its own protocol. It cannot standardise its own consumable. Every closed-loop argument in this vault assumes the apparatus is a constant and the chemical is the variable. Here the apparatus is a batch-manufactured part with lot-to-lot variation that changes cellular behaviour, and the loop has no sensor for it. A campaign that spans two device lots has an unlabelled confound running through the middle of it, and an active-learning policy will happily attribute the step change to whichever chemicals happened to follow it.

Layer the cell supply on top — the review is explicit that quality systems "must also encompass cells, extracellular matrices and reagents, particularly when primary or iPSC-derived cells introduce donor and batch variability" — and a chip campaign has at least three independent batch variables: device lot, cell lot, matrix/reagent lot. None of them appears in the readout.

The auditability requirement, and why it belongs to the model layer too

"Regulatory review requires the analytical path from raw data to the reported outcome to be reconstructable. This includes sample and device identifiers, experimental metadata, data-processing steps, exclusion criteria, software versions and links between manufacturing lots and assay results."

Read that as a specification for a loop's data plane, because that is what it is. It names exclusion criteria and software versions alongside sample identifiers — i.e. the processing decisions, not only the measurements. Current practice, the review says, is reporting "processed summary measurements."

The authors' conclusion on the computational layer is the sharpest line in the paper: "The immediate priority is therefore not algorithmic complexity but reproducible and auditable computation." And the requirement they attach to AI-supported analysis — "an explicit context of use, representative datasets, external validation, version control and monitoring for performance drift" — is a maintenance obligation, which is the one item a closed loop makes structurally harder: a loop exists to update the model, and a model that keeps updating keeps moving out from under whatever qualification it was granted.

What this connects to

  • The provenance argument the vault already holds. The loop-infrastructure map recorded on 2026-07-21 that an agentic toxicology orchestrator's real contribution was a provenance ledger, and that provenance is the loop's missing accounting layer (toxmcp-provenance-is-the-loops-missing-ledger). This review is a regulator-facing statement of the same need, arriving from wet-lab hardware rather than software: the ledger has to reach back to the device lot.
  • The access-gate family. The vault's access_gate modifier records commercial barriers to standing a loop up elsewhere — a single-vendor cell line, a sole validated kit, a licence-gated model. Manufacturing variability is the same family seen from inside: not "can you buy the part" but "is the part you bought the same part."
  • The cross-site problem it sits inside. "Most MPS validation remains concentrated within developer laboratories or specialized academic centers" — feasibility, not transferability. The review's recommended fix is unusual and worth recording: publish the failed transfers, because an unsuccessful transfer identifies where the variance actually lives, and a literature of successes only cannot.

What this does not do

It does not move loop_readiness. These are obstacles to replicating a loop and to believing its output, not to a machine executing the assay and making the call. The liver-chip map stays at automatable-open-loop for the reason it always has: a multi-parametric readout with no published rule for turning it into a decision.

It also does not carry a number. This is a structural enumeration in a review, graded suggestive, and it should be cited as a description of what the field says it needs rather than as evidence that any particular platform fails on it.
