Self-driving labs work — in chemistry. The toxicology crossover has not happened.

Read the grading before the finding

This is a peer-reviewed perspective, not an experiment: no primary data, and every number in it is a citation of somebody else's work. strength: suggestive. The vault's evidence_tier ladder has no rung for a review or perspective — peer-reviewed-method is the closest honest fit and records only the standing of the sourcing instrument. That schema gap is logged in docs/open-gaps-log.md, where it now sits next to the identical gap already flagged for draft regulatory instruments. Two runs, two source types, one missing rung.

And the author has a direction of interest, recorded in full: Thomas Hartung is the former Head of ECVAM — the EU's method-validation body — and now directs Johns Hopkins' Center for Alternatives to Animal Testing. A paper arguing that AI and automation can advance non-animal toxicology advances a position he has argued for twenty years. Not a funding conflict, not disqualifying; a directional pressure, recorded exactly as FDA's animal-reduction mandate was.

Why this source is the sweep's highest-value find anyway

stages/01-sweep: "Self-driving-lab papers overwhelmingly come from chemistry and materials, not toxicology. A paper that closes the loop on a regulatory tox assay is the highest-value find this sweep can make."

This paper is the crossover — and it crosses in the unexpected direction. It is not an AI researcher discovering toxicology. It is one of toxicology's most senior figures writing about agentic AI, in a venue for AI researchers.

And here is what makes it evidence rather than opinion: the direction of his bias predicts the opposite of what he reports. Hartung would like the life-science column to be full. He does not fill it.

The asymmetry, in his own examples

Chemistry and materials — closed loops that ran and produced things:

  • ChemCrow — "a fully autonomous synthesis of twenty-nine organosilicon compounds, eight of which were previously unknown." A loop that proposed, synthesised, and made novel matter.
  • A-Lab — autonomous solid-state synthesis of inorganic powders.

Life sciences — architectures, and the subjunctive mood:

  • Organ-on-chip coupled to high-content imaging and deep-learning analytics.
  • iPSC-derived organoid concentration–response assays feeding a Bayesian optimiser that picks the next dosing schedule.

Read the grammar. The chemistry entries are things that happened. The biology entries are things that could be assembled. No closed loop on a regulatory toxicology assay is cited, because there isn't one.

The vault's central question — which NAM endpoints can actually be put in a closed loop? — is not a question the literature has already answered somewhere the vault hasn't looked. It is open. That is worth knowing, and until tonight the vault was asserting it rather than sourcing it.

The 5% — the number to keep

DeepMind's GNoME proposed 380,000 stable crystal structures. Fewer than 5% have been independently experimentally validated.

That is the cleanest measurement in this source of the gap between what a generative loop proposes and what the world confirms — and it comes from the domain where self-driving labs work best, in a field (materials) where the ground truth is a crystal you can either grow or not. Toxicology's ground truth is human harm, and it is enormously harder to check.

Anyone reasoning from "self-driving labs are working in chemistry" to "therefore toxicology is next" should hold that 5% in view. A loop that proposes confidently and validates rarely is not a discovery engine. It is a hypothesis firehose — and in toxicology, the cost of an unvalidated confident answer is not a crystal that fails to grow.

What he says the bottleneck actually is — and it is not the robots

  • Data, not hardware: "many 'FAIR' datasets are discoverable only through bespoke portals or lack harmonized ontologies."
  • Regulatory friction: "AI can shave years off pre-clinical optimization, but agencies still require explainability dossiers that reconcile black-box embeddings with pharmacological intuition." — which is precisely what fda-model-influence-penalises-autonomy finds written down in FDA's own risk matrix, from the other side.

Both bottlenecks are interpretation and provenance, not robotics. Which is the identical diagnosis tox21-robots-solved-humans-still-interpret reaches from a completely different direction — a 2013 robotics paper about the world's largest toxicology screening platform. Two independent sources, one conclusion: the arm is not the problem.
