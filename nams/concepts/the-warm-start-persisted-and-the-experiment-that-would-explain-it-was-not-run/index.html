The warm start persisted — and the experiment that would explain it was not run

What was actually compared

Two identical closed loops making thin films of a material nobody had reported making before. Same five knobs (spin speed, precursor concentration, annealing temperature, solvent ratio, solute ratio), same characterisation, same optimiser after the first batch. The only difference was where the search started — and, it turns out, how big the search was.

  • Arm A: a language model retrieved published synthesis literature, distilled it into a structured corpus, and from that corpus proposed the parameter ranges and 12 initial conditions.
  • Arm B: Latin hypercube sampling — a standard space-filling design that spreads points evenly across each parameter's range without using any prior knowledge — over the wider generic ranges for this deposition method.

The result. 9 of 36 films from Arm A landed on the Pareto front (the set of films that no other film beats on all three quality objectives at once) against 3 of 36 from Arm B. And the advantage did not wash out: both arms then ran the same three rounds of the same Bayesian optimiser, and Arm A stayed ahead on every matched sample count, ending with a higher best sample and a success rate that was still climbing while the baseline's had stopped.

Why the persistence is the interesting half

The obvious objection to a warm start is that it buys you nothing durable — the optimiser catches up. That is a testable claim and this campaign tested it: after initialisation the two arms were mechanically identical, and the gap did not close over three rounds. The authors put it first in their own list of what to restate.

For a toxicology loop that matters, because the loop's expensive resource is not compute. It is plate capacity, cell lots, and chemical. A method of choosing where to start that is still paying off eighteen experiments later is a different economic object from one that saves you the first three.

⚠ And then the confound, which the authors raise before anyone else can

"The LLM set both the search-space bounds and the initial conditions in the LLM-assisted arm, whereas the baseline arm sampled the wider generic spin-coating ranges given in Methods; the comparison therefore measures the combined effect of narrowing the search space and of choosing points within it."

Two mechanisms, one measurement. Narrowing a search space to a region where the answer lives is a different intervention from picking good points inside a fixed space, and they have different implications: the first is a claim that the literature tells you where to look, the second that it tells you what to try. The disambiguating run — a space-filling design inside the language model's proposed bounds — is named by the authors and was not performed.

Stacked on top of that: one run per arm, one random seed, 18 optimisation samples over 5 parameters. The authors say the magnitude "cannot be separated from run-to-run variability." And one model, never swapped — a small laptop-runnable model at temperature 0, chosen deliberately for reproducibility, which is the right instinct and is also why the result's dependence on model choice is untested.

Where this lands against what the vault already holds

This map's standing conclusion is that the deciding leg of the loop is the leg with the least evidence behind it, and this source both supports and complicates that.

It supports it on the prior question. The vault recorded on 2026-08-14 that telling-the-model-what-the-experiment-means-made-it-learn-slower — adding a natural-language rationale to each result made an iterative design agent worse. Here is the same variable — how much prior knowledge to inject, and in what form — measured a third way, with a positive sign this time. The authors themselves cite work finding that language-model priors help only with domain-specific pretraining, and other work finding language-model experimental-design agents insensitive to permuted outcome labels — i.e. they behave the same whether the results they are shown are real or scrambled. Three published results, three directions. The honest summary of the deciding leg's prior-injection question is that the field does not know, and this campaign is one more observation rather than a settlement.

It complicates it in a way toxicology should notice. The vault's automation-experiment-selection map records that a cost-aware selection policy needs, for every cheap diagnostic it might buy, a likelihood model — how much does this measurement tell me about which hypothesis is right — and that in toxicology that number does not exist. This campaign sidesteps the problem entirely by having the language model bound the search rather than weigh evidence. That is a genuinely cheaper way to inject prior knowledge into a loop, and it is available to toxicology today: a model that reads the literature and proposes which concentration range and which exposure duration are worth running is not making a hazard call and does not need a likelihood model to do it.

It is not toxicology. No assay, no endpoint, no guideline, no chemical safety claim. Nothing here moves any method map's grade, and the transfer argument above is the vault's, not the paper's.

What a reader should take away

A supported hypothesis, not a measured effect. Literature-derived priors appear to give a Bayesian optimiser a head start that survives the campaign — and the study that would tell you whether that comes from knowing where to look or knowing what to try was not run, by the authors' own account. Cite it as the former and you are ahead of the evidence.
