There are three ways to not run an animal study, and this vault had only been tracking two

Nearly everything in this vault is one argument: here is a non-animal method that predicts what the animal study would have said. Ninety maps of it. The whole evidence_tier axis exists to grade how well that argument holds.

The vault holds a second argument in one corner — the threshold of toxicological concern (risk-assessment-ttc): nobody meets enough of this chemical for the answer to matter, so don't generate one. An exposure argument, not a hazard argument, and the vault noted at the time that it is the field's one genuinely agreed numeric rule and it is a rule for not testing.

This source adds a third: the study would run fine and its output would not inform the decision.

The three failures inside one acronym

The label is DIM — difficult, impossible, meaningless — and it bundles three unlike things:

  • Difficult — the substance fights the protocol: insoluble, unstable, volatile, corrosive at the dose the guideline demands, impossible to dose accurately. A technical failure.
  • Impossible — it cannot physically be done on this substance at all. A feasibility failure.
  • Meaningless — the protocol executes perfectly and the resulting number does not bear on the regulatory question. An epistemic failure.

Only the third is new to this vault, and it is the one with reach, because it does not depend on a replacement existing. A registrant making a DIM-meaningless argument is not saying "an in-vitro method covers this." They are saying the required study was never going to answer the question. That argument is available for endpoints where the vault's coverage map is blank — and the vault's monthly sweep found those blanks are systematic, not incidental: every endpoint requiring an organ to keep working over time has nothing past emerging.

Why the framing matters for a loop

An automated safety-assessment pipeline built on this vault's picture would be a hazard-prediction engine: chemical in, endpoint calls out. DIM is a different operation entirely — a decision that a question should not be asked, taken before any measurement, on grounds of what the measurement could mean. Nothing in the vault's three axes grades that, and no assay produces it.

It is also the operation with the worst failure mode. A wrong hazard call is a wrong number that a later measurement can contradict. A wrong "meaningless" call produces no data at all, so there is nothing downstream to catch it. In a loop, that failure is silent by construction: the record shows an experiment that was never run, and no evaluator ever scores it. This is the same shape as the missing-negative-results problem named in the-audit-unit-is-the-claim-not-the-call — what a system discarded is the hardest thing to audit and the least likely to be logged.

The incentive, said plainly

Twelve of nineteen authors work for companies that register chemicals under REACH; two work for an animal-protection organisation; one for an in-silico contract laboratory. The declared competing-interest statement is "no known competing financial interests." The vault records that declaration and does not treat it as settling the question. A registrant arguing that a mandated test is meaningless is arguing to be relieved of a cost, and the argument may still be entirely correct — which is precisely why the criteria, not the declaration, are what would have to be independently checked. ECHA has not done so. Nobody has.

What is not established

No counts, no hit rate, no specificity check on the criteria (how often "meaningless" is applied to a study that was not), and no regulatory standing whatsoever.
