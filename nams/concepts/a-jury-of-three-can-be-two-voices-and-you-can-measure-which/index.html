A jury of three can be two voices, and you can measure which

The vault's rail, measured, in software

This vault's sharpest standing rule is the correlated-blind-spot rail: when two assays in a battery fail on the same chemicals for the same structural reason, that battery casts one vote, not two — and an automated system trusting the battery's decision rule will read the concordance as confirmation and iterate into the error rather than out of it. The canonical instance is metabolic (tgx-ddi-micronucleus-share-the-s9-metabolism-gap); the vault has since found it in a transthyretin probe pair (thyroid-ttr-probe-variants-correlated-blind-spot), inside single cell lines (a-battery-inside-one-cell-line-is-not-five-independent-votes), and across model ensembles (multiple-frontier-models-are-one-vote-not-six).

Every one of those flags is an argument. This one is a number.

Chen et al. take three software predictors they were using as a jury, feed each one 80 real molecules whose true shape is known, and ask: when you two agree, how much of that is agreement and how much is arithmetic? The answer is that two of the three are a correlated block at κ = 0.673 with an interval clear of zero, and their own summary is the rail restated:

"At least two of three members are substantially correlated, so unanimity buys less independent confirmation than its arity suggests."

The methodological point the vault should steal

They used Cohen's kappa and said why. At the observed rates — the three predictors recover the right answer 20%, 11% and 4% of the time — two predictors that mostly say "no" agree about 84% of the time by construction. Raw concordance would have shown three impressively-agreeing independent judges. Kappa strips out the agreement you get for free from the marginals and leaves what is actually shared.

Any battery whose assays mostly return negative has this property. That is most regulatory toxicology batteries: the base rate of a positive is low, so two assays that both usually say "not a hazard" will agree most of the time whatever they are measuring. The vault has been flagging correlated blind spots from mechanism; this paper says the correlation is also directly measurable from concordance data, and that the naive version of that measurement is systematically wrong in the reassuring direction.

That is a concrete, testable ask against the vault's existing rail rows: the FET ↔ RTgill-W1 pair, the ROS assay ↔ 3T3 NRU pair, and the transthyretin probe pair each have published concordance data. None of the rail rows carries a kappa.

The trap they name and could not escape

The least-correlated pairing available to them would have been the best jury — and it is the gaming operator's own success test. Using it would have scored an arm on its own objective, which is the failure the whole paper is about. So the most diverse check available was unusable for exactly the arm that most needed checking.

Their conclusion is not "pick a better combination":

"What no rearrangement of three thermodynamic predictors fixes is the underlying problem, which is why owed experiment (3) asks for a predictor from a different model family rather than a different conjunction of these."

Rearranging a correlated battery does not decorrelate it. That sentence is the answer to a question the vault's battery-composition work keeps arriving at — see oecd-names-conditional-independence-as-a-battery-design-rule and more-assays-without-an-integration-rule-manufacture-false-positives.

What it does not license

One paper, one domain, preprint, n = 80 for the independence measurement — with one panel member so sparse (3 hits out of 80) that its correlations cannot be pinned, which the authors say plainly rather than rounding in their favour.

Nothing here is a toxicology assay. These are folding predictors, and the transfer to assay batteries is this vault's inference.

And a measured kappa is not a licence to trust the remainder. The authors are explicit that panel unanimity is "cross-predictor consistency under the declared computational panel, not independent physical confirmation." Three models agreeing about a molecule is not the molecule. The toxicological version of that sentence is the one this vault opened with: an assay battery agreeing about a chemical is not a person.
