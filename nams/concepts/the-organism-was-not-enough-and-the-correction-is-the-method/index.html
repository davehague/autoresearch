The organism was not enough, and the correction is the method

The conditional clause

Read the result sentence slowly: "Clinical simulations based on the scaled zebrafish exposure-response relationship demonstrated that accounting for in vitro drug sensitivity resulted in close alignment with observed bacterial burden across all doses and timepoints."

The alignment is reported conditional on a correction having been applied. The correction is the ratio of minimum inhibitory concentrations between the fish's mycobacterium and the human's — a cheap test-tube measurement that says how much harder one bug is to kill than the other. Without it, the fish's effective concentration is a number about a different bacterium.

So the whole-organism model was not, by itself, the predictor. The predictor was a three-part chain: a living model organism supplying the shape of the exposure–response, an in-vitro measurement supplying the potency offset, and a human pharmacokinetic model supplying the exposure.

Why this is a claim about composition rather than about fish

There is a persistent framing in the non-animal-methods literature in which methods are ranked by how much biology they contain — a cell is less than an organoid, an organoid less than an organ-chip, a chip less than a whole small organism. Complexity as a proxy for fidelity.

This result cuts across that. The most biologically complete component in the chain — an infected living vertebrate with a functioning immune system forming granulomas — still needed a correction from the simplest possible experiment, a bacterium in a tube. Complexity bought the response shape; it did not buy potency calibration, and no amount of extra biology in the fish would have, because the discrepancy was a property of the pathogen substitution, not of the host.

Generalise it carefully: when a model system substitutes more than one thing at once — different host, different pathogen — each substitution needs its own bridge, and the bridges may come from entirely different kinds of experiment. The vault's dose bridge map already holds the host-side version of this argument, where a concentration in a dish becomes a dose in a person only via a pharmacokinetic model. This adds the agent-side version.

What it means for a loop

A closed loop needs the chain, not the assay — and the chain contains a step that currently requires a per-compound decision.

  • The zebrafish leg is automatable in principle. Larvae are millimetre-scale, well-plate compatible and a known robotics target; bacterial burden is read by imaging or plating, both instrument-readable.
  • The pharmacokinetic leg is arithmetic, and the vault already grades that layer computational-connective.
  • The correction leg is the blocker. Somebody has to decide that a potency ratio is needed, which two organisms to measure it between, and whether to apply it. On this single compound the decision was made with the trial results already known. There is no published, deterministic procedure that would make that choice for a new drug, which is exactly the "no fixed data-interpretation procedure" condition that lands a method at automatable-open-loop on the vault's ladder.

And the honest version of the risk: an automated pipeline that omitted the correction would have produced a confident, precise, wrong dose prediction, with no internal signal that anything was missing. The correction is invisible in the fish data — nothing in a zebrafish experiment reveals that its bacterium is not the patient's bacterium. That knowledge lives outside the loop.

Evidence weight

Single retrospective study, one drug, one laboratory — and the group established the zebrafish model it is translating. The size of the correction's contribution is unknown, because the uncorrected result is not reported in the abstract and the full text is subscription-only. The reasoning in this note follows from the abstract's own conditional and does not depend on the unread numbers; the magnitude of the claim does. evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory, strength: single-study.
