Outcome checking cannot see a wrong mechanism — and a wrong mechanism is exactly what breaks at the boundary

The finding in one line

Seven times out of twenty-eight, an AI agent doing open-ended scientific work produced a correct-looking answer defended by reasoning its own printed numbers refute. Check the answer and you see a success. Check the reasoning and you see a system that has no idea why its answer worked, and therefore no idea where it stops.

Why this vault, which is about toxicology, cares

The vault's third axis, loop_readiness, grades whether a machine could run an assay end to end. Its deterministic ladder turns on two questions: is the readout instrument-readable, and does a fixed decision rule exist. Both are questions about the end of an experiment.

This paper is about a failure that lives before the readout — in the choice of what to measure — and it is invisible to both questions. In this vault's terms, the agent proposed an observable, the observable had a perfectly good instrument-readable readout, a decision rule could have been written for it, and the whole thing was still wrong outside the conditions it was found in.

The vault already has a name for a hazard shaped like this. Its correlated-blind-spot rail says: when two assays fail on the same chemicals for the same structural reason, the battery casts one vote rather than two, and "an AI loop that trusts its decision rule without knowing this will iterate confidently into systematic error." CAWM is that failure moved from the assay layer to the reasoning layer. A wrong mechanism is a blind spot the loop generates for itself, on the fly, and cannot see because its own accuracy metric says it is fine.

The connection to in-silico-qsar-read-across is exact and not a stretch. That map carries a domain_gate recording that the standard applicability-domain check — the similarity test that is supposed to say when a computational prediction can be trusted — is structurally blind to activity cliffs: 235 of 235 held-out chemicals passed the check, including the ones the models got wrong. A model right for the wrong reason, passing the guard designed to catch exactly that. Two literatures, two decades apart in vocabulary, same defect.

The three most useful specific results

1. Discovery and execution are different capabilities. Given only the question, 0 of 5 agents found a mechanistically valid answer. Given the answer and asked to validate it, 5 of 5 did it properly — right implementation, right parameter range, and they flagged where the signal becomes statistically unreliable unprompted. The agents are competent instruments and unreliable investigators, and an evaluation that only runs the second kind of task will report the first kind of number.

For a loop that is meant to choose its own next experiment, that split is the whole ball game, and it is why this note is filed against the self-driving-lab machinery rather than against any assay.

2. Scaffolding moved the failure rather than removing it. A mandatory five-step protocol including an explicit "challenge your conclusions — what could be wrong" step drove CAWM to 0 of 5 in the primary model — and triggered it in both cross-model episodes. The reason is worth the space: the scaffold told agents what to compute but not how finely to resolve it, and four of five swept time windows twenty to a hundred times too wide, which diluted the real signal into noise. A single unspecified resolution parameter hid the correct answer. Anyone who has watched a grid search miss the interesting region because the grid was too coarse recognises this immediately, and it should temper any expectation that a checklist fixes reasoning.

3. Independent agents converged on the same wrong formulation. Three agents, separately, defined the early-signal window relative to each event's own duration instead of absolutely — which measures how many photons there were rather than when they arrived. The author reads three-way independent convergence as "a structural pattern" rather than bad luck. If the same wrong formulation is reachable independently, running more agents in parallel does not average it away — it reproduces it. That is the closest thing in this literature to a correlated blind spot with a mechanism attached.

The check, and why it is cheap enough to be a policy

Regime-shift verification. Take the agent's stated mechanism. Turn it into a directional prediction at one condition the agent did not use as evidence. Get data at that condition — one extra run, and in most of these episodes the data already existed. Score it. Prediction fails → flag for human review before accepting anything downstream.

It caught all seven CAWM cases. And it has a design constraint that matters more than the detection rate: it needs ground truth, not a second opinion. Verbatim: "A model-only reviewer has no ground truth to compare against." You cannot ask another model whether the mechanism is right. You have to go and measure something.

In a toxicology loop, "one extra run at an untested condition" is not free the way it is in a simulation. It is a plate, a chemical, a week, a cell batch. The check is still probably the cheapest verification on offer — but the vault should not import a simulation paper's cost model along with its finding, and does not.

What this does not establish

Twenty-eight episodes, one physics simulation, one author who wrote the coding rule and then applied it to his own episodes with no second annotator and no agreement statistic. A position paper's empirical section is built to support a thesis. And the models are specific mid-2026 versions; nothing here predicts a later one's rate. The mechanism of the failure is well argued and the rate is one person's count.
