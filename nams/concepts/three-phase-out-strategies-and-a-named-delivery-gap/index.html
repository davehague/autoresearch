Three governments wrote the plan; the paper is about whether anyone runs it

The three documents. Canada's "Strategy to replace, reduce or refine vertebrate animal testing under the Canadian Environmental Protection Act, 1999"; the UK's "Replacing animals in science" strategy of November 2025; and the EU's "Roadmap towards phasing out animal testing for chemical safety assessments" of June 2026. Two are already in the vault, both graded proposed — non-binding policy papers, no legal phase-out, no target dates, every commitment hedged. Canada's is a third jurisdiction the vault has never seen and this source does not give enough of its contents to map.

The argument. Publishing the strategy is "an important step forward"; delivering it is the open problem. Three named risks:

  1. "Sustaining commitment to delivery of aspirational plans."
  2. "Increasing engagement in regulatory processes by NAM developers."
  3. "Increasing the regulatory relevance of NAM data."

The second risk is the one the vault did not have

The vault's standing reading of the acceptance axis is regulator-shaped. A method is scientifically strong and regulatory_status: none, and the implicit explanation is that the regulator has not moved yet — the validation pipeline is slow, the committee has not met, the Test Guideline is queued. The vault has dozens of maps in exactly that state.

This names a different mechanism: the developers do not turn up. A laboratory publishes a validation, the paper is cited, the method is real — and nobody submits it into the process that would adopt it, attends the consultation, or answers the regulator's data requests. That is not the regulator failing to act; it is nobody asking. It is also, unlike regulatory inertia, a thing a specific identifiable party could fix cheaply, which is why it is worth carrying separately.

This is the object adoption-system-dynamics-nam-uptake was coined for: what happens after a method counts and is still not used. That map's only prior source was a pair of facilitated workshops thematically analysed into a causal model, with no quantities at all. This adds a second qualitative source pointing the same way, from a different discipline (management and policy rather than toxicology practice), and it does not add a single number.

The third risk is this vault's loop blocker in policy vocabulary

"Regulatory relevance of NAM data" means: the method produces a good measurement that does not answer the question the regulation asks, in the units it asks for, at the confidence it demands. That is the vault's "no agreed decision rule," restated by a policy analyst. OECD Test Guideline 493 is fully quantitative and says outright that no agreed interpretation method exists; the loop cannot close because the number has no rule for becoming a verdict. Two literatures, one obstruction, no shared vocabulary — and the fact that neither cites the other is itself a finding this vault was built to notice.

What this is not

It is not evidence about any method, and it moves no axis on any map. No Status change, no evidence_tier change, no loop_readiness change anywhere. Three non-binding strategies being read approvingly by three academics is not regulatory movement, and the vault's acceptance axis exists precisely to refuse that inference. Graded in-silico-mechanistic — the tier for an argument with no wet evidence, which is the same rung a computational model gets, a known ladder defect logged since 2026-07-12.

And the full text is unread (subscription-only, no PubMed Central deposit), so the comparative analysis itself — where the three strategies agree, where they diverge, what evidence supports the three risks — is not in this record.
