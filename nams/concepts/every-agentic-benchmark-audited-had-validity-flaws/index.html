Five benchmarks audited. Five had flaws.

Almost everything this vault knows about whether machines can run laboratory work is a benchmark number:

  • ProtoPilot — 88.24% instrument pass rate against 32.35% for the nearest AI baseline.
  • Pipette — 37–72% task success on wet-lab robot manipulation.
  • LabBench2 — 1,912 tasks, and a 26–46 point drop under realistic framing.
  • LabRobFail — 90.83% → 71.02% detection off-distribution.
  • AEGIS — F1 0.97 checking the plan, 0.71 watching the world.

The vault has been discounting those numbers for who produced them — four of the five carry funding_flag: [method-developer] or [industry], builders scoring their own systems. This paper says to discount them for something else: how the benchmark was built.

What was found

The authors built LLM "scanners" that read agent transcripts looking for four classes of flaw, wrote human grading rubrics for each, and ran them over five widely used agentic benchmarks. Every benchmark had verified issues.

FlawPlain meaningEffect on the score
Ground truth accessThe answer is reachable in the environmentThe agent reads it instead of solving it
Tool failureThe harness broke, not the modelMiscounts capability in either direction
Guessing vulnerabilityThe answer space is small enough to guessA hit is not evidence of the skill
Answer format ambiguityThe success criterion is under-specifiedRight answers marked wrong and vice versa

The detail worth keeping is SWE-Bench-Verified. The "Verified" in the name means somebody already went through it by hand. It still leaked ground truth — through overly descriptive prompts and through the agent's access to the repository's upstream git history, where the fix exists. Manual verification had already been applied and was not sufficient.

Why this lands here

It is a rail turned on the vault's own evidence base. The vault reads a number like "88.24% instrument pass rate" as a measurement of a capability. This says the number is a measurement of performance on a construction, and constructions leak.

Three things this does not license:

  1. It is not a claim that any specific automation number is wrong. No laboratory benchmark was audited here. None of the five is a wet-lab benchmark; none touches toxicology. The transfer is this vault's inference from the construction method, and it is labelled as one.
  2. It gives no inflation estimate. "Flaws exist in all five" is not "scores are X points too high." Nobody measured that, and reading a magnitude into it would be exactly the over-claim the paper is about.
  3. It is a preprint, not peer reviewed, with no funding statement, and the authors call it "a proof of concept."

What it does license is a change in default posture. The vault's habit has been: a benchmark result from a disinterested party is cleaner than one from the system's builder. That ordering survives — but the floor moved down. A disinterested, hand-verified benchmark is still an instrument with unmeasured error, and there is now evidence for that rather than suspicion.
