A readout the test substance can digest

What happened

LDH release is the workhorse cell-death readout of in-vitro toxicology. Lactate dehydrogenase is an enzyme that lives inside cells; when a cell bursts, it leaks into the medium, and you measure how much is out there. It is cheap, it is machine-read, it needs no interpretation, and it appears as a supporting readout in batteries across this vault.

Here it reported no significant cell death at any dose — including a dose where the tissue was so degraded that the microscope arm of the study could not be performed on those inserts at all.

The reason is that the test substance was a protease: an enzyme whose entire function is cutting proteins. LDH is a protein. Over twenty-four hours in the same well, the thing being tested destroyed the thing being measured.

Why this is a loop finding and not a lab-technique footnote

Consider the three ways an assay readout can go wrong, from an automated system's point of view:

  1. It errors. The plate reader fails, the file is empty. The loop sees the failure.
  2. It returns something implausible. A negative absorbance, a value off-scale. A range check catches it.
  3. It returns a clean, well-formed, in-range number that means the opposite of what the rule assumes.

The third one has no defence. The number here is not weird. "Low LDH" is the most common result in toxicology and the one every threshold is built to expect. A machine reading this plate concludes the chemical is not cytotoxic, and it is exactly as confident as it would be if that were true.

The vault has one close sibling and it is worth putting them next to each other. functional-cardiotoxicity-is-invisible-to-a-viability-assay records a viability readout missing a real effect because the effect is not cell death — the assay measured the right quantity and the quantity was the wrong one. This is different and worse: the assay measured the right quantity and the test substance altered the measurement. The failure is not a coverage gap in the endpoint. It is the reagent being consumed by the sample.

The blast radius inside this vault, and it is not zero

LDH release appears as a readout in two other maps. hepatotoxicity-liver-chip-mps and nephrotoxicity-ciptec-aop-battery both carry it. Neither records that a proteolytically active test article can silence it.

Neither map is downgraded on this, and neither should be. The failure mode is specific: it requires the test substance to attack the reporter, which for LDH means proteases and, plausibly, strong oxidants and extremes of pH over a long incubation. Most chemicals in most batteries do not do this. What the vault records is a domain condition on a shared readout, not a defect in either method — and the reason to record it at all is that the condition is a property of the chemical, so no amount of characterising the assay will surface it.

For the nephrotoxicity battery this matters more than for the liver chip, because that map already carries an intra-battery correlated-blind-spot flag: five key events measured in one cell line, so a shared failure route is counted five times rather than once — see a-battery-inside-one-cell-line-is-not-five-independent-votes. A readout that can be destroyed by the test article is a sixth shared route through the same battery, and it points the same way: toward a falsely clean answer.

The check that would catch it, and why it is cheap

The vault's rail asks batteries whether their members fail on the same chemicals for the same reason. Tonight's variant of that question is narrower and easier: does the test substance have a documented chemical reactivity toward the reporter molecule?

That is answerable from structure before the plate is ever run, by exactly the in-silico layer the vault already tracks (in-silico-qsar-read-across) — protein reactivity is not an exotic prediction, it is the founding key event of the whole skin-sensitisation column (dpra-peptide-reactivity-predictivity). A loop that already computes peptide reactivity to predict sensitisation has, sitting in the same field of its database, the number that says its cytotoxicity readout is about to be eaten. Nobody appears to have connected them.

What it does not license

One study, one substance class, three donors, industry-funded and industry-authored. The mechanism — proteolytic degradation of LDH over 24 hours — is the authors' explanation, and it is a good one, but it is inferred from the discrepancy rather than measured directly.

It does not establish a false-negative rate for LDH anywhere else. No other chemical class was tested, and nothing here says how often this happens in a real screening library.

And the substitutes are not free. ATP and resazurin readouts have their own interferences, and neither is proposed here with data.
