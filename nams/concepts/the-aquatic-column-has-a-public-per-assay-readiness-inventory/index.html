Somebody built a readiness inventory for a whole endpoint column, and published it

This vault invented loop_readiness because nothing in the literature graded whether a machine could run a method. Something adjacent has now been published, from a discipline this vault barely covers.

A systematic review of non-animal methods for aquatic safety assessment — the half of toxicology that asks whether a chemical harms fish and frogs rather than people — screened 4,275 unique articles, read 1,310 in full, and produced as its main artifact not a narrative but a structured inventory:

"A database containing all the reviewed information is freely available and contains 18 metadata fields including for example the captured assay type, species, endpoints, and readiness level."

Why the last two words matter, and why the vault must not over-read them

A readiness level attached per assay, across a column of 1,310 papers, is the object this vault has been hand-building one map at a time for seven weeks. Somebody produced one for the aquatic endpoint at once.

And the vault has read exactly none of it. The abstract states the database exists, is free, and has that field. It gives no URL, no scale, no rung definitions, and no assignment procedure, and the full text carrying those is publisher-gated to this machine. "Readiness" in a regulatory-toxicology review almost certainly means readiness for regulatory use — scientific maturity, standardisation, validation status — and not readiness for machine execution, which is what this vault's axis grades. Those are different questions and the vault has repeatedly recorded that conflating them is the field's most common mistake. Recorded as an open question, deliberately, rather than as a finding: this note asserts that an inventory exists, and asserts nothing about what it contains.

What the review says is wrong with the column

Three barrier classes, in the authors' order: "their regulatory acceptance for environmental safety remains limited due to technical, scientific and policy barriers." And three named gap axes: "gaps in readiness, standardization, and mechanistic integration."

The traditional tests are described as "resource-intensive, low-throughput" — the second charge being the one that bears on whether this column could ever run at machine scale.

Two structural facts worth carrying separately

The scope exclusion is enormous. "The review excluded terrestrial and invertebrate models." This is not "the environment"; it is the aquatic vertebrate column. Everything in soil, and every invertebrate — which is most animals — is out of scope by design. Any reading of this as environmental coverage over-claims by most of the biosphere.

And the column has a definitional problem no axis in this vault can hold. Fish embryos before independent feeding are not protected animals in the European Union, so an embryo assay counts as a replacement there; not everywhere agrees. The review says so: "While embryo assays may not be considered a NAM by all geographical regions and stakeholders, information from existing embryo assays was also reviewed." Whether a method is a NAM at all has, for this endpoint, different answers in different jurisdictions — and this vault's regulatory_status ladder assumes a single global answer per method. Logged as a schema gap.

A small loop fact hiding in the methods

The review screened its 4,275 records with SWIFT-Active Screener, a machine-learning tool that ranks records by predicted relevance so reviewers read the probably-relevant ones first. An AI decided what this systematic review read. That is a real instance of the loop this vault is about, applied to the literature rather than the bench, and it arrived without comment in a methods sentence.
