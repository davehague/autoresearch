The score that went up answers a question a screening programme never asks

A toxicity model was given extra context — a knowledge graph wiring each chemical to the genes and proteins the published literature associates with it — and its headline number improved on 33 of 37 assays, significantly, across two independent architectures. On the numbers that describe whether you would act differently, it did not beat a random forest running on 1980s molecular fingerprints.

The two metrics, in English

AUROC — the metric that improved. Pick one truly toxic chemical and one truly safe chemical at random. How often does the model score the toxic one higher? It went from about 85 times in 100 to about 89 times in 100. That is a statement about ranking.

AUPRC and MCC — the metrics that did not. These describe what happens when you have to draw a line and act on it: of the chemicals you flag, how many are really toxic, and how many real ones did you miss? The semantic model was significantly worse than a random forest on both; the joint variant clawed back to a tie.

The authors state it against themselves:

"semantic models improve AUROC but do not always improve AUPRC or MCC."

Why the gap exists, and why the two metrics come apart here

Because these assays are lopsided. Twelve of the 37 have fewer than 5% active compounds. When the thing you are hunting is rare, AUROC is generous — it is dominated by how well the model orders the enormous inactive majority, and getting that ordering right is not the job. AUPRC and MCC are unforgiving of exactly the errors that matter: a flood of false flags, or a missed real hazard.

The mechanism was measured, not guessed — see the-knowledge-graph-is-thinnest-where-the-actives-are.

Why this vault keeps it

This is a clean, well-reported instance of a failure mode that will recur every time somebody proposes a model as the interpretation layer of an automated screen. A closed loop does not rank chemicals; it decides about them. It picks a threshold, calls a chemical active or inactive, and spends the next experiment accordingly. A model that ranks better and decides no better changes nothing a loop can use.

And the paper proposes no operating threshold at all. It reports threshold-free scores throughout. No chemical is called toxic; nothing says what score licenses what action. That is the vault's dominant blocker appearing inside a machine-learning paper: a better predictor is not a decision procedure.

Credit where it is due

The authors' own limitations section states that "the gains ... are, overall, modest", that "for some highly imbalanced assays the baseline models remain competitive or superior on AUPRC and MCC", and that "the marginal accuracy gains should be weighed against the added model complexity." They then diagnose the mechanism of their own weakness. The senior author built the knowledge graph being evaluated (funding_flag: method-developer), which makes the candour more notable rather than less.
