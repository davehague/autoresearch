An AI on the validation side of the loop

Every autonomous-lab source this vault has ingested puts the AI inside the experiment loop — Robin picking hypotheses, LAP driving instruments, ProtoPilot executing protocols, ToxMCP wrapping tool calls in provenance, Tox21's robots running plates. This is the first source that proposes putting an AI on the other side: the part that decides whether the method is still trustworthy.

The mechanism, in engineering terms. It is monitoring and CI for a scientific method:

  • AI companion agents — post-deployment systems tracking a validated method's performance in the field, continuously, rather than at a one-time certification event.
  • Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCPs) — a pre-agreed envelope of allowed changes (retraining, preprocessing refinement) inside which the method keeps its validated status. Anything outside the envelope requires re-review. This is lifted directly from how regulators already handle software-as-a-medical-device, where the same problem — a model that must be allowed to update without re-certifying from scratch — was solved first.
  • Numeric re-review triggers, which is the part that makes it implementable rather than aspirational: >5% absolute performance drop, detected covariate shift, code or library changes, or a new intended use.

An engineer will recognise all four. They are drift alarms, dependency pinning, and a change-approval boundary. The novelty is not technical; it is that a regulatory status is being made contingent on them.

Why this is the right shape for the vault's central problem. The vault has been climbing loop_readiness as if automatability were the thing standing between today and a closed loop. FDA's draft AI guidance already supplied the correction that autonomy has a price — model risk = influence × consequence, and a loop maximises influence by construction. This framework supplies the other half: a proposed way to pay it. If a method's continued validity is monitored continuously, with agreed triggers and an audit trail, then the evidentiary burden of high model influence has somewhere to be discharged other than "run another ring trial." Continuous monitoring is the only validation regime that a continuously-running loop can actually satisfy.

Three reasons to hold this loosely.

  1. Nothing is adopted. regulatory_status: proposed. No regulator has accepted an AI companion agent as evidence of anything. PCCPs are real for medical-device software; their transfer to toxicological method validation is the proposal, not the precedent.
  2. The 5% trigger is illustrative, not derived. The paper says "for example". A drift threshold with no empirical basis is a placeholder, and a governance regime is only as good as the number it fires on.
  3. The monitor needs a ground truth, and nobody says where it comes from. To detect a >5% performance drop in the field you must know the right answers for the field samples. In a validation study you have them — that is what the reference set is for. In deployment, the reason you ran the assay is that you didn't know. A companion agent can watch technical health (control responses, replicate variance, covariate shift) without ground truth, but it cannot watch predictive accuracy without it. The framework's headline trigger is the one that needs the thing deployment does not have. That gap is unaddressed in the paper and it is the first thing an implementation would hit.

Connects to: validation-as-continuous-service-not-event (the same claim from the AI-NAM direction), ai-decision-rules-are-not-deterministic (why an event-based validation cannot hold a retraining model), and ml-model-reporting-reproducibility — which counted how little of the provenance any of this needs is actually reported today. The gap between what a companion agent would require and what published tox-ML work currently records is the real distance to this proposal.
