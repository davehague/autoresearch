The robot's calendar is an experimental variable, and it is not in anybody's data model

Two experiments, one robot arm, one set of instrument stations. Something has to decide who goes first. For as long as self-driving laboratories have existed that decision has been treated as an IT problem with an IT objective: minimise the total wall-clock time.

Zhou et al. ran the same two syntheses under a conventional time-minimising scheduler and under one that treats inter-step timing as a hard scientific constraint. The recipes were identical. The products were not.

Read the spreads, not the means. Seven and a half times the variability in where the peak sat; fifteen times in how wide it was; ten to thirty times in crystal uniformity. Nucleation is timing-sensitive chemistry: let a step wait four extra minutes because the arm was busy on the other experiment and you have run a different experiment.

The reason this sits in a toxicology vault

This vault's central question is whether a machine could run an assay end to end. Every entry on the loop_readiness ladder is a claim about one method in isolation — its readout, its decision rule. Nothing in the schema asks what happens when the machine is running six other things at the same time, which is the only configuration in which the economics of automation work at all. Tox21's robots did not screen 10,000 chemicals by doing one plate at a time.

The failure mode is the vault's recurring one, arriving from a new direction. A closed loop assumes a change in the readout means a change in the chemistry. Here the readout changed because of the queue. The scheduler's decisions are not in the loop's data model — they are infrastructure, logged (if at all) in a different system by a different team — so a learning loop attributes timing-induced variance to the composition it was varying, and updates. It is the same shape as an undeclared serum-lot change (2026-08-12) and the same shape as embryo density corrupting a count (the-robot-upstream-of-the-assay-loses-one-embryo-in-seven): a variable that moves the measurement, is invisible to the model, and correlates with nothing the model is allowed to see.

And the authors say the quiet part: the variance "undermine[s] the reproducibility and data quality essential for artificial intelligence modeling." Their scheduler is not primarily a throughput improvement. It is a data-integrity control for the thing downstream that learns.

What it does not establish

  • No biology anywhere in it. Gold nanoparticles and a metal-organic framework. Whether a cell-based toxicology assay is timing-sensitive in the same way is an open question — plausibly yes for anything with a defined exposure window, incubation period or wash step, and unmeasured. That is a gap, and it is logged as one.
  • Both test cases were chosen because they are nucleation-sensitive — the class where the effect should be largest. Fair for demonstrating an effect exists, poor for sizing how often it bites.
  • Beat a greedy baseline of the authors' own choosing, on a metric their algorithm optimises, on their own platform, once. evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory and it stays there until someone else runs it.
  • It is not a claim about correctness of results, only about their consistency. Nothing here says the greedy-scheduled nanoparticles were wrong for any downstream purpose — only that they were different from the intended product and far more variable between runs.

The practical reading for a toxicology loop

If a chemical-synthesis loop's product identity moves with the queue, then before anyone puts a cell assay inside a shared multi-tasking robotic platform, the timing tolerances of that assay need to be written down and enforced by the scheduler, not discovered afterwards in the variance. No OECD Test Guideline this vault holds states a tolerance for "how long may the plate wait between step 4 and step 5." They state incubation times as targets. On a bench with one human doing one thing, the difference does not arise. On a shared robot running six protocols, it is the whole problem.
