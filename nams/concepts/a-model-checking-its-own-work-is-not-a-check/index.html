A model checking its own work is not a check — and a checker that passes everything is not one either

The measurement

Seven generator models were asked to turn English protocols into structured robot instructions. Three checker models were asked to catch their errors and send them back for another try, up to three times. That is 9 × 3 combinations, scored on how many of the protocol's individual parameters — volumes, times, speeds, wavelengths, well positions — came out right.

Two negative results and one positive one:

  • Generator checks itself → nothing. "The self-validation combinations produced almost no effect." Changes across the three correction rounds were "nearly absent," and several models dipped slightly on the first attempt before flattening.
  • A different model checks it, but that model is lenient → also nothing. Two of the three checkers "assigned passing judgments too liberally," and under both of them "the contribution of the regeneration stage effectively disappears."
  • A different model checks it and is willing to fail things → real recovery. Under the third checker, accuracy rose sharply after one round for all seven generators and then held.

So the finding is sharper than "use two models." Two models where one rubber-stamps is worth the same as one model. The working ingredient is the checker's willingness to reject.

Why this belongs on the correlated-blind-spot rail

This vault's rail says: two assays in a battery that fail on the same chemicals for the same structural reason cast one vote, not two. The canonical instance is metabolic — the transcriptomic biomarker and the micronucleus assay both lean on the same borrowed rat liver, so a chemical that liver cannot activate reads negative in both, and a weight-of-evidence rule records two negatives as increased confidence (tgx-ddi-micronucleus-share-the-s9-metabolism-gap).

A model verifying its own output is that structure with the sample size reduced to one. The paper names the mechanism in the same terms the rail uses: "a model with different training data and architecture does not share the error patterns that a single model would reproduce in both generation and verification." Identical error patterns, counted twice. The vault has been describing this shape in cell culture for five weeks; here it is in software, measured, with the "almost no effect" to prove the two votes were one.

And the lenient-checker result extends the rail rather than repeating it. Two assays can be independent and still useless as a battery if one of them never says no. Independence is necessary and not sufficient; a vote only counts if it can be cast against.

What it does not license

The ranking of the three checker models is a snapshot, not a property. Three specific model versions in mid-2026, on one task, against one team's answer key. Nothing here says a vendor builds better critics; it says these three builds behaved differently at rejecting bad structured output on this task, on this day.

The absolute numbers matter more than the relative gains, and they are sobering. The weakest model went from getting 7% of parameters right to 13%. That reads as an 88% relative improvement and it is a system that still cannot be used. "The recovery margin is limited for models with very low baseline performance." Inference-time review makes a mediocre generator decent and does not make a bad one usable — which is exactly the shape the vault should expect, and exactly the shape a headline percentage hides.

And this is the developers' own measurement. They built the framework, wrote the ground truth, and chose the reported demonstrations. Preprint, not peer reviewed. 30 protocols, one assay family, no toxicology and no Test Guideline anywhere in it.

The converging pair, filed the same night

epistemic-honesty-is-local-not-global arrives at the same place from a physics simulation with no shared authors: an agent that correctly rejected a false claim handed to it then defended its own claim with assertions its own printed numbers contradict. One paper measures that self-review does not improve accuracy; the other shows the behaviour that explains why. Two independent preprints, two domains, one conclusion — the verifier has to be somebody else, and it helps if they are difficult.

Rebutted by: pressure-on-a-model-grader-is-net-corrupting — the closing clause above ("the verifier has to be somebody else, and it helps if they are difficult") does not hold when the second model argues with the verdict rather than checking the artifact. Measured on nine frontier models across 14 judging tasks, a difficult second model challenging a grader's call flips it on 62–91% of items and 70% of those flips move away from the correct answer. Independence plus willingness-to-reject improves a check-and-regenerate loop and degrades a re-adjudication loop. Holds for grading an object, not for litigating a decision.
