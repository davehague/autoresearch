One company put a non-animal result in a live submission, then took it out again

The validation literature this vault reads is full of assays succeeding and occasionally failing under controlled conditions. What it almost never contains is an assay failing in a real filing, with a regulator already asking questions about it. This survey has one, in a company's own words, and it is worth the whole note.

The sequence:

  1. A company ran a novel cytokine-release assay — a test asking whether a drug candidate triggers an immune-signalling storm — on an antibody product.
  2. The novel assay came back strongly positive. The conventional assays showed nothing.
  3. The company debated internally and submitted the data anyway, before first-in-human trials.
  4. The health authority asked several questions about context of use and clinical relevance. The company answered them.
  5. After review, it emerged that the novel method produces non-specific positive reactions in certain antibody formats — which format is not disclosed.
  6. The company removed the data from the investigator's brochure.

Why it is worth recording rather than filing under "assays have false positives"

Because of the order the information arrived in. The disagreement between the new method and the old one was visible at step 2 and was not resolvable at step 2. Both readings were available: the new assay sees something the old ones miss — the entire promise of human-relevant non-animal methods — or the new assay is wrong in a way nobody has characterised yet. The company picked the first, in good faith, and the second turned out to be true for this antibody format.

That ambiguity is not a defect of this particular assay. It is the standing condition of a method whose applicability domain has not been mapped, and this vault's maps are full of them.

And the consequence for an automated system is direct. A closed loop reading "novel assay positive, conventional assays negative" has exactly the same two hypotheses and none of the mechanism for choosing between them that resolved this case — which was months of regulatory review, a format-specific artefact being noticed by someone, and a human decision to retract. There is no measurement in the loop that distinguishes finds what others miss from is wrong in an uncharacterised way, and the vault's domain_gate modifier exists precisely because that distinction is not derivable from the assay's own output.

The second case, briefly

Another company evaluated off-target binding — whether an antibody sticks to human proteins it was not designed for — after consulting the European regulator. The screen returned more binding hits than expected, and each one then needed its own follow-up functional assay to work out whether it mattered.

The non-animal method did not reduce the work. It relocated it, and multiplied it. That is a different failure mode from a false positive and is rarely priced into the case for adoption: a more sensitive method on an endpoint with no agreed interpretation rule generates work, not answers. It is the same structure the vault records on maps stuck at automatable-open-loop, arriving as a project-management problem rather than a scientific one.

What it does not say

  • It does not say cytokine-release assays are unreliable. One product, one undisclosed antibody format, no false-positive rate, method not named.
  • It does not move any method map — the assay is unidentified, so there is nothing to move.
  • It does not argue against submitting non-animal data. The survey's own summary of the five cases lists real benefits alongside: fewer animals, lower cost, risk assessment supplemented where animal models are limited, and better internal discussion.

What it does is put a number's worth of texture behind the survey's headline: of 34 companies, 5 had ever filed such data, and one of those 5 filings ended in a retraction. When the vault says a method's regulatory acceptance is none or under-validation, this is what the interval between those words and OECD-TG-adopted actually looks like from inside a company.

The brakes

  • Anonymous free-text self-report in a trade-association survey; no assay named, no format disclosed, no rate quantified, nothing independently verifiable.
  • funding_flag: [industry] — collected and published by the association whose members are describing their own experience. A retraction is not the flattering story, which is a point in the account's favour and does not remove the flag.
  • n = 1 for the withdrawal, out of five submission experiences, out of 34 responding companies, in one country.
