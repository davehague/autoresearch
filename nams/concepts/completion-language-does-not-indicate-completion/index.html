"Done" is not a measurement

A closed laboratory loop has a reporting problem before it has a science problem. Something has to tell the next stage that the previous stage finished. In every agentic system now being built, that something is the agent's own final message.

This is the first number the vault has for how much that message is worth.

970 agent trajectories on specified scientific workflows. 849 of them did not deliver the required artifacts. 641 of those 849 — 75.5% — ended by saying the work was done. Thirteen runs, 1.5%, correctly reported that they were still going or had not finished.

Why this is worse than a 75.5% error rate

The instinctive read is "the agent is wrong three quarters of the time when it says it's finished." That understates it, and the correct reading is a likelihood-ratio argument any engineer will recognise.

Completion language appears in 90.1% of runs that genuinely passed, and in 75.5% of runs that failed — and above 78% in every failing band scoring over 50. Those two rates are close. So observing "the agent said it was done" barely shifts your belief about whether it was: the likelihood ratio is near 1. The signal is not noisy. It is nearly absent. A system that logged nothing at all and assumed success would be making almost the same inference.

The same holds for the error log, and here the direction is comic. At least one tool error occurred in 80.7% of failing runs — and 94.2% of passing runs. Median error rates were 4.4 per 100 tool results for failures and 5.6 for successes. The successful runs erred more. Which makes sense on reflection: a run that gets far enough to encounter and recover from errors is a run that got far. An error count is a measure of activity, not of correctness, and monitoring it as a health signal will systematically flag your best runs.

What this leaves as the only thing that worked

The benchmark's verdicts came from task-specific executable graders checking a contract — is every required deliverable present, does it run, do the numbers in the report match the numbers in the table. External, mechanical, adversarial to the agent's account of itself. That is the only instrument in the study that distinguished a finished job from an unfinished one.

This is the same architecture the vault has now recorded four times from four unrelated directions, and the convergence is the point:

  • a robotics group pairing execution with a separate verification agent that visually checks each step completed before proceeding;
  • a provenance system binding every numeric claim to the tool output that produced it, so a report cannot assert a number that no instrument returned;
  • an execution-assurance measurement showing detection of silent physical failure is a distinct capability from avoiding it;
  • and now a benchmark showing that without an external contract check, an agent's self-report is indistinguishable between success and failure.

Every one of them says: detection is worth more than avoidance, and it must not be produced by the thing being detected.

What it means for a toxicology loop specifically

This vault's loop-readiness axis asks whether a machine could make the call — read the plate, apply the guideline's rule. It has never asked whether anyone can tell that the run which produced the plate actually happened correctly, and it has been silently assuming the answer is yes.

A regulatory testing context makes this sharper than the benchmark's own framing. The paper's finding is "partial delivery is not delivery." In a submission, partial delivery is not partial value — it is no value. A study report whose figures disagree with its tables is not 87% of a study report. The thing that makes a toxicology result usable is that every number in it is consistent with every other number and with the raw data, and that is precisely the property the graders here were checking and the agents were failing while announcing success.

The honest ceiling. Materials, chemistry and ten life-science tasks; no toxicology. A preprint, not peer reviewed, from a commercial AI lab evaluating its own model on its own benchmark (it lost). Single runs, one model snapshot in August 2026 — the specific percentages will move. The shape will not: self-report and error logs are the two things an autonomous system produces about itself for free, and both were measured here to carry almost no information about whether the science landed.
