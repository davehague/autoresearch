The in-vivo test this endpoint does not have

What the vault already knew before this source arrived

Phototoxicity is unusual among the endpoints in this vault, and the vault recorded why on 2026-07-11 from OECD Guidance Document 397: this endpoint has no validated in-vivo method at all. There is no animal Test Guideline that the non-animal methods were built to replace. The reference standard against which the in-vitro phototoxicity tests are judged is human clinical data — case reports and controlled photopatch studies in people.

That is a genuinely different situation from, say, skin sensitisation or eye irritation, where a non-animal method is measured against a specific, guideline-defined animal test with its own known reproducibility. It also means the phrase "consistent with in vivo tests" should stop a reader in this domain.

The sentence, and the hole in it

The validation claim in this source is: "Results consistent with in vivo tests were obtained for all nine substances." Nine out of nine.

Three things are unread and all three are load-bearing:

  1. Which in-vivo tests. A guinea-pig photo-irritation protocol? A rodent study run in-house? Published human photopatch data described loosely as "in vivo"? These have very different standing, and the endpoint's own guidance says the validated one does not exist.
  2. How the comparator itself was validated. If the reference answers come from a method with no guideline, "agreement" measures agreement between two unvalidated things.
  3. The four positives. Nine substances break into four phototoxic UVB absorbers and five non-phototoxic controls. The four positives carry the entire detection claim — every statement about the method's sensitivity rests on four compounds, and no sensitivity figure can be computed from four.

This is not an accusation that the comparator is bad. It is a statement that the vault cannot grade the central claim of the source without it, and that the reference-standard problem is the endpoint's, not the authors'.

Why the applied half is the least informative half

The paper's second experiment ran five agrochemicals — etoxazole, tolclofos-methyl, esfenvalerate, mandestrobin and flumioxazin — that had tested negative under UVA in Test Guideline 432 and are UVB absorbers. On the paper's own premise, those UVA negatives are exactly the results you cannot trust. Under UVB, all five were negative again.

Two readings, and the honest answer is that this study cannot distinguish them:

  • The compounds are genuinely not phototoxic in either band, and a real gap in the testing scheme has been checked and closed for them.
  • The method's sensitivity, established on four reference positives in the same laboratory, is not sufficient to be sure a negative means anything on a new chemical class.

A negative result from a new method demonstrates nothing about the method. Only positives do. The agrochemical arm produces zero of them, which is why the whole detection case still rests on four reference compounds.

And ⚠ who ran it matters here. All three authors work for Sumitomo Chemical, a major agrochemical manufacturer: the company built the method, and then used it to return a clean result on its own industry's product class. funding_flag: [industry, method-developer]. That is recorded at the front, not the back.

What would settle it

An independent laboratory, a larger and chemically diverse reference set with more positives, a stated prediction model, and an explicit account of what the in-vivo comparator was. None of the four exists yet. Until then the vault records this as a real and well-motivated gap in the phototoxicity scheme, with a proposed fix at proof-of-concept weight — and keeps the spectral blind-spot finding, which does not depend on this source's validation, separate from the method's own standing.
