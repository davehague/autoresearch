The loop's hands, finally measured — and they are the weakest part

This vault has been assembling the autonomous lab from parts, one source at a time: the mind (Robin — generating and prioritising hypotheses), the hands at scale (Tox21 — ~10,000 chemicals in triplicate in a week), the wire (LAP — an agent-to-instrument protocol), the executor (ProtoPilot — running protocols end to end), and the ledger (ToxMCP — binding every numeric claim to a tool output for audit). Each arrived with an honest ceiling recorded.

None of them measured general physical dexterity, and this vault has been assuming it. Tox21 is why: robots demonstrably run plates at industrial scale, so the hands looked solved. This source says the hands are not solved — they are the least solved thing in the stack.

What was measured. A simulated benchmark of 12 basic lab tasks — moving samples, handling culture-ware, operating devices, placing things precisely — with three general robot-learning policies trained on 30 demonstrations each:

The best number on the board is roughly seven successes in ten — for picking up a tube. In a simulator, where nothing is wet, nothing is transparent, nothing has surface tension, nothing condenses, and nothing behaves unexpectedly. The companion system paper from the same group names transparent and reflective labware as an open problem, which is the tell: simulated numbers here are the optimistic case.

Why Tox21 does not rescue this. Tox21's robots achieve near-perfect reliability, and they do it by giving up the only thing a closed loop needs: choice. Tox21 is a fixed workflow, one plate format, purpose-built hardware, one national programme, many years. That is automation, and this vault has recorded from the start that Tox21 is automatable-open-loop — the robots were never the missing piece. The missing piece is what happens when an AI decides the next experiment needs a different vessel, a different instrument, or a step nobody pre-programmed. That requires general manipulation, and general manipulation is at 44–72% in simulation.

The arithmetic that should worry an engineer. A real assay is not one manipulation. It is a chain — thaw, seed, incubate, dose, wash, fix, stain, read — with dozens of physical steps. Even at a generous 95% per-step success (far above anything measured here), a 20-step protocol completes 36% of the time. At 90% it is 12%. Reliability compounds multiplicatively, and everything else in the loop — the hypothesis generator, the decision rule, the provenance ledger — sits downstream of a chain that has to complete before there is any data to reason about. A loop whose sophisticated parts are gated on an unreliable physical layer does not degrade gracefully; it produces confident analysis of runs that silently went wrong.

Which is exactly why the companion architecture matters more than the raw success rate: the same group's system pairs execution with a verification agent that checks visually whether each step actually completed before proceeding. Detecting the failure is worth more than avoiding it. A loop that knows it dropped the tube can retry; a loop that doesn't, analyses garbage. That is the same argument the vault made for ToxMCP's provenance bundles, arriving now from the physical end.

strength: single-study, stance: pushes-back — a non-peer-reviewed simulation benchmark authored by the group whose system it evaluates, built on a commercial 3D-asset pipeline. It does not establish what real lab robots do. It establishes that nobody has demonstrated they do it well, and that this vault's loop-readiness axis has been grading the decision half of the loop while taking the execution half on trust. The axis measures whether a machine could make the call. It has never asked whether a machine can reliably run the plate that produces the data the call is made on.
