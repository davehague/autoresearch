The loop can be perfectly logged and still leave a wrong conclusion untraceable

This vault has spent weeks on whether a machine can run an assay and decide what its numbers mean. This source asks the question that sits after both: when the machine has run and decided, what record exists that lets anyone check it?

The claimed answer today is: a trace. Autonomous systems log prompts, tool calls, completions, timings — a nested tree of everything that happened. The argument here is that this tree is necessary and does not answer the questions a scientist asks:

"A span-centric system asks, 'which call happened next?' … A scientist auditing an autonomous system asks different questions: which candidate contains this claim, what evidence did it read, which evaluator accepted it, and what branch selected it?"

The precedent cited is not hypothetical. Autonomous materials-synthesis systems have produced novelty and yield claims that required later correction or dispute after the loop had completed. The loop ran, the claim was published, and the log did not make the error findable.

The five audit questions, and the two that this vault has no axis for

QuestionThe failure it exposes
Where did this result come from?the final candidate descends from a failed or out-of-policy branch
What supports this claim?a manuscript cites a table whose value contradicts the sentence
Who or what accepted it?one unstable evaluator dominates a multi-evaluator pipeline
How was the search steered?a human correction is overwritten by a later autonomous step
What was discarded?negative results and failed replications disappear from the record

The first three are recognisable versions of things the vault already tracks. The last two are not, and both are load-bearing for toxicology specifically.

"A human correction overwritten by a later autonomous step" is the failure mode hiding underneath every human-in-the-loop tier in this vault. That tier records that a trained person is load-bearing in the call. It says nothing about whether the person's call survives — whether a later automated step re-derives, averages away, or silently supersedes it. In an unattended overnight campaign nobody would notice. The vault grades the presence of the human and has never graded the durability of what the human did.

"Negative results and failed replications disappear from the record" is publication bias implemented in software. The vault already holds a source on the missing-negative-results problem in NAM literature; this is the machine-speed version of it, and it is worse, because a discarded candidate in an automated archive leaves no author to have chosen not to publish it. It is also the exact structural weakness in the-third-argument-for-dropping-an-animal-test: a decision that an experiment is not worth running produces no data to later contradict it.

The design idea, in one line

Make the claim an object. Not an annotation buried in a generated document — a first-class artifact with its own identity, evidence references, optional bindings recording which field was read and which value extracted, and verifier outputs typed as supported / unsupported / contradicted / needs_human_review. The consequence the authors draw is the part worth keeping:

"This lets a later system invalidate a claim without rewriting history. The old claim remains in the log, a new fitness record captures the contradiction, and a revision operator can produce a corrected claim."

That is an append-only ledger in which a finding can be downgraded without being erased — which is, structurally, exactly what this vault's own dated through-line bullets and both-ways Status rule are for. Two literatures reaching the same design from opposite ends is worth noticing, and the vault has recorded that convergence pattern before.

What it is not

Nothing was built and nothing was measured. No implementation, no case study, no reviewer study; the paper's own Section 8 proposes those evaluations in the conditional. No toxicology, no assay, no endpoint, no chemical — closed-loop laboratories appear as one of six described trace shapes. And the authors state the limit themselves, which is the sentence to carry:

"A trace can faithfully record a bad novelty criterion or a biased benchmark. The point is that the criterion and benchmark become visible objects."

Observability is not correctness. A perfectly instrumented loop that trusts a battery with a correlated blind spot will produce a perfectly auditable trail of a systematic error. What the trail buys is the ability to find it afterwards — which the vault should not undersell, because at the moment there is nothing to find it with.

The open problem the authors name is unanswered for this domain: granularity. In toxicology, what counts as the auditable individual — the plate, the well, the replicate, the concentration point, or the derived potency estimate? Nobody has said.
