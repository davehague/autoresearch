No single switch: which nuclear receptor drives a drug's fatty-liver effect depends on the drug

hepatotoxicity-steatosis-battery is built on the premise that a tiered in-silico layer can predict which ligand-activated transcription factor a chemical perturbs, then measure the downstream consequences. This paper tests that premise mechanistically, from the receptor side rather than the chemical side, and the answer complicates it further.

What was done

Individual nuclear receptors were knocked down, one at a time, in HepG2 and differentiated HepaRG liver cells. The cells were then exposed to four drugs already known to cause steatosis as a side effect: valproic acid (an epilepsy drug — also the steatosis map's own reference chemical), amiodarone (a heart-rhythm drug), tamoxifen, and rifampicin. A parallel arm used primary human hepatocyte spheroids, comparing the drugs' effects against direct pharmacological activation of specific receptor pathways (liver X receptor, PPAR-alpha, PPAR-gamma).

What was found

The fatty-acid positive control behaved as expected: knocking down receptors strongly changed oleic-acid- induced lipid accumulation. That is the sanity check working.

The drugs did not behave as one conserved pathway would predict. Which receptor's knockdown mattered, and how much, depended on the specific drug. In HepaRG cells, losing a receptor changed the baseline lipid state of the cell more than it changed the valproic-acid-specific response — the knockdown's effect was mostly about the cell's resting biology, not about blocking the drug's mechanism. In the spheroid arm, directly activating LXR, PPAR-alpha or PPAR-gamma produced strong lipid accumulation on its own, while most of the four drugs produced only modest responses by comparison — the drugs are not simply acting as agonists of the receptors whose direct activation produces the strongest phenotype.

Why this lands on the steatosis battery specifically, and sharpens an existing finding rather than opening a new one

hepatotoxicity-steatosis-battery already carries two independent findings (2026-08-13, Finnish PFAS reanalysis; 2026-10-21, OBERON's DEHP multi-system network) that the steatosis adverse outcome pathway's "conserved core" is reached by system-specific routes — different biological systems, same endpoint, different paths. This paper adds the same structure one level down, inside a single system: even within one cell type, which receptor is load-bearing is compound-specific, not one pathway. Three independent sources now converge on the same shape from three different angles (across systems; across chemical classes; now across receptors within one system), which strengthens the pattern considerably while still not constituting a validation of any specific prediction rule.

What this means for the battery's in-silico triage layer: that layer's job is to predict which receptor a chemical perturbs, in order to decide which downstream key events to measure. If receptor involvement is as compound-specific as this paper suggests, a triage step trained on a small reference set risks mispredicting the relevant receptor for a novel chemical and consequently measuring the wrong — or an incomplete — set of key events. This is a mechanistic reason for the battery's own domain_gate, not a new blocker; loop_blocker and domain_gate on the steatosis map are unaffected, since nothing here changes whether a decision rule exists.

The honest ceiling

Single laboratory (Maastricht University), two authors, four drugs, no accuracy figure, no comparison to the steatosis battery's own nine-chemical set, and no claim by the authors that this resolves which receptor should anchor a screening assay. evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory, regulatory_status: none, funding_flag: [none] — an academic mechanistic study with no commercial platform to defend.
