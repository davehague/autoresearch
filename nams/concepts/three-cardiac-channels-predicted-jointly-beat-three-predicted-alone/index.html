Predicting three heart channels at once, and doing worst on the one everybody screens for

The scientific claim is a correction of a known over-simplification. For twenty years the standard early cardiac-safety screen has asked one question: does this molecule block hERG, a potassium channel in heart muscle? Blocking it is a risk factor for Torsades de Pointes, a sometimes-fatal rhythm disturbance. But a heartbeat is the product of several currents, and a molecule that blocks potassium and calcium can be safer than one that blocks potassium alone, because the effects partly cancel. A single-channel answer to a multi-channel problem is precisely why the field built the CiPA initiative, and this vault holds that story on cardiotoxicity-hipsc-cm-proarrhythmia.

This is the computational version of the same correction: one model, three channels — hERG (potassium), Cav1.2 (calcium), Nav1.5 (sodium) — predicted jointly from molecular structure alone.

What the numbers say, and where they are weakest

Area under the ROC curve is the probability the model ranks a random blocker above a random non-blocker; 0.5 is a coin toss and 1.0 is perfect.

ChannelROC-AUC across three external sets
Cav1.2 (calcium)0.93 – 0.98
Nav1.5 (sodium)0.88 – 0.96
hERG (potassium)0.79 – 0.95

The channel with the most public data and the most regulatory attention is the one the model does worst on. That is worth stating rather than smoothing over — a 0.79 floor is useful for triage and is not a verdict on a compound.

The ranges are wide because the authors did something disciplined: they built three external validation sets stratified by Tanimoto similarity to the training set, deliberately grading the test by how chemically unlike the training compounds it is. The floor of each range is the model on unfamiliar chemistry. Reporting that spread rather than one flattering headline number is exactly what an applicability domain means, and most models in this vault do not report it. It is the paper's best feature.

Multitask beat single-task consistently, which is the mechanistic argument: the three channels share pharmacophores — the structural features that make a molecule bind — so learning them together learns the underlying chemistry rather than three shadows of it. Potency regression reached R² = 0.94 (multitask), though the abstract does not say on which validation set, so this note does not attribute it to the hardest.

SHAP attribution — a method for explaining which input features drove a prediction — is reported to align with known ion-channel pharmacophores. That is a plausibility check against existing chemistry knowledge, not an accuracy measurement, and it must not be read as one.

What does not change

in-silico-qsar-read-across's standing split holds exactly as it stood: computation is a supporting tool within a weight-of-evidence assessment nearly everywhere, and a licensed stand-alone replacement in exactly one bounded domain — ICH M7, bacterial mutagenicity of pharmaceutical impurities. Nothing here touches that boundary. regulatory_status: none. The paper states the platform supports screening "in line with the CiPA paradigm", and alignment with a paradigm is not acceptance by a regulator — this vault does not infer one axis from another, and there is nothing here to infer from anyway.

loop_readiness: computational-connective — off the ladder, as every pure model is. This is part of the model layer a loop would update, not an assay a loop would run.

Two brakes on the science. It is retrospective throughout: every figure is agreement with channel data that already existed, and nothing was predicted before it was measured. And the full text was not obtainable, so the split protocol, the leakage controls and the definition of the blockade label could not be checked; the numbers above are the abstract's own. The commercial brake is a separate object and lives in the-second-cardiac-rule-this-vault-cannot-read.
