Two labs read the same glowing cell and call it two different things

The adopted method. OECD Test Guideline 442D is the lab-grown-cell test for the second step in how skin allergy develops: does a chemical put keratinocytes — the main cell type of the skin's outer layer — into a defensive state? It reads that state through ARE-Nrf2 induction. Nrf2 is the protein a cell releases when it comes under oxidative or electrophilic stress; it switches on protective genes through DNA sequences called antioxidant response elements (AREs). TG 442D wires a firefly-luciferase gene behind those elements, so the cells glow in proportion to Nrf2 activity, and the guideline converts that glow into sensitiser / non-sensitiser through fixed thresholds. Five methods now occupy that slot — KeratinoSens™, LuSens, EpiSensA, Epi²SensA and α-Sens® — and all five read the same event.

The new study. Armouch et al. built a redox-profiling assay in human keratinocytes under physiological oxygen, saw salicylic acid activate Nrf2, and wrote that they interpret it "as antioxidant-response engagement rather than skin-sensitization classification."

Same cell type. Same pathway. Same measurement. Opposite conclusions about what it means.

Why this is a real finding and not a semantic quarrel

Because the adopted assay's own performance numbers say the disagreement is not hypothetical. KeratinoSens's specificity against the mouse Local Lymph Node Assay is 76% — 84 of 110 non-sensitisers correctly called negative. Roughly one non-sensitiser in four is already being flagged as an allergen by the adopted test. The vault has carried that number since 2026-07-05 as a bare statistic with the phrase "specificity is middling" attached to it and no mechanistic account of what the misses have in common.

This supplies a candidate account: Nrf2 activation is the cell's general chemical-stress response, and skin sensitisation is one of several things that trigger it. Anything that engages the antioxidant response — an oxidant, a redox-active particle, a mild irritant, a chemical that perturbs the cell's peroxide balance without ever haptenating a protein — produces the reading the guideline converts into "allergen."

That is a structural false-positive class, not a noise floor, and structure is exactly what the vault's correlated-blind-spot rail is built to notice. It has consequences the raw specificity number does not carry:

  • It is directional. The error is over-calling, not under-calling. An automated system running TG 442D is systematically biased toward declaring things allergens, which is the safe direction for public health and the expensive direction for a testing programme.
  • It reproduces perfectly. A redox-active non-allergen will give the same positive every single time, in every one of the five methods, in every lab. Reproducibility does not detect it. The vault has made this argument before about batteries; here it applies to repetition of a single assay.
  • ⚠ And it may be a correlated blind spot inside the KE2 slot itself. All five adopted methods read ARE-Nrf2. A laboratory that runs two of them for confidence has run one measurement twice. The 2-out-of-3 defined approach protects against this by requiring three different key events (chemical protein-binding, keratinocyte activation, dendritic-cell activation), which is a design that already anticipated the problem — the risk is at the level of the individual key-event slot, not the battery.

What must not be read into it

This does not downgrade TG 442D, and this vault is not doing so. The Status on skin-sensitization-keratinosens stays consensus and its loop_readiness stays closed-loop-ready, both correctly. The reasons are specific:

  1. A four-material proof of concept from the method's own developers is not a credible higher-tier result against an adopted guideline with formal validation and ~85% inter-laboratory reproducibility behind it. Status aggregation is highest-tier-wins; this is the lowest tier the vault grades.
  2. Salicylic acid was not tested as a sensitisation question, was not run in TG 442D, and no comparison against the guideline's thresholds is reported. The two results were produced by different assays for different purposes, and reading one as refuting the other is more than the evidence carries.
  3. The guideline already says this. TG 442D describes a single key event, explicitly not a stand-alone hazard call, and exists to be combined with the other two key events. The over-call this note describes is a known property of a screen designed to be used inside a battery.

So the finding is not "the assay is wrong." It is "the assay's known false positives now have a proposed mechanism, and the mechanism predicts which chemicals they are" — redox-active ones. That is a domain_gate-shaped claim rather than a status claim, and it is one an automated loop should carry, because a loop running the KE2 slot on a library of oxidants and nanoparticles will generate a confident, reproducible run of allergen calls that a human toxicologist would have looked at twice.

Recorded as an open question rather than as an established carve-out: nobody has tested how much of TG 442D's 24-point specificity gap is redox-driven, and until somebody does, this is a hypothesis with a plausible mechanism and one anecdote.
