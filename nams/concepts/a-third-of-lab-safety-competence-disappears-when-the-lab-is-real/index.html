Knowing the rule and applying it in a room are two different scores, and the gap is a third

LABSHIELD asks 32 multimodal models — models that see images as well as read text — two kinds of question about the same 164 laboratory tasks. The first kind is a safety question. The second kind is: here is the scene; what do you do?

The gap between the two is 32.0% on average.

The grading standard is not invented for the paper. Tasks are scored against OSHA, the US workplace safety regulator's rules, and GHS, the international scheme that decides which hazard pictogram goes on a chemical container — and which is, incidentally, the classification the toxicology assays in this vault exist to produce. So the benchmark is measuring against the same published hazard vocabulary the field already uses.

The failures concentrate in two places the authors name: hazard interpretation — noticing that something in the visual scene is dangerous — and safety-aware planning — sequencing actions so as not to create a hazard. Both are situated capabilities. Neither is tested by asking a model what the GHS pictogram for a corrosive means.

Why this belongs in a toxicology vault. Every closed-loop-ready rating here is a claim that a machine could execute an assay and apply its rule. For the physical assays that is not an abstraction: a closed loop for skin corrosion involves an agent handling corrosives; one for phototoxicity involves handling photoreactive compounds under UV; one for genotoxicity involves handling mutagens. The loop-readiness axis says nothing about whether the operator can be left alone with them, and the axis was never designed to — but the reader of a closed-loop-ready rating is entitled to know that the only measurement of that capability puts current systems a third below their own quiz scores.

The pattern this is the second instance of. In the same run, the vault ingested a measurement of autonomous research agents failing to refuse false scientific premises (seven-research-agents-and-a-273-percent-ceiling-on-refusing-nonsense). Both are the same shape: a general-capability score that does not survive contact with the situated task, and in both cases the deficit is in judgement about the situation rather than in the mechanics of the task. It rhymes with two findings already here — that a model's strongest general capabilities did not include structured lookup (the-thinking-half-of-the-loop-is-worst-at-looking-things-up), and that turning up reasoning effort produced more confident wrongness (more-reasoning-effort-produced-more-hallucinated-signal). Four independent sources now say the same thing in different words: capability measured in the abstract is a poor predictor of capability in the loop, and it fails in the optimistic direction.

Limits, and they are real. Unrefereed preprint, one group, one benchmark of its own design. The vault's own audit source says every agentic benchmark examined had validity flaws (every-agentic-benchmark-audited-had-validity-flaws), and that applies here. Most specifically: a multiple-choice track and a planning track are not obviously on the same scale, so part of the 32.0% may be a property of the measurement rather than of the models, and the abstract does not settle it. And there is no toxicological endpoint, guideline, or assay anywhere in this paper — it reaches this vault through GHS alone.

regulatory_status: none, and that is the durable finding rather than the number: no regulator anywhere has set a safety-competence bar that a laboratory agent must clear before it is allowed to run experiments unsupervised. The bar does not exist to be failed.
