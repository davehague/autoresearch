The better method is the commercially worse one, and two vendors said so

The vault's adoption picture has been built on a single implicit assumption: that the obstacle to replacing animal tests is confidence. Get the science strong enough, get a regulator to accept it, and use follows. The vault already holds one measurement pushing against that — a survey of seventeen food companies naming regulatory acceptance rather than scientific confidence as the binding constraint (regulatory-acceptance-is-the-dominant-adoption-barrier-by-user-report).

This is a different and sharper mechanism, and it is stated by two people who sell the methods.

"LOAELs from rodent studies are not protective of human health unless non-empirically derived fit-for-purpose uncertainty factors are applied. Conversely, points of departure derived from NAMs yield more conservative, protective, hazard estimates. While this approach is arguably an improvement for assessing potential human health risk, it may shrink safety margins used to advance product development, which could be a deterrent for industry acceptance."

Read the mechanism carefully, because it is not cynicism

A point of departure is the dose where an effect starts. Everything downstream — the safe exposure level, the margin a product must clear — is computed from it. A lower point of departure is a more protective one, and it makes the product's headroom smaller.

So the sequence is: the non-animal method is more human-relevant, therefore it flags effects at lower doses, therefore the calculated safety margin shrinks, therefore the ingredient that passed under the animal test may not pass under the replacement. Nobody has to act in bad faith for this to slow adoption. Every party can prefer the more accurate method in principle and still find that adopting it reclassifies products they already sell.

What it does to the vault's three axes

Nothing. No method's evidence_tier, regulatory_status or loop_readiness moves — this is not a claim about any assay's performance.

What it does is name a force the vault's model has no slot for. The vault grades whether a method is valid, whether it is accepted, and whether a machine could run it. It has a fourth map for whether anyone actually uses it (adoption-system-dynamics-nam-uptake), populated so far with survey answers and systems diagrams. This is the first source giving that map a mechanism with a direction: adoption is resisted in proportion to how much the replacement moves the answer, and the replacement's whole selling point is that it moves the answer.

The loop reading

The autonomous-lab case for toxicology is usually made as a throughput argument — run more chemicals, faster. This says the binding constraint may be on the output side rather than the input side. A loop that generates protective points of departure at scale generates, at scale, the number a regulated party has an interest in not having. Automating a measurement does not automate acceptance of what it measures, and the vault should stop treating "could a machine run it" as the last question before adoption.

The interest to declare, and it cuts the useful way

funding_flag: [industry, method-developer] — both authors sell the assays in question (Toxys, Litron). That normally makes a claim about those assays suspect. Here the claim is against their own interest: they are saying the market has a reason to resist their products that has nothing to do with whether the products work. Recorded with that noted, and still carried as strength: suggestive — the direction rests entirely on five cited studies the vault has not read, and the vault holds no measurement of the size of the effect.
