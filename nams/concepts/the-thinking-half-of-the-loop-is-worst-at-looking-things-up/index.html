The thinking half of the loop is worst at looking things up

The vault splits a closed loop into three parts: a robot that runs the assay, an AI that picks the next experiment, and a model that updates. It already holds a benchmark for the robot part (Pipette, 2026-07-22 — simulation only). LABBench2 measures the middle part, and its result is not the one the loop narrative expects.

The expectation is that the AI's limit is reasoning. The paper says otherwise: "improvements in reasoning alone are insufficient." The measured bottleneck is finding the right document and locating the right thing inside it, and the single weakest family is DbQA2 — navigate a specialised scientific database with non-standard schemas, identifiers and near-duplicate entity names, and pull out an exact record. It stays the hardest family even when the models are given web search and code execution.

The benchmark's headline is the same point in aggregate: replacing multiple-choice answers and hand-fed context with open responses and retrieval costs frontier models between 26 and 46 percentage points. A quarter to a half of the apparent capability was the format.

Why this lands on toxicology specifically

Run through what a tox loop actually does between plates:

  • pull a chemical's record from the US EPA CompTox Chemicals Dashboard
  • find the relevant adverse outcome pathway in the AOP Knowledge Base
  • retrieve prior ToxCast or Tox21 activity for a structural analogue
  • check whether a Test Guideline's applicability domain excludes this substance
  • locate a validation report's reference value for a control chemical

Every one of those is a DbQA2 task, and DbQA2 is where frontier models are weakest. The loop's thinking leg does not mostly reason about biology; it mostly performs precise lookups against heterogeneous, badly standardised registries. Regulatory toxicology's registries are, if anything, worse than the ones in this benchmark: guideline text in PDFs, applicability domains stated in prose, validation reports outside any database at all.

This is the vault's inference from the task descriptions rather than a measured tox result, and it is labelled as such. It is also, in the vault's judgement, the most transferable thing in the paper.

The new task family that describes this vault's own discipline

LABBench2 adds SourceQuality (150 tasks): can an agent "surface the most epistemically salient reason a study is inappropriate for a research question", which the authors distinguish from checklist compliance — "models must combine relevance assessment with methodological scrutiny."

That is very close to the grading this vault runs on every ingest: is this single-lab or ring-tested, who paid for it, does its applicability domain cover the question, is the funder the method's developer. It is now a measurable capability with a public test set, and it is reported as an area where models are not yet strong. Anyone proposing to automate evidence appraisal — as the EU's thyroid validation team proposes to do for potency literature (see the-reference-answer-was-built-by-committee-because-no-animal-test-could-supply-it) — is proposing to automate the thing this benchmark says is hard.

Caveats that limit the claim

A preprint, not peer reviewed, from a group that builds and sells autonomous-science agents. No human expert baseline is reported for LABBench2 itself, so "models are bad at DbQA2" is a statement about models and not about how hard the task is for a person. Per-model scores live in figures rather than tables, so this note quotes only the reported aggregate range. Public dataset and evaluation harness are genuine mitigations; the authorship interest remains.
