The scorer has a domain gate too, and nobody checks it

The claim

"When using DL algorithms, caution must be exercised to appropriately define the range of applicability of a model and avoid domain shift (i.e., discrepancies in statistics between training and test data)."

The vault's domain_gate modifier records applicability-domain carve-outs a loop must check before trusting the decision rule: OECD TG 435 screens out much of pH 4.5–8.5; OECD TG 249 is bounded to log KOW −4 to 7; TG 494 enforces its own gate with a pre-test the robot can run. All of these are chemical gates.

A trained image scorer adds a second, non-chemical gate stacked on top: the set of images the model was trained on. Its boundary is not a property of the compound. It is a property of the microscope, the staining batch, the cell density, the operator's exposure settings, the plate vendor — everything level-zero-the-instrument-nobody-specifies describes. Two chemicals well inside the assay's chemical domain can land inside and outside the model's domain depending on which imaging system ran them.

Why this is worse than it sounds for a closed loop

It is invisible to every check the assay already runs. The assay's acceptance criteria test the biology: did the positive control respond, did the negative control stay quiet, is the variability within range. All of those can pass on images that are out of the model's training distribution. The plate is valid; the scorer is extrapolating; nothing reports it.

And a loop drifts into it by construction. Reagent lots change. A lamp ages. Firmware updates. The loop adapts its own next compounds. Every one of those moves the image statistics, slowly, in a direction nothing is measuring. The assay stays valid the whole time.

The vault has already recorded a concrete instance of this failure mode. carcinogenicity-cell-transformation-assay's escape hatch — Masumoto 2021 — scores AUC 0.95 on the chemical it was trained on and 0.87 on unseen chemicals. That eight-point drop is domain shift, measured, in a published scorer the vault already holds. The vault filed it as "single-lab, weaker evidence." It is more specific than that: it is the model's applicability domain being crossed and the performance cost being visible because someone happened to test for it.

What the paper asks for, and why an engineer will find it familiar

The recommended controls are not toxicology controls. They are release engineering:

  • "Open and transparent ML model training documentation, along with version control of both training data and models"
  • Verification "with a fixed set of input images and by proving that the results obtained are consistent" — a regression test with golden files
  • "The performance of the ML/DL model used for image analysis should be accurately measured with problem-specific metrics" — not accuracy on the abundant class
  • Explicit checks on "label accuracy and class balance to prevent model bias" and validation across "multiple annotators to avoid annotator bias"

The class-balance point lands directly on a finding the vault already holds: genotoxicity-buccal-micronucleus-cytome's reviewed models score 98%/95% on the abundant easy cell classes and 82–85% on the micronucleated cells that carry the entire signal. Textbook imbalance — strongest on the common case, weakest on the event of interest — and exactly what "problem-specific metrics" is meant to catch.

Status

strength: suggestive. This is a recommendation paper, not a measurement, and none of the vault's four machine-vision escape hatches has had its training-set domain characterised. The vault's position after this source: a machine-vision scorer is not a free upgrade to a human-in-the-loop assay. It is a second model, with a second applicability domain, a second version history, and a second way to be silently wrong — and the vault should stop describing any of the four as a straightforward path to promotion.
