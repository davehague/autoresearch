A coverage blank, recorded as a blank

This vault holds maps for skin, eye, liver, kidney, lung, heart, blood, brain, thyroid, testis, and fish. It has held none for the gut, and that absence had never been written down.

Gastrointestinal toxicity is not a marginal endpoint. Damage to the gut lining is among the most common dose-limiting toxicities in cancer treatment, and it is frequently the reason a patient stops therapy — the drug works and the person cannot tolerate it. It is a first-order safety question for a large fraction of the pharmacopoeia.

The non-animal coverage is, as far as this vault's records go, nil:

  • No adopted OECD Test Guideline.
  • No validation programme at ECVAM, ICCVAM or JaCVAM that this vault has ingested.
  • No prediction model: nothing converts a gut-toxicity measurement into a hazard call.
  • No applicability domain, because there is no method to bound.
  • No regulatory acceptance route in any of the vault's eight regulatory-acceptance framework maps.

What exists is a research tool. Colonoids — gut-lining tissue grown from a donor's own stem cells — are genuine human primary tissue with real architecture, and they are the right starting point. They are also, in the authors' own words, epithelium and nothing else: "missing vascular, immune, and systemic context means clinical validation remains necessary."

Those three omissions are not incidental to this endpoint; they are most of its mechanism. Chemotherapy gut injury involves the blood supply that delivers the drug and clears it, the immune cells that turn tissue damage into inflammation, and systemic effects including the microbiome and the rest of the body's handling of the compound. An epithelium-only model can tell you what the drug does to gut lining cells. It cannot, by construction, tell you what happens to a gut.

Why this belongs in a vault about closed loops

Loop-readiness has a floor, and this endpoint is under it. The vault's ladder bottoms out at bench-bound, and that rung splits into two entirely different problems that must never share a label:

  • animal-input — a method exists, is quantitative, has a fixed decision rule, and is bench-bound only because its own maths consumes an animal study. Fixable by inventing a new prediction model.
  • no-nam-exists — there is no non-animal method for the endpoint at all. Fixable only by building assays.

Gastrointestinal toxicity sits close to no-nam-exists at the endpoint level, and the qualifier matters. It is not literally empty — a colonoid is a non-animal method, and the map that holds it is graded automatable-open-loop, because a transcriptomic readout is machine-readable and no agreed interpretation procedure exists to sit on top of it. What is absent is anything a regulator could accept: no validated method, no guideline, no decision rule, no acceptance route. So the endpoint is one rung better off than reproductive/fertility toxicity, which the vault records as genuinely no-nam-exists, and the two are still the same kind of investment — assay and decision-rule development, not robotics. No amount of automation moves an endpoint whose methods have not been shown to predict anything.

The honest framing of the opening source

The study that opens this group is a mechanistic comparison, not a method: three tissue systems, one drug, a transcriptomic readout, no prediction model, one collaboration. Its most useful contribution is a negative one — that the agreement between a human model and an animal study depends on the resolution you score it at, and the rankings flip (species-and-format-imprint-at-different-data-resolutions).

A single source does not make an endpoint covered. The group is opened here so the blank is visible in the index and countable in the coverage-gap analysis, not because the gut now has a method.
