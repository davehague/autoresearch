Making a loop-ready assay more informative moved the new readout down the ladder, not up

OECD Test Guideline 249 is one of a small number of methods in this vault that a machine could run end to end. The reason is not that fish gill cells are special. It is that the guideline pairs an instrument-read output — three viability dyes on a plate reader — with a written, deterministic rule: take the lowest of the three midpoint concentrations and report it. Read the plate, apply the rule, done.

This source keeps the cells and replaces the readout with something far richer: stain multiple organelles, photograph every well, extract hundreds of features per cell, and see what the chemical did at doses that do not kill anything. Ninety-four features resolved the test chemical's effect. Lysosomes moved and mitochondria fragmented before viability dropped. The processing pipeline proved robust — even minimal processing detected the concentration-response.

And there is no rule. Nothing states what magnitude of change, in which features, at what concentration, constitutes a hazard. The paper produces a picture of divergence; the guideline it sits beside produces a number and a comparison.

The general form, which is the point

This is not a criticism of the paper — it is a proof of concept and says so. It is an instance of a pattern worth naming, because the vault will keep meeting it:

Richness and decidability trade against each other, and the loop-readiness axis is measuring decidability.

A viability dye returns one number per well, which is why somebody could write a rule about it. A phenotypic profile returns a high-dimensional fingerprint, which is why nobody has. Every step toward biological realism — more features, more compartments, more time points, sub-lethal sensitivity — enlarges the space that a decision rule would have to cover, and the rule is the scarce object. The vault already holds the human-cell version of this same trap: Cell Painting sits at automatable-open-loop with the note that its blocker is a consequence of the method's design rather than a gap in its guideline.

For anyone choosing what to automate first, the uncomfortable implication is that the assays easiest to close a loop on are the ones that measure least. Nothing here changes TG 249's own grade — its viability readout and its rule are untouched, and this map stays closed-loop-ready on those merits. What changes is that the vault can now name the direction the field is moving in and say what it costs.

And it does not touch the blind spot

Worth stating explicitly because it is the tempting misreading. A gill cell monolayer has no nervous system and no full metabolic competence — which is why this map and the fish embryo test are recorded as one vote, not two. Photographing the same cells in more detail produces a more detailed picture of a cell that still cannot bioactivate a chemical. More features is not more independence. The fix for a correlated blind spot is a measurement that fails differently, and this is a measurement that fails identically in higher resolution.
