The loop closed. Look at what it was pointed at.

The result

GPT-5 designed the experiments. Ginkgo Bioworks' robotic cloud laboratory ran them. The data came back to the model, which designed the next round. Six months, 36,000 reaction compositions across 580+ plates, roughly 150,000 datapoints. Starting zero-shot — the first round used no example reactions at all, only what the model already knew.

It beat the published state of the art for cell-free protein synthesis: $422 per gram of protein against $698, a 40% cost reduction, while simultaneously raising yield — +27% in tubes, +188% in plates.

This is the vault's most complete demonstration that all four legs of a closed loop — design, execute, measure, update — can run together at scale on real wet biology. automation-tox21-qhts-platform has had the robot half since 2013 and never built the decision half. This has both.

The property that made it possible

The objective was a number the instrument reports. Minimise dollars per gram; do not lose titer. Titer is fluorescence, read off a plate reader. Cost is arithmetic over a reagent price list. The loop could evaluate every candidate against its own goal, immediately, with no interpretation step and no external authority.

Regulatory toxicology does not have that number, and this is not a technology gap. Consider what the analogous loop would need:

A closed loop optimises. Hazard testing classifies against an external definition. Optimisation has a gradient in the data; classification against a definition does not. The loop can only get better at hazard calling if the correctness signal reaches it, and in regulatory toxicology that signal lives in a validation report, not in the well.

Which is exactly why the vault's decision-rule blocker is the binding one

The vault's largest category, 14 maps and now 15, is "machine-readable readout, no agreed decision rule." That has always looked like a paperwork problem — someone just needs to write the rule down. This result reframes it: the data-interpretation procedure is the loop's objective function. Without it there is nothing for the loop to optimise, no matter how good the robot is. It is not paperwork; it is the reward.

That also explains why the vault's closed-loop-ready maps really are the loop-ready ones. GL 497's 2-out-of-3, TG 439's ≤50% viability, TG 494's three indexes — those are objective functions. A committee wrote them, which is an unfashionable way to get one, but they are computable in the well, and that is the property that matters.

Honest caveats, stated plainly

  • Not toxicology. No chemical was assessed for harm. Nothing here shows a loop can make a hazard call.
  • Written and funded by the two companies that sell the loop — OpenAI (the model) and Ginkgo (the lab). funding_flag: [industry, method-developer], the maximum the vault records. Not peer reviewed.
  • The autonomy is bounded and the boundary was hand-built — see the-guardrail-was-a-schema-not-the-model.
  • A single study. strength: single-study, evidence_tier: single-lab-exploratory. One laboratory, one problem, one model.

And the mitigation that stops this being pure vendor material: the headline claim is a measured wet-lab quantity — dollars per gram of a standard benchmark protein, cross-checked in three vessel formats against a named prior publication. That is a much harder thing to flatter than a self-scored benchmark, and the vault records it as the reason this preprint is worth holding despite its provenance.
