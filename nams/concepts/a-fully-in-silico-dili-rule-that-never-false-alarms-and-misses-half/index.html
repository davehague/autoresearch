Every input a machine can get, every step deterministic — and it misses half

Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) is a drug damaging the liver. It is a leading cause of drugs failing in development and of drugs being withdrawn after approval, and it is notoriously hard to predict before a human takes the drug.

Geci et al. (2026) assemble a prediction rule out of four parts, and what makes it interesting to this vault is that not one of the four requires a person or an animal:

  1. Cytotoxicity IC50 — the concentration that kills liver cells in a dish. Plate reader.
  2. Mitochondrial toxicity IC50 — the concentration that poisons the cell's energy machinery. Plate reader.
  3. BSEP inhibition IC50 — the concentration that blocks the bile salt export pump, the transporter that pumps bile acids out of liver cells. Blocked, they accumulate and corrode the cell. Plate reader.
  4. Cmax — the peak concentration the drug reaches in blood. In the prospective version this is not measured in anybody: it is simulated by a physiologically based kinetic model, a whole-body ADME simulation whose own inputs are themselves predicted from chemical structure.

The rule: Cmax ÷ (lowest of the three IC50s) ≥ 0.25 → flag. One division and one comparison.

Performance. ROC AUC — pick a harmful drug and a safe drug at random, how often does the method rank the harmful one higher; 50% is chance — reaches 96% using measured Cmax and 91% using the fully simulated one. Dropping the human measurement costs about five points of AUC and changes 13.94% of individual classifications.

And then read the operating point, which is where the interesting thing is. At the chosen threshold the rule is 100% specific and 51% sensitive. On this dataset it flagged no safe drug wrongly and it missed half the drugs that cause liver injury. That is a very unusual shape for a safety screen. Most tox assays in this vault sit the other way round — high sensitivity, leaky specificity, over-calling to avoid missing a hazard (the h-CLAT's 93% sensitivity against 66% specificity is the archetype). This one is built to never cry wolf.

Why that shape is exactly wrong for an unsupervised loop, and exactly right for a triage step. A perfectly specific, half-sensitive rule is a positive oracle: when it fires, believe it. When it does not fire, it has told you nothing, and the failure is silent — no error bar, no abstention, just an absent flag indistinguishable from a clean result. An autonomous system optimising against this signal would learn to avoid the chemical space the rule can see and would be blind, with no internal warning, to the half it cannot. A rule that is never wrong when it speaks and silent half the time is the most dangerous possible input to a loop that treats silence as safety.

The threshold is a judgement frozen into a constant. The authors chose 25% for mechanistic plausibility; the statistically optimal value on their own data was 1.1%, twenty-three times more conservative, which they rejected as implausible. They say plainly that a single fixed threshold "may not have been optimal." So the one number that converts this from a score into a decision is a human call, made once, at a whiteboard — and then it is a constant in a pipeline, and it stops looking like a judgement at all. This is the same pattern the vault records at the-adopted-cut-off-carries-an-uncertainty-band-the-decision-rule-does-not: automatability is a property of the rule, and the rule inherits every assumption of whoever set the number.

Provenance of the numbers. This is a retrospective meta-analysis of published potencies, not a battery anyone ran. Coverage is uneven — 205 of 241 drugs had cytotoxicity data, 170 had BSEP, and only 93 had mitochondrial toxicity. "Lowest of three" is doing different work for different drugs depending on which of the three exists.
