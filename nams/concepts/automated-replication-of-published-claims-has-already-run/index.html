A robot has already re-tested the literature, and about half of it survived the change of conditions

The vault holds a lot of proposals for what automated science could check. This is one of the few things that has actually run on published claims, in a wet laboratory, with the conditions deliberately varied.

Eve — the second robot scientist, built for early-stage drug discovery — was pointed at claims extracted from the cancer-biology literature and re-tested them using two different experimental teams and breast-cancer cell lines. Reported outcome:

  • 43 statements showed significant evidence of repeatability — the result came back.
  • 22 statements showed evidence of reproducibility or robustness — it came back after the operator, team and cell line changed.
  • Two serendipitous findings fell out of the re-testing that nobody was looking for.

Roughly half of what could be repeated also survived a change in who and what did the repeating. Whether that number is encouraging or alarming depends on a base rate the surveyed text does not give: how many claims were attempted in total is not stated, so 43 could be most of the set or a fraction of it. Recorded as a gap rather than guessed at.

The argument that follows it

King's case for automating replication is not that machines check better. It is that humans will not check at all, for reasons that have nothing to do with capability:

"Reproduction studies are difficult to fund, may be difficult to publish and can provoke hostility from the authors of the original work. Researchers receive greater career rewards for claiming a novel discovery than for establishing whether an existing claim is reliable."

So the proposal is a system that extracts testable claims and protocols from papers, formalises them, ranks candidates by importance, uncertainty and feasibility, translates the chosen protocols to whatever automated laboratory is available, runs repeated and independent experiments, updates the probability on each claim, and publishes positive, negative and inconclusive outcomes alike — turning the literature "from a largely static collection of claims into a continuously tested and probabilistically weighted knowledge base."

Why this matters to a toxicology vault specifically

Two reasons, and the second is the sharper one.

First, the shape is already familiar here. A ranked, funded, deliberately-varied re-test of a claim other people published is what a ring trial is. validation-paradigm-nam-confidence records a field mid-argument about whether ring trials are affordable and whether they should be replaced by something continuous. What King describes is not an alternative to that argument — it is one possible implementation of the continuous option, and it is the only one this vault has seen with a wet-lab result attached.

Second, and less comfortably: nothing about the machinery says the re-test is independent. The companion finding from the same survey (a-robot-repeats-a-systematic-error-with-exceptional-precision) is that automation buys repeatability and not reproducibility, because a robot repeats a systematic error precisely, two instruments implement one abstract step differently, and shared software makes several laboratories reproduce one artefact. An automated replication service running one codebase across many sites has, by construction, the property that makes agreement uninformative. The Eve result took care to vary the team and the cell line, which is exactly the right instinct and is the reason it counts for anything. A scaled version that varied neither would produce a large number of confirmations and very little evidence.

The brakes

  • Reported second-hand. This is a 2026 survey's account of a 2022 experiment; the primary paper was not retrieved this run, and the total number of claims tested is unknown.
  • Cancer biology, not toxicology. No regulatory endpoint, no hazard classification, no Test Guideline anywhere in the demonstration.
