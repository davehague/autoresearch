Two of the three barriers are engineering problems wearing lab coats

Kim & Choi name three things standing between in-vitro non-animal methods and real regulatory use. The first is a science problem. The other two are the kind of problem a software engineer solves every week, and they are the ones that decide whether a loop can be built.

Barrier 1 — limited regulatory relevance. In-vitro assays measure upstream mechanistic signals; regulators need to know whether an organism gets sick. "No single NAM currently captures all critical hazard endpoints." This is a genuine scientific gap and no amount of tooling closes it.

Barrier 2 — the dose axis does not mean what it says. Nearly every in-vitro result is reported against the nominal concentration: the amount of chemical pipetted into the well, divided by the volume. That is not the amount that reached the cell. Chemical adsorbs onto plastic, binds to serum proteins, evaporates out of the medium, gets metabolised by the cells themselves. Two labs running the same nominal 10 µM can expose their cells to concentrations that differ by an order of magnitude, and neither will know.

For a loop this is a units bug in the most load-bearing variable in the system. Every decision rule in this vault that reads a concentration — the IC50 threshold, the stimulation-index cut-off, the point of departure — reads a number whose relationship to the physical quantity of interest is uncontrolled and varies by chemical. An active-learning system choosing its next dose is optimising in a space whose axis is mislabelled, and it will do so with perfect internal consistency.

Barrier 3 — nothing agrees on a format. "Inconsistent protocols, data formats, and analytical pipelines across platforms complicate integration." This is the plainest statement of the loop's actual blocker to be found in a risk-assessment review. A closed loop is by definition a data pipeline: assay → parse → model → decide → next assay. The parse step is not optional, and the field has not standardised it. The vault already tracks this from the other end — nam-data-processing-pipeline records that the same raw plate can yield different regulatory calls depending on the processing choices applied to it, and ml-model-reporting-reproducibility records how rarely those choices are written down.

The order of difficulty is inverted from how the field discusses it. Barrier 1 gets the conference sessions; barriers 2 and 3 get the appendices. But barrier 1 does not stop a loop from running — a loop happily optimises against an incomplete hazard picture, which is precisely what makes it dangerous. Barriers 2 and 3 stop it from being built at all, and unlike barrier 1 they have known solutions: measure free concentration, publish schemas, version pipelines. The scientific gap is the one nobody can fix and the engineering gaps are the ones nobody is funded to.

The review's proposed architecture — anchor to an adverse outcome pathway, bridge the dose gap with high-throughput toxicokinetics (pbk-qivive-toxicokinetics), apply in tiers — is a direct answer to barriers 1 and 2 and says nothing about barrier 3.
