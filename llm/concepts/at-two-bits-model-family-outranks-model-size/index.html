Eight models, one method, and the size trend goes the wrong way

small-models-amplify-quantization-error-large-ones-attenuate-it is a load-bearing claim on post-training-quantization, and ultra-low-bit-is-a-large-model-technique confirmed it a day earlier with three clean points at fixed everything else — 7.92 points lost at 7B, 6.21 at 13B, 4.20 at 70B. Both are within-family sweeps.

This source supplies the test neither had: eight checkpoints from three families, one method, one bit width, one calibration protocol, one codebase. Two of the three families run the other way.

Llama2 behaves as the vault's rule predicts. Llama3.x and Qwen3 both invert it, and Llama3-8B — the largest model in either — takes the largest hit in the table.

The obvious objection is a floor effect, and it is partly right. These are six multiple-choice tasks whose collapsed baselines land near 38%, so a model starting at 54.19 has 16 points of room to lose and one starting at 74.61 has 37. Normalizing against that floor is the fairer comparison:

  • Llama2: 57.3% (7B), 69.6% (13B) of headroom retained
  • Qwen3: 38.2% (0.6B), 40.7% (4B), 34.1% (8B)
  • Llama3.x: 31.5% (1B), 31.9% (3B), 28.8% (8B)

The floor correction does not rescue the rule. It replaces it with a cleaner statement: family separates by a factor of two and size barely separates at all. Llama2-13B keeps 70% of its headroom at 2 bits while Llama3-8B, a smaller and stronger model, keeps 29%. Inside Llama3.x the three sizes span 3 points; inside Qwen3, 7.

The most likely explanation is one the field already suspects and this paper never mentions: training-token density. Llama2 is the least token-dense family here by a wide margin, Llama3 and Qwen3 the most. A checkpoint that has had more information packed into the same parameters has less redundancy for a 2-bit grid to throw away. That is a hypothesis this study is consistent with and does not test, which is why this note is suggestive and not strong — no ablation isolates token density from architecture, tokenizer, or post-training recipe, all of which also differ across these families.

What it licenses is a scope condition rather than a reversal. The amplification law is a within-family statement, and at 2 bits it is dominated. ultra-low-bit-is-a-large-model-technique is still right about why you would go to 2 bits — to make something fit that otherwise does not — and its practitioner conclusion survives intact. What does not survive is using model size as a proxy for how much a 2-bit run will cost you. On a modern, token-dense checkpoint, the answer is "more than the scaling story suggests," and the only reliable way to find out is to measure that checkpoint.

A second bound worth attaching: this is the same multiple-choice family that quantization-preserves-the-answer-and-changes-the-derivation identifies as least sensitive to compression damage, so every magnitude here is a floor and the family separation could widen or narrow on generative tasks.
