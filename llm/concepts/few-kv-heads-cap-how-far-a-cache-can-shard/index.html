Few KV heads cap how far a cache can shard

sharing-kv-heads-buys-decode-speed-at-near-zero-quality-cost is one of the cleanest results transformer-architecture holds: GQA-8 reaches roughly MHA quality at 5.4× decode speed, and the field adopted it as a default without much argument. This is the bill, arriving two months later from the serving side, and it does not refute the result — it locates it.

Tensor parallelism partitions the KV cache by attention head. That is the smallest divisible unit available, so the number of KV heads is a hard ceiling on how many ranks the cache can be spread across. Below the ceiling, TP splits it cleanly. Above it, the cache duplicates: raise TP for compute and you multiply the cache back. Multi-head latent attention is the limiting case — one shared latent vector across all query heads, nothing to split by, so the cache is replicated in full on every rank.

So the same architectural decision reads opposite ways at different scales. On one device, fewer KV heads is strictly good: smaller cache, faster decode, negligible quality cost. Across devices, fewer KV heads means fewer shards, and the memory saved per device is handed back as replication across devices. A cache reduction and a cache partition are competing uses of the same head count, and the architecture papers that established the first were not measuring the second.

The practical form is a threshold rather than a warning. The replication only starts once tensor_parallel_size exceeds num_key_value_heads, so a local multi-GPU box — two cards, TP=2, a GQA model with num_key_value_heads=8 — has not crossed it and sees only the win. The problem is a datacenter problem in its origin and a scaling problem in general: it appears exactly when you add GPUs to serve longer contexts or more concurrency, which is the same moment the cache is under most pressure. a-replicated-cache-can-be-sharded-instead-of-compressed is the runtime-level answer, and its existence is the evidence that the constraint bites in production.

Worth noting what this does not say. Nothing here argues for going back to MHA. The quality-per-byte case for GQA and MLA stands, and DCP restores the shardability without giving up the compression — the two compose. What it changes is how the vault reads architectural efficiency claims generally: an architectural choice that removes a dimension also removes the axis somebody downstream was parallelizing along, and that cost lands on a different team, measured on different hardware, months later.

Bounds. T2, and the structural claim is the strong half — it follows from how TP partitions state and is stated plainly by the people who implement it. The quantitative half is one configuration (8×B200, Kimi K2.6, NVFP4) and is really a measurement of the fix, not of the constraint. No sweep over num_key_value_heads, no crossover point measured, and no comparison of GQA-8 against MLA under matched TP, which is the experiment that would price this properly.
