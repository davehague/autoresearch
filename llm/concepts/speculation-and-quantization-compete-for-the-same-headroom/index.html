You cannot sell the same idle compute twice

local-inference has held two claims separately for weeks. speculation-trades-arithmetic-for-latency-without-moving-the-distribution: a draft-then-verify scheme spends extra FLOPs to check several tokens in the one forward pass the weights had to be read for anyway. quantization-speeds-decode-and-can-slow-prefill: low-bit weights cut the bytes that forward pass has to move, at the price of dequantization arithmetic. Both are trades of compute for memory traffic, made against the same decode step, and a decode step has a finite amount of idle arithmetic to sell.

This is the first measurement of what happens when both are attempted. Quantization gets there first, because it changes the cost of the forward pass itself. Speculation then arrives to find the pass much cheaper in bytes but unchanged in FLOPs, and its verification step — which is a real GEMM over the whole draft tree — is now large relative to what it is being compared against. The paper's decomposition makes this exact: speculative speedup depends on the accepted length τ, the draft-to-target time ratio, and the target verification-to-decoding ratio T_v(n)/T_t, which should sit near 1 because verifying n tokens and decoding one both read the same weights. At draft-tree size 60 that ratio is below 1.2 for FP16 and W8A8, 1.8 for W4A16 on an A100, and roughly 2.3 on an RTX 3090.

Three consequences the vault should carry.

It is not an agreement problem. The obvious hypothesis — a quantized target disagrees with its draft more often, so fewer tokens are accepted — is measured and rejected. Average accepted length under W4A16 and W8A8 tracks FP16 within noise. The distributional property speculation depends on survives quantization; the throughput arithmetic does not.

The tuning advice inverts. For a full-precision model, bigger draft trees and more draft passes buy more speedup, which is why the defaults are what they are. For a 4-bit weight model the curve runs the other way: shrinking EAGLE-2's tree from full to half raises W4A16(8B) from 2.7× to 2.9× while dropping FP16(8B) from 2.3× to 2.1×, and cutting the passes from six to three takes W4A16(8B) to 3.4×. So the practitioner rule is on a 4-bit model, draft shallower and flatter than the defaults — and taken to its limit, drop the tree entirely: on a W4A16 70B, plain sequential speculative decoding beats EAGLE-2 (2.31× vs 1.80×) despite drafting more than eleven times slower, because per-iteration verification costs 29.7 ms against EAGLE-2's 45.8 ms.

The consumer card is where it bites hardest, and the vault's premise lives there. In the paper's whole grid of quantization schemes crossed with hardware, integration beats either technique alone in every cell but one: W4A16 on the 8B model on the RTX 3090. A consumer card has less spare arithmetic per unit of bandwidth than an A100, so the verification term saturates sooner. The generalizable form — and this is a prediction rather than a measurement — is that any pair of techniques both spending compute to buy bandwidth will run out on weaker silicon first, which is the opposite of the usual assumption that inference tricks matter more the smaller your machine is.

Rebutted by: verified-tokens-and-activated-experts-are-different-costs — holds for a resident model, where both levers spend idle arithmetic, and appears to invert when the weights are offloaded: standard speculative decoding loses to plain autoregressive decoding on seven of eight BF16-expert pairs and wins on all four MXFP4-expert pairs, so where the scarce resource is PCIe bytes rather than compute headroom, quantization looks like the precondition for speculation rather than a competitor for it. Confounded with the model in the one source that shows it.
