The drafter you download was fitted to someone else's workload

local-inference holds speculative decoding as the lever that costs nothing but idle arithmetic. This source supplies the failure mode that turns the cost negative, and it is not a subtlety about kernel overhead — it is a domain-transfer failure large enough to invert the sign of the whole technique.

EAGLE-3's official released checkpoint, used as published: about 67.2% acceptance on XSum, the dataset in its original evaluation, and 8.4% once moved to a 60K-token long-context setting. In the head-to-head table it reads 1.95% acceptance and 0.89× speedup on LongBench v2 — slower than not speculating — and 2.78% / 0.92× on ∞-Bench. LayerSkip, a different training-based approach, fails in the same direction from a different starting point: 0.80× even on normal-length inputs, because its 41.4% acceptance yields too few accepted tokens to amortize the verification pass.

Compose that with speculation-length-has-a-closed-form-inversion-point and it is not mysterious. Once α collapses, ∂S/∂k = (α − γ)/(kγ+1)² is negative everywhere and the method spends drafting compute to produce almost nothing; the observed 1.07 average accepted tokens per step means the drafter is essentially always wrong on the first token, so every step pays a full draft and a full verify to emit what one verify would have emitted anyway.

Three things this map should carry from it.

A speculative speedup number is not portable across workloads, and this is the first in-vault measurement of how far it fails to travel. The map's evidence for speculative decoding has been a mechanism guarantee (the output distribution is preserved) plus reported speedups. The guarantee holds unconditionally; the speedup does not survive a change of domain. A practitioner reading "2.2× with EAGLE-3" and applying it to long-context work is reading a number from a distribution they are not in.

And the condition is exactly the one this vault's readers occupy. Trained drafters are downloaded, not trained — training one requires the target model, a corpus and a training run. So the practitioner is structurally in the transfer regime, which is the regime where the checkpoint fails. That is why this grades pushes-back rather than merely extends: the vault's practical recommendation, "use speculative decoding, it's free," needs the rider "if you have a drafter fitted to what you actually run, and you almost certainly do not."

The alternative is a self-drafting method that has no training distribution to be out of. Draft with the target model itself over a sparsified cache and there is no distributional bet: 72.9% acceptance and 2.79× in the same cell where EAGLE-3 reads 1.95% and 0.89× (a-recallable-sparse-cache-is-a-free-drafter). This map already holds the MoE version of the same lesson from 2026-08-02 — where the training-free self-draft was slower than not speculating and a small self-distilled draft expert fixed it. Put side by side, the two results say the choice between a trained and a train-free drafter turns on whether the target is dense or sparse, and on whether the drafter's training data resembles the deployment. Neither source tests the other's configuration.

Two honest limits. This is a transfer result, not a refutation of trained drafters: nobody re-trained EAGLE-3 on long-context data, which is the obvious control and would cost far less than the model it drafts for. And the verifier here is a deliberately unfused dense-attention kernel (needed to materialize the per-head attention statistics the winning method reuses), so C_v is inflated for every method in the table — which flatters absolute speedups on both sides while leaving the acceptance-rate collapse, the actual finding, untouched.
