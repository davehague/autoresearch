Teacher preference diverges from verified correctness as context grows

distillation-small-models' transfer-mechanism strand argues about what actually moves from teacher to student — on-policy versus off-policy dynamics, novelty over teacher rank, pass@K collapse. This adds a failure axis none of them cover, and the diagnostic is worth more than the method built on it.

On-policy distillation scores each token the student generates by the teacher-minus-student log-probability gap. That is a measure of teacher preference, used as a stand-in for task success. The two are usually close enough. This paper measures how close, as a function of input length, on frozen response sets before any training: across longer input ranges, trajectory-level distillation scores become progressively less aligned with verifier rewards.

The mechanism argued is specific to long context and plausible: a correct long-context answer must integrate evidence scattered across distant positions and satisfy global constraints, while dense token-level teacher support "can favor locally plausible responses that omit evidence distributed across the input." The teacher likes fluent, locally-correct prose. The verifier asks whether the answer aggregated the right things. Those come apart exactly where the task gets hard.

A follow-up sweep over four task families with at least 500 prompts each, plus an aggregate over all nine, finds the disagreement is real beyond the original two tasks but task-dependent in prevalence — reported as prevalence, not as a performance claim, which is the right way to report it.

The scope condition this puts on the rest of the map is the finding. Every on-policy-distillation result in this vault — on-policy-distillation-buys-sampling-efficiency-not-capability, on-policy-distillation-runs-on-a-shared-high-probability-token-set, on-policy-distillation-degenerates-without-a-leash-to-the-initial-policy — was established at ordinary context lengths. This says the signal those results depend on has a length-dependent validity range, and short-context evidence should not be extrapolated to long-context students without checking.

The ablation is the second finding, and it is cleaner than the headline. Holding the coefficient and token-allocation rule fixed and varying only what gets added to vanilla on-policy distillation: an extra distillation-derived term is worth +0.04 (nothing), the direct group-normalized verifier reward +0.63, and the signed teacher–verifier residual +1.10. Adding the verifier's opinion helps; adding the verifier's disagreement with the teacher helps almost twice as much. Separately, how the residual is spread matters: absolute-advantage +0.38, uniform +0.72, relative-advantage +1.10 — discarding the sign of the advantage assigns large credit to strongly negative tokens and costs most of the gain.

The margins demand this map's usual discount and mostly survive it. Raw → on-policy distillation is +10.23 / +8.44; the calibration on top is +1.16 / +1.09, and against the best competing method +0.97 / +0.64 — sub-point, single seed, no intervals, one model family with a same-family teacher, 100 steps. The ablation is the part that is argued under identical conditions and is what should be carried.

And it is bought with a loss: GC-OPD wins the five-task mean at 8B while scoring 58.30 on LongBench-v1 QA against 60.40 and 60.20 for two methods it beats on average. The paper discloses this. The recipe costs eight 80 GB datacenter GPUs, which puts it outside this vault's premise entirely as a procedure — the diagnostic is what travels.
