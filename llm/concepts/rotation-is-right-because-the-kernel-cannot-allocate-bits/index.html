The reason rotation works is that the kernel threw away bit allocation

This map holds a shelf of rotation results — rotating-a-transformer-deletes-its-outliers-for-free, rotation-recovers-the-code-loss-of-four-bit-weights, a-rotations-value-is-its-mixing-support-not-its-structure, an-exactly-optimal-rotation-can-be-worse-than-no-rotation — and not one of them can say why rotation is the right kind of operation rather than a trick that kept replicating. This supplies the principle, and it is more interesting than "spread the outliers."

Classical transform coding, since 1963, says: decorrelate, allocate bits, quantize. Its optimum concentrates energy, because with a free bit budget you spend more bits where the variance is and the objective is a geometric mean. A deployed 4-bit matrix instruction does something else entirely — it carries one absolute-maximum scale per group and equal bits everywhere, with no allocation at all. Against that objective the cost is set by the group peak, and the optimum is to flatten. The two prescriptions are not merely different heuristics; the paper proves them strictly opposed under within-group majorization, so each is optimal against its own surrogate and neither is evidence for the other.

The consequence worth carrying on this map: rotation is a workaround for a missing feature. The reason a Hadamard helps is that the kernel cannot spend different bits on different coordinates. Restore per-coordinate allocation and the correct move inverts. That reframes half this map's allocation strand — every note about where the bits go is, in this light, an attempt to recover by construction what fixed-rate hardware deleted from the format.

It also explains a pattern the map had recorded as an oddity. a-rotations-value-is-its-mixing-support-not-its-structure found that what a rotation buys is mixing support rather than any particular algebraic structure; that is exactly what a flattening objective predicts, and not what a decorrelation objective predicts. And an-exactly-optimal-rotation-can-be-worse-than-no-rotation — a result that reads as paradoxical under the decorrelation story — is unsurprising once the target is the group max rather than the spectrum: optimizing the wrong functional can move you away from the right one.

One structural claim in the taxonomy is worth stating on its own: no rotation can supply whitening beyond what the covariance sees. That bounds the entire learned-orthogonal family (SpinQuant, optimized QuaRot) against its data-free counterparts in a way no empirical comparison on this map has.

Limits, and they are real. The inversion is proved against surrogate objectives — the geometric mean and the group max — not against the extreme-value cost a deployed model actually pays, and the author says plainly that it "is not on the published record": there is no end-to-end model experiment demonstrating it, only single-layer illustration. The Bennett high-resolution model underpinning the derivation is exact as Δ→0 and is described by its own author as "merely good at four bits," which is precisely the width everything here targets. The cross-product ΔX ΔWᵀ term is dropped and its magnitude at aggressive W4A4 is unquantified. Single author, no code, literature frozen at June 2026. This is a strong theoretical organizing claim with a thin empirical floor, and the map should hold it that way.
