Low precision stops paying when the accelerator outruns its bandwidth

transformer-architecture's recurring lesson is that systems progress expires with the accelerator generation it was tuned for. This is the same lesson aimed at quantization, and it runs the other way round: the hardware improved, and the software technique's advantage evaporated.

The argument is a roofline balance point, BP = P_compute / B — the arithmetic intensity at which a kernel stops being memory-bound and starts being compute-bound.

PlatformINT8 throughputMemory bandwidthINT8 balance point
Ice Lake (3rd Gen Xeon)27.03 TOPS175 GB/s154.5 OPs/byte
Granite Rapids (6th Gen Xeon)524.29 TOPS700 GB/s749.0 OPs/byte

BERT-large's GEMM shapes have INT8 arithmetic intensity of 227.6, 248.2 and 341.3. So the same three matrix multiplies are compute-bound on the older chip and memory-bound on the newer one. AMX raised integer throughput 19× while bandwidth rose 4×, and the workload fell off the compute roof.

What that costs, concretely. Against FP32 the newer chip still looks great — 5.82× — but FP32 is not the alternative anyone deploys; the same chip has native BF16. Against BF16 the analytical ceiling is 1.53×, and measured: BERT-large 1.59×, DistilBERT 0.98×, XLM-RoBERTa 0.99×. Two of six configurations are a net loss, because finding activation scales at runtime under dynamic quantization costs more than the shrunken GEMM saves.

The refinement the paper adds is worth keeping too, because it is the mechanism by which "memory-bound" gets partly rescued: the INT8 model is 442 MB against a 504 MB last-level cache, so weights can sit resident at 87.7% LLC occupancy while activations stream from main memory. Effective bandwidth rises to 807–875 GB/s and the balance point falls to 599–649 — still above the workload's intensity. INT8 remains memory-bound; it is just less badly so. Once the weights fit in cache, the bottleneck moves to the activations, and the byte count stops predicting the runtime — the server-CPU statement of what the-kv-cache-costs-more-than-its-bytes-at-batch-one found on a consumer CPU.

The generalization this vault should carry: a quantization speedup number is dated by hardware, not by method. A 4× INT8 result from an older generation does not transfer forward, and the direction of the error is that newer silicon makes it smaller. The footprint benefit — fewer bytes to store, fewer to move, more that fits in cache — is durable. The compute benefit is a claim about a specific balance point and expires.

Two scope lines that must not be blurred. These are BERT-family encoders at batch 1, sequence 256 — a prefill-shaped, compute-bound workload. Decoder LLM decode is memory-bound at batch one for entirely different reasons and this vault has separate measurements there (quantization-speeds-decode-and-can-slow-prefill); the roofline method transfers and the ratios do not. And these are Intel authors on Intel hardware, with the caveat that the finding is unflattering to the product — the newest chip is the one where their technique matters least — which is the main reason to trust it.
