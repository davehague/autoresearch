Training on the answer format is most of a benchmark win

evaluation-contamination catalogues the ways a benchmark number stops meaning what it says. Most of them involve the test items reaching the training data. This is the one that does not, and it arrived the same night as the source that gave it a name — the "inflation, not memory" cell of a-detector-needs-a-no-verdict-branch's four-outcome table, where a score is wrong through format overfitting with no leakage anywhere.

The instance is unusually clean because the model's own report documents the mechanism. Mimir v1 rebalanced its 70.48B-token corpus "away from multiple-choice toward free-form generation… aligning with evaluation suites that prioritise exact-match scoring (GSM8k, MATH, DROP) over multiple-choice accuracy (ARC-C, MMLU, Hellaswag)," and 56.1% of its tokens are in categories scored by exact match or free-form generation. It then posts an English suite average of 69.0 against Qwen 3.5 4B's 69.3 — parity with a model four times its size — assembled from DROP F1 83.1 against 48.0 and GSM8K 89.9 against 60.5, while losing MMLU by 18.3 and ARC-C by 11.3.

Neither of those wins is readable as capability. A 4B instruction model scoring 60.5 on GSM8K and 48.0 F1 on DROP is not a measurement of what it can do; it is a measurement of whether its output shape survived the harness's scorer. a-fifteen-point-baseline-gap-is-a-claim-about-the-baseline is the standing rule and it applies at full strength — but the addition here is that the effect does not require a mis-run baseline at all. Exact-match scoring rewards emitting the answer bare; a model trained on multiple-choice emits a letter, a hedge, or a sentence around the number. Both models may be doing exactly what they were trained to do, and the benchmark still reports a 35-point gap.

The consequence for reading any small-model result is a subtraction, not a discount. An aggregate average is a weighted vote over scoring formats, so a corpus tuned to the format distribution of the average is buying points that do not transfer to a different harness, a different prompt template, or a user. averaging-a-suite-hides-its-weakest-dimension is the mechanism by which this stays invisible: 69.0 built from 83.1 and 57.5 is not the same object as 69.3 built from 48.0 and 75.8, and the average is the only number that gets quoted. And it compounds with alignment-is-format-selection-not-knowledge-acquisition from the post-training side, which established that alignment selects among behaviours the model already has — here the selection is being made against the evaluator rather than against a user.

The practical screen is cheap and this vault should run it on every small-model claim: look at the scoring format of the benchmarks a model wins on versus the ones it loses. If the wins cluster on exact-match and the losses cluster on multiple-choice (or the reverse), the average is reporting format alignment. The comparison that survives the screen here is the same-architecture one — Mimir against HRM-Text 1B, identical architecture and scale, different corpus, +36.7% on math and code — because both sides share the format exposure.

Graded T2 / suggestive: this is a self-reported evaluation in a technical report with released weights, not an independent study of format effects, and the format explanation is inference from the report's own stated design decision plus implausible baseline numbers rather than a controlled ablation. Nobody re-ran the baselines under a generation-friendly harness, which is the experiment that would settle it. The Danish results are a separate matter and largely survive — Mimir is the only model in that table trained on Danish at volume, which is a real capability difference.
