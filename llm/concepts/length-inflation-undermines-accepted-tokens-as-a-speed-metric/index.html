Block efficiency was always blind to quality by construction; this map's standing acceptance-rate metric is also blind to its own cost — the completions get longer, and nobody who reads accepted-tokens-per-round would know

This map's exactness equation has always separated two costs: how much a verification rule accepts, and what a round costs. Every lossy-verification entry here so far (lossy-verification-silently-replaces-the-target-distribution) measured the second cost in task accuracy — a benchmark score that moves (or doesn't) once a looser rule starts admitting tokens the target would have rejected. This paper measures a third, previously unmetered cost: completion length itself. A lossless rule can't move it — the sampled distribution is provably unchanged, so length is pinned to the lossless baseline by construction. A lossy rule has given that guarantee up, and across 360 cells the length grows (1.54x) faster than the acceptance count that is supposed to justify the method (1.34x). The paper's own framing is exact: "accepted tokens per round alone is an unreliable measure of decoding speed under relaxed verification."

Mechanism, not coincidence. The extra tokens are not noise spread evenly across outputs — they are concentrated in reasoning traces, and they are disproportionately tokens the target model itself rates as unlikely (the tightest-relaxing rule's extra tokens are still the target's first-or-second choice; the loosest rules' are not). On grade-school math the extra length trails a correct answer the model already wrote — pure waste. On harder competition math it delays the answer, sometimes past the generation budget, directly costing correctness. This gives the map's "exactness dial" open question (previously answered only by one paper's 1.52-point worst-case accuracy cost under a different protocol — natural-EOS completion, not a forced budget) a second, independent failure mode: even where a relaxed rule's per-token quality cost looks small, its length cost compounds separately and is not visible in any metric this map's other lossy-verification sources report.

What closes and what doesn't. The paper's own mitigation — forcing a relaxed run's reasoning trace to end near the token budget rather than running it out mid-thought — recovers most of the accuracy loss (126 → 439 correct answers across 1,132 affected runs) but explicitly does not recover the speed advantage, since the run still spends most of its token budget either way. That is a real, if narrow, answer to part of the map's standing question about whether the exactness dial survives an evidence check: for reasoning-heavy workloads specifically, the honest framing is that lossy verification's throughput number and its accuracy number cannot both be taken from the same unconstrained run. The map's other named check — the fixed-trace replay protocol from kv-cache-management's 2026-08-06 work, which would test whether surviving lossy-verified chains are still valid derivations rather than merely correct-looking endpoints — remains unrun; this paper measures length and accuracy, not chain validity, so it narrows the open question without closing it.

Caveats carried forward. All five tested rules and all four targets are cluster-measured, 8B-20B scale — nothing here on consumer hardware, which is this map's standing complaint. The accuracy cost is explicitly task-dependent (large on math-reasoning, smaller on chat), so "accuracy typically drops substantially" is not a fixed universal number, and the forcing mitigation needs the generation budget tuned close to the trace's natural end — information a real serving system does not have in advance.
