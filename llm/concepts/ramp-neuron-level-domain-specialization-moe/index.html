MoE domain specialization lives inside experts, at the neuron level, not between them — a routing-aware neuron importance measure (RAMP) finds it, and pruning on that measure (RAMPr) beats footprint-only pruning by also reporting a throughput gain

TL;DR: Zhou et al. propose Routing-Aware Magnitude Profiling (RAMP) to measure domain specialization inside MoE language models at the neuron level rather than the expert or token-routing level most prior work uses. They find neurons specialized for a given domain overlap heavily with each other (58-74%) but little with neurons specialized for other domains (15-24%), and these specialized neurons are scattered across many experts rather than clustered in a small subset — meaning expert-level statistics (which expert handles which domain) cannot see this structure. Importance rankings shift substantially between pretraining checkpoints even while expert-level rankings stay stable, but post-training mostly preserves whatever neuron-level ranking pretraining settled on. Built on RAMP, their pruning method RAMPr selects neurons across expert boundaries rather than removing whole experts, preserving multi-domain capability: 91-98% of original performance at 75% retention (six models), and at 50% retention a 36-43% cut in resident GPU memory alongside a 17-39% batched prefill throughput gain — reporting both a footprint and a speed number together, which mixture-of-experts's and structured-pruning's existing quantization-footprint literature frequently does not.

What it says. This reframes rather than contradicts the map's coining-era "routers learn syntax, not topics" finding and its own 2026-11-10 SAE-based pushback (which decomposes a layer's output exactly over experts and finds specialists below the domain label's grain): all three instruments operate at or above the expert boundary, while RAMP looks inside the expert and finds the domain structure none of them were positioned to see. It gives the map's standing "nobody has isolated the specialization claim" open question — which calls for a DeepSeekMoE-style lesion test on domain-peaked experts — a result one level more granular than what that question asked for: instead of lesioning a domain-peaked expert, RAMP's overlap statistic already shows which neurons carry the domain signal, scattered across many experts, which means the lesion test as originally framed (remove an expert, measure the domain loss) would systematically undercount the damage since no single expert holds a domain's full specialized-neuron set. For structured-pruning, RAMPr is a pruning unit below this map's existing menu of width/depth-structural and expert-level (REAP/REAM) granularities — a neuron-level, cross-expert cut not previously represented on either map.

Caveats. Six-author team, no stated institutional affiliation found on this read; code is released (https://github.com/ydyjya/Neuron-Level-Specialization-in-MoE). The abstract/landing-page read did not surface per-task benchmark names or deltas behind the aggregate "91-98% of original performance" figure, nor model names/sizes for the six models tested. No head-to-head against REAP or REAM (this map's existing expert-level pruning baselines) at matched removal ratios on the same models — RAMPr's standing against the map's incumbent pruning methods is unmeasured.
