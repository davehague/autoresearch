A game-theoretic, fixed LoRA-expert router rivals learned gates on routing quality and makes it possible to trace a harmful behavior to one expert and retrain only that expert

TL;DR: InflEns allocates training data to LoRA experts over a frozen dense backbone using a location game in a fixed prompt-trait space: each expert chooses a position to maximize its own expected share of the prompt distribution, with no reference to expert losses, and a kernel-general theoretical criterion determines when co-located positions become locally unstable and separate into distinct specializations. At inference, the same game — not a trained gate — predicts which expert should answer a given prompt: the highest-predicted-share expert has the lowest held-out negative log-likelihood on roughly 90% of prompts across four kernels under a sharpened simplex representation, against roughly 15% once the training allocation is permuted across prompts as a control, and performs comparably to published learned gates without training one. Because this router is a fixed, inspectable function rather than a black box, the authors trace a harmful behavior to the single responsible expert and retrain only that expert, cutting HarmBench attack success rate from 0.51 to 0.02 while preserving instruction-following accuracy. The game also exposes explicit knobs over how experts specialize — kernel choice and parameters, trait-space geometry, number of experts — that the authors argue generalize to any encoder or data distribution.

Why it matters here. This sits in peft-methods's existing mixture-of-LoRA-experts cluster (VANE's reader/writer split, HydraLoRA, MixLoRA, and the 200-language/9,000-setting EACL sweep), all of which argue about the forgetting-vs-adaptation trade-off or about routing accuracy. None of them connects routing design to interpretability or safety — InflEns is the first to show that making the router fixed and game-derived, rather than learned, buys a concrete safety payoff (single-expert traceability and retraining) at no apparent cost to routing quality. Cross-linked to mixture-of-experts: this remains the adapter-side half of that map's 2026-08-05 open question (routing lives in the LoRA layer over a dense base, same category as the EACL sweep), so it does not resolve the still-open base-side half — a routed expert layer inside a genuinely sparse base model — but it adds a new reason that half is worth answering: whether a fixed, game-derived router could offer the same safety-traceability property there too.

Caveats. No institutional affiliation or code/weight release stated; the 90%-vs-15% comparison and the HarmBench result are the authors' own evaluation on their own constructed seven-benchmark safety corpus, with no independent replication. "Comparable to published learned gates" is not quantified in the abstract-level account used here. No test of InflEns's own forgetting-vs-adaptation behavior against the EACL sweep's eight MoE-LoRA baselines, and the generality claim (any encoder, any data distribution) is asserted rather than tested outside this paper's setup.
