The third answer to "the right setting is a search nobody runs"

Three unrelated literatures in this vault reach the same structural conclusion and stop there. FSDP's sharding factor is sharding-is-a-dial-between-memory-and-communication with measurably wrong settings in both directions. Gradient checkpointing is activation-memory-is-a-tunable-continuum-not-a-fixed-cost with an interior optimum. PipeDream's stage partitioning is the-parallelism-configuration-is-searchable-not-a-dial. In each case the paper demonstrates that the right value is found by a search over a budget, and in each case no shipped tool performs that search, so practitioners take a default or borrow someone else's number.

This source is the first to price both halves of that gap on the same axis.

Skipping the search costs 0.97 points. Deriving the expert-set size from the effective rank of the router's commitment-weighted demand — the exponential of its entropy, so concentrated demand yields a small set and diffuse demand a larger one — lands on average 0.97 percentage points below the best measured fixed budget across five swept pairs, and 1.83 below in the worst case. That is the price of not sweeping, and it is small.

Borrowing someone else's number costs 9.1 points. B = 25 is the best measured budget for GPT-OSS-120B on HumanEval. Applied to Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B on the same task, it lands 9.1 points below that model's own best fixed-budget result. Same task, same family of method, different model, and the transferred optimum is nearly ten times worse than having no optimum at all.

The general form is worth stating because it applies well beyond expert selection: a swept hyperparameter is a property of the (model, workload, hardware) triple, and a closed-form estimate computed from runtime statistics beats a stale sweep by a wide margin even though it loses to a fresh one. Every default in this vault's tooling — r=16, alpha=32, a fixed checkpointing boolean, a fixed KV budget — is a borrowed number of exactly this kind, and this is the first measurement of what borrowing costs.

Two qualifications. The comparison is against the best measured point of a discrete sweep rather than a true optimum, so the 0.97 is generous to the sweep at fine granularity and harsh at coarse. And the estimator here is unusually well-posed: expert demand is a probability distribution the router already computes, so its entropy is free. Whether the same trick exists for a sharding factor or a checkpointing granularity — where the quantity to be estimated is a memory-versus-communication trade rather than a distribution's spread — is open, and it is the more valuable question.
