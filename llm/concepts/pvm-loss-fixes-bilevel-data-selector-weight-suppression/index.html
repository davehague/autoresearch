A proven mechanism for why bilevel data-selector training fails, and a loss that fixes it — the map's first theoretical account of selector-training pathology rather than selection-outcome pathology

This map has repeatedly measured what a selected dataset does to downstream performance — selection dominates scale, at pretraining, SFT, and RL-prompt-curation stages. TESS asks a different question: why does the selector itself, when it's a learned meta-network rather than a hand-written filter, sometimes fail to learn a useful ranking at all? It proves (Theorem 1) that the standard bilevel loss (ScaleBiO) makes the scorer's softmax weights collapse onto a single example at rate O(t^{-1/3}) — weight suppression — and (Theorem 2) that its gradient gap stays non-decreasing rather than shrinking, meaning the loss never stops chasing easy, shortcut-correlated signal. A direct regression loss against a measured per-example loss-difference target (Pointwise Value Matching) avoids both: its gradient gap decays exponentially instead.

The validation task is worth reading carefully — one half (LLM safety) measures whether the scorer can find the most harmful fine-tuning examples (ASR, attack success rate, where higher means better discrimination of harmful data), a stress test of ranking power rather than a recommendation to select harmful data for training. The other half (targeted instruction tuning) is this map's familiar territory: select data to boost a specific downstream skill, where TESS beats LESS and RDS+ on GSM8K-targeted selection.

The practical result worth carrying forward: a small (0.5B) scorer's selections transfer to a 7B target model at 77-84% of the benefit a same-size scorer would get, for a 5.65-6.60x selection-time speedup — meta-network data selection does not require scoring with the model you're actually training, at least across this one capacity gap.
