A single phrasing is not a measurement

a-benchmark-score-is-one-draw-from-an-unreported-distribution established the first half of this on GSM-Symbolic: template a benchmark's questions, regenerate them, and a model spans 12–15 points across draws of identical reasoning problems. That note closed on a limit it could not clear — it cannot separate leakage from brittleness, because perturbing names and numbers measures both superimposed, and the same models that lose points to a changed number also lose them to an inert added clause (an-irrelevant-clause-collapses-arithmetic-accuracy).

This clears it, by construction. The answer is held fixed and validated to be fixed — variations whose ground truth moved are discarded — so nothing memorization-shaped is being probed. What is left is pure brittleness, and it is larger than the templating result by a factor of four: Phi-4 reads 93.4 on GSM8K as published, 38.2 if every validated rephrasing of a problem must be answered correctly, and 99.2 if any one may be.

Two things here are new to this map.

The sign flip is the finding. The intuitive story is that phrasing sensitivity is a weakness that capability erodes. It does not erode; it reverses. On GSM8K every model is net-negative and the strongest are the most negative (GPT-OSS-20B −52.6, Phi-4 −49.4) while the two weakest sit at −2.8. On MATH-Hard, where the weak models have room, they go net-positive — Mistral-7B +28.6, with 37.6% of its problems flipping right against 9.0% flipping wrong. A weak model has little to lose and knowledge the original wording failed to elicit; a strong model has much to lose. The consequence for reading any leaderboard is uncomfortable and direct: a top-of-table score is the score most inflated by its particular phrasing, so the gap between first and second place is partly a measurement of which one got the friendlier wording. That compounds with training-on-the-answer-format-is-most-of-a-benchmark-win, where a corpus tuned to a suite's scoring format bought 35 points with no contamination anywhere — two independent routes to a score that is real, uncontaminated, and not about capability.

Positive drift is a category this map did not have. Every instrument here — templating, parallel benchmark construction, membership detection, decontamination — is built to detect inflation. This one finds that rephrasing also recovers correct answers a model could produce all along, at 20–38% rates on the weak end. A reported score can therefore understate a model as well as overstate it, and the two failure modes are not symmetric across the field: they are indexed by where the model sits on the benchmark. Nothing in a-detector-needs-a-no-verdict-branch's four-outcome table anticipated a score being wrong downward.

Two supports for reading this as a property of evaluation rather than of the pipeline that measured it. Swapping which model generates variations, validates them and judges answers moves average negative drift by at most 11.9pp and preserves the fragility ranking. And drift is not "the problems got easier": negative drift is lowest when length is preserved (19.6%) and rises when problems get shorter (28.9%) as well as longer (34.7%).

Bounds worth stating plainly. All three benchmarks are closed-form, and the authors say they do not know whether this holds for open-ended tasks. The largest model is 34B, so "strong models lose more" is established across a 48-to-95 band and not at the frontier. Temperature 0 throughout, which removes sampling noise and also removes any interaction with decoding temperature — relevant here because contamination-dose-is-diluted-by-fresh-tokens-and-exposed-by-temperature found temperature to be the axis that exposes the other failure mode.
