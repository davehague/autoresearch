This map's layer-wise and token-wise levers have always been scored as rivals; a joint allocation treats them as a budget split instead, and the split beats either axis alone

Every prior entry on this map's "spend a fixed budget" strand has picked one axis — evict or preserve by token (anchor-plus-residual, the eviction family), or allocate bits by layer (per-layer sensitivity ranking) — and compared axes against each other rather than asking whether a single budget should be split between them. MultiAlloc is the map's first source to profile both signals (layer sensitivity, token importance) and derive one static allocation policy spanning both, rather than committing to one and measuring against the other. The ablation is the informative result here: layer-only and token-only variants of the same method score 25.58 and 24.88 on LongBench-v2, and the joint allocation scores 25.85 — a real but modest edge, meaning most of the achievable gain already comes from either axis done well, and the remaining headroom from splitting the budget is smaller than the headline multi-baseline comparison suggests.

Where this lands relative to the map's open questions. This is adjacent to, but not the same as, the standing "does the factor decomposition predict which compositions interfere?" question — that question is about whether independently-designed levers (eviction, low-rank projection, per-channel quantization) stack additively because they act on different distortion factors. MultiAlloc is a single method that was designed to span two axes jointly from the start, so it doesn't test whether two off-the-shelf levers from different papers compose; it tests whether one paper's own two-axis design beats either of its own single-axis ablations, and the answer is a modest yes. The throughput number (1.25-1.26x KVmix, A100-only) and all quality numbers are reported on one 80GB datacenter card — the consumer-crossover gap this map has flagged since 2026-08 remains open here as everywhere else on this map's quantization strand.

What it does and does not establish. It establishes that a profiled joint allocation beats three published baselines at matched memory and beats its own single-axis ablations. It does not establish how it composes with eviction, cross-model transfer, or depth-partitioned caching — the other three levers this map already tracks — nor does it report perplexity, a consumer-hardware number, or calibration-data sensitivity beyond acknowledging that calibration is task-specific and must be redone per benchmark.
