Why a swept rank never transfers, stated as a theorem instead of an observation

This map has accumulated a lot of evidence that rank is the wrong thing to pick by rule of thumb and no account of what rank actually measures. adapter-rank-is-a-dial-between-what-you-learn-and-what-you-keep found acquisition saturating at 16 and retention collapsing past it, on one task. low-rank-capacity-binds-when-the-domain-is-new found low rank a real capacity ceiling when the domain is far from pretraining. Both are empirical, both are task-specific, and neither says why the answer should be task-specific rather than a property of the method. This source gives the reason, and it is a clean one.

The rank you need is a property of ΔW crossed with the task's input distribution, not of ΔW. Formally: the best achievable rank-r attention KL is bracketed by an explicit function of T_r, the downstream-weighted tail energy — the residual spectrum of the target update after it has been reweighted by the queries and keys the downstream task actually produces (D* = G^{1/2} Δ* Σ^{1/2}, with G the expected centered key Gram and Σ the query second moment). A large singular direction that the task's inputs never activate contributes nothing to T_r. A small one that repeatedly changes which tokens get attended to contributes a lot.

That single fact explains three things this map has recorded separately as puzzles. A rank swept on one task does not transfer to another because the weighting matrix changed, not because the optimizer got lucky. Spectral-norm or singular-value heuristics for choosing rank fail because they omit the weighting entirely. And the saturation in adapter-rank-is-a-dial-between-what-you-learn-and-what-you-keep — acquisition flat from rank 16 — is what a tail energy that has already gone to near zero looks like: past the rank that covers the task-activated directions, additional rank has nothing left to represent, and (per that source's retention curve) it spends the capacity on damage instead.

The second theorem is worth keeping on its own. Attention KL is quadratic in small score errors and only linear in large ones (ψ(t) = min{t², t}), and a purely quadratic global lower bound provably cannot hold. So the damage from an under-ranked adapter does not blow up without limit — it flattens. That is an argument for the low-rank regime being safe in a way the empirical results here keep finding and never explaining.

The extensions map onto real configurations. Fused multi-head LoRA — one shared projection across heads, which is what most implementations do — is covered with a shared rank budget, so the cost of rank sharing is separable from the cost of rank. Joint query/key adaptation uses rank(C) ≤ r_Q + r_K and isolates the extra error from being forced to factor the best effective score update into two separately-budgeted low-rank factors — the first statement in this vault of what target_modules costs beyond what it buys.

And a concrete architectural caveat that reaches outside this map. For joint Q/K LoRA, RoPE couples the factors across relative positions, so the single-matrix spectral specialization does not apply directly in a rotary model — which is every model this vault's readers fine-tune. The author notes the obstruction is absent in NoPE architectures. position-can-be-a-rotation-not-an-addition recorded RoPE as the cheap win it is; this is the first entry noting a place where the rotation makes the analysis harder.

Now the discounts, which are large enough that this note is graded suggestive despite being a proof.

There are no experiments of any kind — no trained model, no calibration set, no numerical check on a real attention head. The author states it directly: "We have not established that any of these constants are numerically tight on trained, real-model attention heads." The bounds are on attention KL of one head, explicitly not on head output or task loss, and converting between them needs assumptions about values, output projections, residuals, the FFN and later layers that the paper declines to make. They are population statements with no finite-sample guarantee: the probability floor, geometry constant and moment constant enter as theorem assumptions, not as things a calibration set certifies, so a practical lower curve must report sensitivity to assumed values rather than a number. They describe representation, not optimization — the best candidate in a rank-constrained class, not what SGD finds, which is precisely the confound the empirical rank sweeps on this map cannot separate either. And the procedure needs a trained high-rank target first, so it can tell you whether a smaller adapter would have sufficed and cannot tell you what rank to start at.

What it is good for, then, is not choosing rank up front. It is compressing an adapter you already have with a stated bound, and — more valuable for this map — supplying the reason the empirical answers keep refusing to generalize.
