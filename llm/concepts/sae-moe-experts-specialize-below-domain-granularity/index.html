MoE experts divide labor at a grain far finer than domain labels -- and the field's standard instrument (corpus-level selection-frequency counting) could never have detected it, because it measures which domain-labeled corpus selects an expert, not what feature the expert's output actually contributes

TL;DR: This map's coining-era finding -- "routers learn syntax, not topics," two labs independently finding no extractable "math expert" -- and its 2026-10-30 routing-confidence/utility-alignment entries all measure specialization the same way: count how often a domain-labeled corpus selects each expert. SAE Interprets MoE (ByteDance Seed/SUFE) replaces that instrument with an exact decomposition in feature space. Because an MoE layer's output is a router-weighted sum of expert outputs and a trained sparse autoencoder's encoder is affine, every SAE latent's pre-activation decomposes exactly over the experts that produced it -- no approximation, no corpus label needed. On three layers of Qwen3-30B-A3B, this reveals specialists the selection-frequency method's coarse taxonomy could never resolve: a Java expert distinct from a front-end-web-code expert, a geometry expert distinct from an arithmetic/polynomials expert, with coarse domains (code, math) receiving very unequal numbers of experts underneath.

What it says. The decomposition: latent_pre-activation(t) = sum_j g_j(t) * (encoder applied to expert_j's output at token t), exact because the SAE encoder is affine and the MoE output is router-weighted-sum. Aggregated over a corpus, each expert gets a contribution profile across every latent; thresholding builds a dominance graph. On layers 4, 30, and 46 of Qwen3-30B-A3B: contribution concentrates sharply (most latents dominated by one or two experts, far more concentrated than gating/selection frequency implies); dominant-set overlap between expert pairs is mostly near-zero and concentrates on a handful of pairs, with roughly half of each expert's dominant set uniquely its own; and auto-interpreting dominant-latent sets by activating context yields specialization below any domain label used in prior gating-frequency work (e.g. E57 = front-end web code, E50 = Java/Android, E1 = geometry, E76 = arithmetic/polynomials). Rolling these fine-grained sets up into coarse domains shows a strongly unequal allocation of experts per domain -- a distribution the coarse view never exposed.

Caveats. pushes-back targets the instrument this map's prior specialization claims were measured with (corpus-level selection-frequency counting), not a specific accuracy number -- experts-specialize-by-syntax-not-semantics remains correct on what it measured (routing selection is near-uniform across analyst-chosen domain labels at the resolution those labels offer); this source shows a finer-grained, feature-space account was available underneath, at a grain below where either study's domain labels could see. One model (Qwen3-30B-A3B), three layers, no replication on a second MoE architecture or router design, so whether the result generalizes beyond this model's training is untested. No controlled ablation shows routing causes this specialization via content rather than surface/token-identity features (the map's open content-vs-surface-feature question) -- the paper reframes the measurement, it does not settle that dispute. Auto-interpreted latent labels ("Java expert") carry the usual no-held-out-human-validation caveat. No downstream task-accuracy consequence of this finer specialization (for routing, pruning, or merging) is measured. Code and trained SAEs released (github.com/Bytedance-Seed/SAE-Interprets-MoE).
