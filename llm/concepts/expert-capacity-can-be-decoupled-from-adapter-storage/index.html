An expert becomes a vector, and the adapter becomes a function

The mixture-of-LoRA-experts family buys capacity the obvious way: instantiate N complete adapters and route among them. That makes adapter storage O(N · r · d) — linear in the expert count — and freezes the set of available adaptations at training time, since a new input can only ever be served by a combination of adapters that already exist.

MoEGen's move is to stop storing the experts. Each expert becomes a conditioning vector E_i of dimension d_h. A shared hypernetwork generates the LoRA A matrix row by row, taking the routed combination of expert codes plus a learnable coordinate embedding p_j^(l) identifying which row of which layer it is producing. One generator serves every expert and every layer; what varies per expert is a small vector, and what varies per instance is the routing over them. Capacity and storage come apart, and the adaptation becomes instance-conditioned rather than task-conditioned — the weights are produced for this input, not selected for this task.

The results are consistent and small in the place that matters. Against the strongest MoE-PEFT baseline the margin is +0.6 / +1.2 / +1.9 across three backbones; against vanilla LoRA it is +6.6 / +7.1 / +2.5, shrinking sharply on the strongest base (Qwen3-8B). That shrinking is the tell: the gap closes as the base gets better, which is what an artifact of a weak baseline looks like and also what a genuine ceiling looks like.

Graded single-study, and the reason is a result this vault already holds. adapter-variant-gains-are-a-learning-rate-artifact established that nine LoRA variants tuned per method land within 1–2% of each other, while their optimal learning rates differ by up to 18.2×, because these methods raise the largest Hessian eigenvalue and a default step size overshoots. MoEGen reports no learning-rate sweep. Its vanilla-LoRA margin sits squarely in the band that paper attributes to an untuned baseline, and its MoE-PEFT margin is inside the ±0.79 standard deviation that paper measured. The architecture may well work; this experiment cannot separate it from the confound.

Two further reasons to hold the number loosely. The instrument is an eight-benchmark commonsense average, and a-commonsense-benchmark-predicts-a-narrow-slice-of-commonsense — ingested the same night, by authors with no knowledge of this paper — finds that suite has cross-family predictive validity for only a narrow subset of downstream tasks. And the paper's own ablation undercuts its framing: the frozen sentence encoder that supplies the semantic routing signal is worth 0.7 points, and widening routing from top-1 to top-2 is worth 0.33. The routing the architecture is organized around is nearly free to remove, which suggests the win is in the generation, not the mixture.

Rebutted by: a-commonsense-benchmark-predicts-a-narrow-slice-of-commonsense — the eight-benchmark commonsense average this note's entire effect size is measured in has cross-family predictive validity for only a narrow subset of downstream tasks, and which tasks it predicts does not follow the domains the benchmark labels imply. The margins here (+0.6 to +1.9 over the strongest baseline) are readings of an instrument now known to be partial, so they license a claim about that average and not about capability.

What survives regardless is the structural point, and it is genuinely new here: an adapter can be a function of the input rather than a stored object. That is a fourth thing to vary alongside the four levers where-the-adapter-budget-goes-matters-more-than-how-much-there-is laid out (placement, initialization, parameterization, rank), and unlike those it changes what an adapter is rather than how it is configured. Nobody has measured what generating weights per instance costs at inference, which for a vault whose premise is one or two consumer cards is the number that decides whether it is usable at all.
