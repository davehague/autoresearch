Why MoE routing collapse resists correction and why some collapsed experts recover while others effectively don't: a geometric vanishing-gradient term built into softmax itself, decomposed from a task-specific advantage term whose scaling exponent alone decides whether recovery is cheap (logarithmic) or practically unbounded (power-law)

TL;DR: This map has carried standard fixes for routing collapse (balance losses, router z-loss, expert-choice routing) since its coining, and three separate router-trustworthiness findings about what a trained router's output does and doesn't mean — but no prior source gave a mechanistic account of why collapse happens or what determines whether a suppressed expert recovers. This paper supplies that account from first principles: softmax's own Jacobian vanishes as a probability approaches zero, so a collapsing expert's recovery signal mechanically weakens exactly as it needs it most — collapse is partly a geometric fact about the softmax parameterization itself, not purely a bad-training-dynamics artifact any particular fix happens to patch. The useful payoff is the recovery-time scaling law: whether a suppressed expert's advantage term Γⱼ decays as a flat constant (α=0) or shrinks polynomially with the expert's own probability (α>0) as that probability approaches zero determines whether recovery time is logarithmic in the target threshold (cheap, roughly collapse-depth-independent) or polynomial in its inverse (expensive, blowing up for deeper collapses). This reframes "does a collapsed expert recover" as an empirical question about which regime a given training setup's Γⱼ sits in, rather than a binary the field has mostly treated it as. The one Transformer-MoE validation measures a specific intervention (removing a class-conditional router-gradient contrast term) and shows it measurably slows confirmed recovery — evidence that at least one component of Γⱼ is causally load-bearing for recovery speed in a real (if small) trained model, not just in the theory's idealized flows.

What it says. What "collapse" and "recovery" mean here. Collapse is treated as probability mass concentrating onto a proper face of the routing simplex (a strict subset of experts) with the complementary mass tending to zero; recovery is that complementary mass returning to a fixed positive threshold. The geometric term. Under bounded gradients, a suppressed probability's rate of change vanishes as the probability itself approaches zero — this is a property of the softmax Jacobian's structure, present regardless of the loss function, architecture, or data. It is why collapse, once deep enough, tends to be self-reinforcing: the correction signal available to fix it shrinks exactly as the problem worsens. The task-specific term. Γⱼ packages what is NOT purely geometric: a covariance between the suppressed expert and whichever experts are actively competing for the same tokens, plus the suppressed expert's relative gradient advantage. This is where any given training setup's specific choices (balance losses, router-gradient shaping, capacity constraints) would show up, though the paper tests only one such choice directly. Recovery-time law. The exponent α describing how Γⱼ scales near x=0 is the single quantity that decides the recovery-time regime: α=0 gives Θ(log(1/ε)) — recovery is cheap and its cost barely depends on how deep the collapse started; α>0 gives Θ(ε^-α) — recovery cost blows up as the required threshold ε shrinks, meaning deeper collapses take disproportionately, possibly impractically, longer. Validation. The scaling law is checked against constructed low-dimensional geometries (not a trained model) with prescribed Γⱼ behavior, matching the predicted Θ(·) order against numerically measured hitting times. The one real-model check removes a specific gradient-contrast term in a small (6-layer, 8-expert, AG News) Transformer-MoE and finds recovery measurably slower without it (44-59 step delay, CIs above zero) — consistent with that term mattering for Γⱼ's behavior, though the paper does not report which α regime (log vs. power-law) this specific model/setup actually sits in.

Caveats. The scaling-law validation uses constructed toy geometries, not measurements on a trained model — the one trained-model experiment tests a different, narrower claim (does removing one specific intervention slow recovery), not the α-regime claim itself, so there is a real gap between the theory's headline result and what is empirically confirmed on anything resembling a production MoE. The Transformer-MoE validation is small (6 layers, 8 experts, AG News — a classification-flavored dataset, not an LM pretraining or instruction-tuning run) and tests one specific gradient-contrast intervention; no comparison is run against this map's standard collapse mitigations (balance losses, router z-loss, expert-choice routing), so whether any of those already-standard fixes operate by changing Γⱼ's exponent, by a different mechanism entirely, or not at all relative to this paper's framework is unaddressed. Nothing in the paper measures or estimates which α-regime real production MoE routers' underperforming experts actually sit in, so the practical question a practitioner would ask — is my model's collapsed expert on a fast or slow recovery clock — remains open pending a measurement this paper does not supply.
