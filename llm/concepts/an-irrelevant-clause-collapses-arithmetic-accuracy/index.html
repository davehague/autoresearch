One inert sentence, and two thirds of the score is gone

"Oliver picks 44 kiwis on Friday, 58 on Saturday, and on Sunday double what he picked on Friday, but five of them were a bit smaller than average. How many kiwis does Oliver have?" The clause changes nothing. Both a small open model and a reasoning-trained frontier model subtract the five.

Do this systematically — append one grammatical, topical, operationally inert clause to each templated question — and every model tested drops, most of them catastrophically. The small open models lose 57 to 66 points; the frontier models lose 32 to 40; the reasoning-trained ones lose 17.5 to 29.1, which is materially better and still large for a class sold on reasoning. A common failure the authors observe is models converting a statement about "discount" into a multiplication regardless of context, i.e. treating the presence of a familiar word as an instruction to perform its usual operation.

The repair that should work does not. Provide eight shots of the same question, drawn from the perturbation family, so the model has the exact reasoning chain in context; performance stays within a standard deviation of the unhelped score, and in one case falls further (29.4 → 20.2). Shots drawn from other problems that also contain inert clauses — which share the lesson that the clause should be ignored — do not fix it either. The authors' conclusion is that this "cannot be alleviated by in-context shots and needs further investigation." Two models buck the pattern strikingly, one recovering from 16.2 to 62.5 with same-question shots, which is unexplained and slightly softens the claim.

The gentler version of the same probe is arguably more informative because it is not adversarial. Add or remove ordinary, relevant clauses to build four difficulty levels, and every model's distribution shifts left and widens — one 9B model reads 84.4 (±2.4) → 79.1 (±3.0) → 68.1 (±4.8) → 41.8 (±6.0) across the four, while a reasoning-trained model reads 94.9 → 94.5 → 94.3 → 89.1. The rate of decline outpaces the number of reasoning steps added, and the growing variance says the search that produces the answer is getting harder rather than the arithmetic getting longer.

Three consequences this vault should carry.

For evaluation-contamination it is the reason perturbation testing is a blended instrument: brittleness and leakage both produce a drop when you rewrite a benchmark, so a perturbation gap is not by itself a contamination verdict.

For distillation-small-models it is an unwelcome pattern rather than a proof. The Phi family occupies four of the five worst positions on this test, and Phi is the canonical case of a small model made strong by curated and synthetic training data — the pipeline that data-curation found, on 2026-07-27, carries paraphrased benchmark items by construction when it generates from public seeds. High benchmark scores plus extreme distractor sensitivity is what that pipeline would predict. The models with the most to lose also lose the most, so the vault cannot conclude it from this evidence; it can name the test.

For post-training-rl it is a real, bounded win for pure-rl-elicits-reasoning and distilled-reasoners-beat-direct-rl: whatever reasoning training installs, it roughly halves distractor sensitivity relative to instruction-tuned models of the same era. It does not remove it.

The authors' own reading is that this is better explained by in-distribution pattern matching — aligning a question and its solution steps against similar ones seen in training — than by formal reasoning, since no formal reasoner is perturbed by a name change or an inert sentence.
