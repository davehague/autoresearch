Amdahl's law, measured instead of assumed

The kernel number is real: 12–14× over scalar, ~2× over the vectorized FlashAttention already shipping in ggml, on physical RISC-V hardware, with generated tokens verified to match the baseline exactly. The end-to-end number is also real, and it is 4% on the one configuration where it is positive and "comparable" everywhere else.

Nothing went wrong. The authors state the arithmetic plainly: linear projection and feed-forward layers are byte-identical between their build and the baseline, and attention is about half of per-layer execution during prefill and about a tenth during decode. A tenth of the work made fourteen times faster caps the total gain near 9%, before you account for prefill being a one-off and decode being where an interactive session spends its life.

Two things make this worth a note rather than a footnote.

The first is that almost no kernel paper reports it. local-inference carries kernel and runtime speedups from a dozen sources, and the convention is to report the accelerated component. This vault already has one instance of the same hygiene failing in the other direction — a-single-op-benchmark-measures-synchronization-not-dispatch, where timing a single GPU operation overstated its cost by 20× because the measurement captured synchronization the deployed path pays once per token. Both cases have the same moral: the component measurement and the deployment measurement are different quantities, and the ratio between them is large and unpredictable. When a source gives only one, treat the other as unknown rather than proportional.

The second is what it implies about where CPU-class local inference is actually bounded. If attention is a tenth of decode, then decode is dominated by streaming the weight matrices of the linear and feed-forward layers — which is exactly the memory-bandwidth story attention-is-memory-bound-not-compute-bound tells about GPUs, relocated to a device where the bandwidth is far scarcer. It converges with the-cpu-inference-bottleneck-was-the-memory-allocator and at-batch-size-one-the-bottleneck-is-dispatch-count, both of which found the binding constraint on single-user local inference somewhere other than the arithmetic everyone optimizes. Three unrelated instruments, three different non-attention bottlenecks.

There is a sharp corollary in the same paper. The layers that dominate — linear and FFN — are precisely the ones the source shows cannot use wide vectors at all under ggml's Q8_0 layout (the-quantized-weight-layout-caps-usable-vector-width). So the 4% is not a ceiling imposed by physics; it is a ceiling imposed by the format, sitting on the 90% of decode that the kernel work never touched.

Scope, stated honestly: one board at 256-bit vector length, models from 0.4B to 1.1B, throughputs of 2–8 tokens per second, no energy measurement. The long-vector projections are simulation. A consumer GPU is a different machine and none of this transfers to it numerically — what transfers is the discipline of demanding the end-to-end number, and the specific finding that on bandwidth-poor hardware, attention is not the thing to optimize.
