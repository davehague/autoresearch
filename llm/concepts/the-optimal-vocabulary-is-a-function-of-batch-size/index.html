The optimal vocabulary is a function of batch size

Vocabulary size gets picked once, before anything is trained, and then never revisited. This is the first source in the vault to ask what that choice costs downstream and to answer with a swept measurement rather than a convention. The answer is that the question "what is the right vocabulary size?" has no scalar answer: V* moves 16× across the serving batch, from 32k at B=1 to 524k at B=64+, and the mechanism is vocabulary-size-is-a-bandwidth-term-at-batch-one — the V×d unembedding read is either paid per token or amortized across the batch.

The lifecycle table is where the practitioner content sits. At λ=0 — a model you train and never serve — the optimum is 16k at every batch, because a larger vocabulary costs training throughput (198,401 tok/s at 8k down to 83,132 at 262k). Introduce any serving volume and the optimum moves right, but how far depends entirely on B: at B=1 it stops at 32k no matter how large λ gets, while at B ≥ 256 it runs to 262k. Two deployments of the same model, same weights, same task, want vocabularies an order of magnitude apart.

For this vault's premise — open weights, your own hardware — the relevant cell is B=1, and it reads 32k against the vocabularies actually shipping: Qwen3 at 151,936, Gemma at 262,144. Those were chosen for multilingual coverage and for the batch sizes their labs serve at, both defensible reasons, and neither is a reason that transfers to a laptop. This does not make them wrong; it makes them someone else's optimum, inherited. The lever that exists on the receiving end is corpus-driven row deletion (an-unused-vocabulary-is-deletable-weight), and this supplies the half of that argument the pruning paper was missing: the pruning work measured parameters and never measured time, this measures time and never prunes, and they point the same direction for the same deployment.

The generalization worth carrying past tokenization: an efficiency parameter frozen at training time is a bet on a serving regime. local-inference's currency test says a lever bought in one currency is charged in another. Here the same shape appears one stage earlier — a large vocabulary buys compression (fewer decode steps per character) and charges bandwidth (a bigger matrix read per step) — and the sign of the trade is decided by a number nobody has yet when the tokenizer is trained.

Bounds. Largest training run 2.3B for 5k steps, so the optimum at 7B+ rests on a V* ∝ √N extrapolation, and the authors flag that their V=262k model may simply be undertrained at step-matched budget. English FineWeb-Edu only. Decoder-only GPT only — MoE, state-space and encoder-decoder models have different body/head cost ratios. The int4 extension is analytic with no measurement behind it.
