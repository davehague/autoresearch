BM25 ties the oracle, and attention scores do not

Nearly every KV-cache method in this vault selects what to keep using a signal derived from attention: accumulated attention mass (H2O), attention from an observation window (SnapKV), recent attention (TOVA), per-head budget allocation over those scores (AdaKV), counter-causal surprise, and the recycled per-head statistics that make train-free self-speculation work (a-recallable-sparse-cache-is-a-free-drafter). The shared premise is that the model's own attention tells you what matters.

This source runs the selector as an isolated variable on a controlled RULER single-needle sweep and finds the premise does not hold at length. Oracle selection and BM25 both hold 100 at 8k, 16k and 32k. Recency falls to roughly 64 and reader-attention to roughly 78 by 32k. The authors' conclusion is blunt: "the observed bottleneck on this diagnostic is therefore selection, not cached-state fidelity."

Two things follow, in opposite directions, and the map should carry both.

The uncomfortable one, for this map. A large fraction of KV-compression research optimizes the representation of what was kept — better scoring, better merging, better reconstruction of the omitted mass — on the assumption that selection is roughly solved and fidelity is the frontier. If a lexical retriever from 2009 ties an oracle where attention-derived selection loses 20–36 points, then on retrieval-shaped workloads the ordering of effort is backwards. That is a real tension with what-eviction-deletes-is-two-sums-not-a-set-of-tokens, which improves fidelity at fixed budget and finds its largest gains on distributed-evidence tasks — Variable Tracking, FWE, multi-key retrieval. The two results are compatible and the boundary between them is legible: when the evidence is one identifiable span, selection is the binding constraint and lexical matching finds it; when the evidence is diffuse across many low-scoring positions, there is nothing to select and aggregate fidelity is what is missing.

The reassuring one, for anyone building. The best selector here needs no training, no model, no calibration and no attention statistics at all. It is BM25 over chunk text with top-12, hop width four and three automatic frontier-expansion rounds. That is cheap enough to sit in front of any of this map's other levers.

Three limits keep this suggestive. RULER is synthetic and single-needle retrieval is the case most favourable to lexical matching — a needle inserted as literal text shares surface form with its query almost by construction. The source says as much in its limitations: "lexical retrieval may be less suitable when relevant passages share little surface form with the query," and on the natural dialogue benchmark the picture is less clean. The comparison is not fully like-for-like: an eviction method's attention scores select tokens inside a resident cache, while BM25 selects 512-token chunks from an external store, and the second has strictly more information available (the raw text) than the first. And it is one backbone, Qwen3-8B, on one benchmark family.

What survives all three: the selector is a first-class design choice that this map's literature has treated as an implementation detail, and nobody has run the obvious ablation — a lexical or dense retriever as the scoring signal for token-axis eviction, against the attention scores everyone uses.
