Quantization damage has a task term, not just a weight term

Every allocation method on post-training-quantization scores a weight's importance and spends bits accordingly. This decomposition says that scoring is structurally incomplete. Write the signal-to-noise ratio of a quantized module in the log domain and it splits three ways:

  • M — the magnitude of the original weight relative to its quantization error. Pure weight-space, computable with no data, and the only term a quantizer designer manipulates directly.
  • S — "the energy of the task-specific activations along the direction of the full-precision weight." How much the workload actually uses this weight.
  • N — "the activation energy along the direction of quantization error." How badly the error happens to point where the workload's activations live.

Two modules with identical weight-space error can therefore differ arbitrarily in what they cost you, because S and N are computed against activations and activations belong to the task.

This is the mechanism underneath quantization-multiplies-the-decision-margin, and the two accounts do not collide despite looking like they should. The margin study fitted a multiplicative relation at the decision level and beat three additive alternatives by BIC in 85% of damaged cells; this decomposition is additive — but additive in log-SNR, which is multiplicative in the underlying ratio. Same shape, two levels of description, no shared citation. What this adds is the composition of the constant: the surviving fraction c is not a free parameter of a model, it is M modulated by S and N and then run through a per-model propagation gain (small-models-amplify-quantization-error-large-ones-attenuate-it).

That composition predicts the margin study's most operationally annoying result rather than merely agreeing with it. Borrowing another model's fitted c costs 0.2–8.5pp at 4 bits and 18–33pp at 3 (a-paired-margin-set-prices-quantization-damage-without-generation). If c contains a task-dependent alignment term, non-transfer is the expectation — and the prescription there is under-strict: a paired margin set should be re-measured per model, per bit-width, and per task family. The margin study's own asymmetry (at 3 bits an agent stops calling tools while which tool it picks is untouched) is two tasks on one model disagreeing, which is exactly what a task-dependent N produces.

It also explains a negative result the map holds and could not account for. Five repairs — including importance-based weight protection in four variants against a random-weight control — failed to beat spending one more bit. Importance scored in weight space moves M alone. An extra bit moves M for everything, including wherever N happens to be large, which is unknown to a weight-space scorer. The obvious experiment follows and nobody has run it: an N-aware quantizer, calibrated on the target workload's activations against the error direction rather than the weight direction, tested against the extra-bit control.

Finally it draws the boundary of the vault's data-free programme precisely. a-frozen-weight-spectrum-can-replace-a-calibration-set argues the spectrum can answer questions about a weight's role. M is reachable that way; S and N are activation-space and have no weight-space proxy here — the same wall activation-outliers-are-the-barrier-to-w8a8 hits from the other side. Try the spectrum when the decision is about role; keep calibration when the decision is about range now has two named terms explaining why the second half will not go away.

Graded strong on the decomposition (fitted across a four-quantizer × four-bit-width × eleven-task grid with a within-family scale ladder) and deliberately not graded as a recommendation: the paper proposes no method, measures no repair, and is weight-only PTQ throughout, with the authors stating the findings should not be extrapolated to activation quantization, quantization-aware training, or combined compression.
