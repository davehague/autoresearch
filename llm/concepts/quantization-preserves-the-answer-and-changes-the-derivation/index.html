The answer survives; the derivation does not

post-training-quantization's damage model has two axes as of 2026-08-01. Damage is task-shaped — load-bearing for multi-step arithmetic, near-free for single-step commonsense (the-gguf-quant-ladder-is-graded-not-a-cliff) — and signal-shaped, costing the model's ability to know whether its own answer is right (four-bit-costs-the-confidence-signal-not-the-answer). This adds a third, and it is the one that is hardest to instrument: damage is process-shaped.

The setup is unusually careful for a venue-less preprint. Thirty thousand chain-of-thought outputs across a 5 × 3 × 4 grid, classified by an LLM judge into a six-category taxonomy that separates how a correct answer was reached from why a wrong one failed, with the taxonomy itself validated against two human annotators at κ = 0.906 and per-category reliability reported rather than hidden. Greedy decoding throughout, so a distributional shift cannot be sampling noise.

Accuracy behaves exactly as this map already says it does: maximum 3.1 points anywhere in the grid, every interval overlapping. "By any standard evaluation checklist, none of these five models would get flagged for degradation."

Underneath, on LLaMA 3.2-3B under NF4, the composition of wrong answers moves: Shortcut Collapse 44% → 78% while Confidence Snowballing 15.8% → 0.2%. The reading the authors give is mechanical and plausible — Confidence Snowballing requires holding a coherent multi-step chain together long enough for an early error to propagate through it, Shortcut Collapse is what happens when the model jumps straight to an answer, so the shift says precision reduction is degrading the capacity to sustain a chain rather than adding uniform noise. Both produce a wrong answer, so the aggregate does not move.

Three things constrain how far this can be pushed, and the vault should carry all three.

The size dependence is the actionable half. Two models shift and three do not, and the two are the smallest (3B and 8B). Models at 12B and above are statistically invariant. That is the same direction as at-a-fixed-footprint-take-the-larger-quantized-model — quantization damage concentrates at the small end — and it points the same practitioner rule at a different metric.

The judge is not equally reliable across categories. Cross-judge agreement is 80.6% for Shortcut Collapse and 0.0% for Confidence Snowballing, which the authors state and flag. So the rise is the supported half and the collapse is suggestive. This is a live instance of llm-judges-match-human-agreement-and-carry-systematic-bias applied to a categorical rather than a preference judgment, and the paper handles it about as well as an LLM-judged study can.

And the biggest single move in the paper is not caused by 4-bit at all. LLaMA 3.1-8B's Hollow Convergence rate goes 29.9 → 13.8 from FP32 to FP16 and then 13.8 → 13.6 from FP16 to NF4 — so more than 99% of that model's shift happens at a precision step nobody considers aggressive, and the χ² the paper cites for it is a three-way test across all precisions rather than an NF4 effect. Only the 3B model shows the move at the NF4 step. The defensible claim is therefore about precision reduction generally, not about the four-bit floor this map is organized around — which is a weaker claim about 4-bit and a stranger one about FP16, since nobody expects half precision to change anything.

Scope, from the authors: BitsAndBytes NF4 only, and GPTQ and AWQ round weights against calibration data rather than mapping onto a fixed NormalFloat grid, so there is no guarantee the shift reproduces under them. English only, instruction-tuned models only, effect sizes small by convention (Cramér's V about 0.23–0.24).

Rebutted by: a-longer-reasoning-trace-absorbs-approximation-error — not on this measurement but on the generalization drawn from it, that multi-step derivation is where approximation damage lands. Under an arithmetic-side approximation (expert channel coalescing), Qwen3 loses 3–4% on GSM8K in non-thinking mode and nothing in thinking mode (88.0 against an 87.3 baseline), which says a longer chain can absorb per-step error rather than compound it. The scope condition that reconciles them: perturbing the record of a derivation is worse on long chains; perturbing the arithmetic producing each step is better on long chains. One study, final answers only, and nobody has separated the two.
