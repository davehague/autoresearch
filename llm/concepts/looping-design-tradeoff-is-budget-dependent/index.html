Looping a transformer is not one design choice but a family of them, and which point in that family wins depends on whether parameters or training FLOPs are the binding constraint — which reframes this map's three named looped-architecture releases as three points in one space rather than three independent inventions

TL;DR: This map has carried three named looped-architecture instances — Nanbeige (2026-08-18, prefill activation memory), BeNice (2026-08-24, KV-cache sharing fix), and Accordion (2026-10-26, fixed-prefix-window MoE looping) — each measuring the "reuse the stack, pay elsewhere" trade in a different currency, with no source testing whether any two of them occupy the same point in design space. This paper is the first systematic answer: a from-scratch scaling study (not one named model) sweeping what gets looped (full stack vs. a middle window), how many times, and how loop iterations are specialized, across 100M-1B parameters and up to 1.07×10²¹ training FLOPs. The headline result is that the "right" thing to loop is not fixed — it depends on which budget binds. Under a fixed parameter count, loop the whole stack. Under a fixed training-FLOP count, loop only a middle window, because each loop iteration costs less per pass. Neither looping variant beats a model that is simply built deeper at matched FLOPs; looping buys depth cheaply in parameters, not in compute. Loop-specific low-rank adapters narrow but do not close that gap, at far lower parameter cost than doubling depth outright, and the narrowing grows with loop count. Against Accordion specifically — a fixed-prefix-window (layers 7-18 of 24) looped MoE reaching real training scale — this paper's middle-block-wins-at-fixed-FLOPs result reads as a plausible explanation for why Accordion chose a middle window rather than the full stack, though the paper does not test Accordion's own architecture and this reading is this vault's inference, not the source's claim.

What it says.

The design space. "Looping" re-applies one block of transformer weights for L forward passes before continuing to the next distinct block — adding effective depth with zero extra parameters. The paper varies three axes: which layers are looped (the entire stack, vs. a contiguous middle window), the loop count L (up to 32 at the smallest scale; compute-limited to L≤8 at 200M-500M and L≤4 at 1B), and how loop iterations are specialized (three schemes tested; a per-iteration low-rank transform, "Loop Matrix," wins).

Budget-dependent winner. At a fixed parameter budget, full-stack looping reaches the lowest DCLM bits-per-byte among the swept configurations. At a fixed training-FLOP budget, a middle-window loop wins instead — each pass through the smaller looped window is cheaper, so the same compute buys more effective depth. Across the board, a non-looped model built with genuinely more distinct layers still beats every looped variant at matched training FLOPs; looping approximates depth cheaply in parameter count, not in compute, and the paper does not claim otherwise.

Specialization and its limits. Loop-specific low-rank ("Loop Matrix") adapters are marginal at L=2 and clearly helpful at L∈{4,8}, with the benefit growing as loop count rises. At 300M parameters they add only 1.1-4.3% extra non-embedding parameters, against 51.4% for literally doubling depth — a far cheaper partial substitute, not a replacement, since the literally-deeper model still wins at matched FLOPs.

Parallel Loop Transformers (PLT) get a stability fix. PLT avoids the sequential-decode cost ordinary looping imposes at inference by combining the current token's embedding with the previous token's hidden state into one modified-attention input — but the original (summed, unnormalized) formulation is unstable: dropping the current-token-embedding term makes a "shifted" PLT variant underperform a no-looping baseline, and some training runs diverge outright. Separately RMS-normalizing the current-token embedding and the previous-token hidden state, then mixing them with a learned gate, fixes this: at 1B parameters and L=2, "Normalized Shifted PLT" beats full-stack looping's DCLM BPB at every training budget tested, tracking closely behind its own unshifted control; at L=4 it stays competitive with full-stack looping but its gap to the unshifted control widens.

Caveats. Compute-limited sweeps mean the largest loop counts are only tested at the smallest model scales, and loop-count benefits saturate, reverse, or become unstable at high L even at small scale (100M). Loop-specific specialization does not remove the sequential-decode cost of ordinary (non-parallel) looping — PLT exists separately to address that, and the PLT fix's own benefit narrows as loop count rises. No exact DCLM BPB numbers, downstream benchmark deltas, or KV-cache multipliers are reproduced in the text read (the paper's quantitative comparisons are carried in figures); no code or weight release is stated. This paper trains every configuration from scratch on one data mixture and does not evaluate any of this vault's three named looped releases directly — the reading that it explains Accordion's middle-window choice is this vault's cross-map inference, not a claim the source itself makes.
