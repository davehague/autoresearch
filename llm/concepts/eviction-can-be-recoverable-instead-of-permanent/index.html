Eviction does not have to be a permanent decision

Every token-eviction policy in this vault — StreamingLLM, SnapKV, AdaKV, H2O, PyramidKV, the counter-causal surprise criterion in predictable-past-tokens-are-the-redundant-ones — shares an assumption so basic that nobody states it: a state scored as unimportant is gone. The scoring methods differ, the budget differs, the reversibility does not exist.

QEvict's diagnosis is that the assumption is empirically wrong. Importance drifts as generated queries evolve, and windows that fall out of the retained set later receive substantial attention under the full-cache model — measured at a 6.18% revival rate against an oracle's 0.98% (see a-quiet-cache-window-comes-back-six-percent-of-the-time). If a policy is going to be wrong about 6% of its retention decisions, the useful question is not how to score better but how expensive it should be to be wrong.

The answer is a three-tier cache rather than a two-way switch. The highest-ranked windows stay in the native datatype. The next band goes to an INT2 tier that attention can still read. Only the lowest band is deleted. Every Ω = 8 tokens, cumulative attention scores re-rank the candidates, and a quantized window whose score recovers is dequantized and promoted back. The detail that makes the oscillation safe is migration-stable quantization: the codes and scales produced on a window's first demotion are kept and reused on every later transition, so a window that cycles between tiers does not accumulate requantization error the way a naive re-quantize-on-demote scheme would.

This is the first source in the vault that puts KV quantization and KV eviction inside one policy on one budget — the composition kv-cache-management was coined to want and has listed as its first open question since. Two honest qualifiers on reading it as an answer to that question. The two levers are applied to disjoint window sets, not stacked on the same states, so what is demonstrated is that a fixed budget can be partitioned between precision and deletion — not that quantizing an already-evicted cache is safe, which is still unrun and still has a reason to interfere. And the ordering in shrink-the-cache-by-bits-before-you-shrink-it-by-tokens is refined rather than overturned: bits still beat tokens where they compete, and the working scheme spends most of its budget on bits (42.6% of attention mass in INT2 against 19.7% at full precision).

The gains also grow as the budget tightens — the 5% column is where the margin over the best eviction baseline is largest (33.6 vs 27.4) — which is the regime a memory-bound reader is actually in. Mistral at 10% is the one cell where QEvict loses, by 0.2 points, and the paper prints it.

What none of this touches is whether the surviving cache still supports a derivation. Every number here is task accuracy, which accuracy-is-an-asymmetric-diagnostic-for-a-compressed-cache established one day earlier is inverted on exactly this kind of comparison.
