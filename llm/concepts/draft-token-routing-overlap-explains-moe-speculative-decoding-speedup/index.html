Draft-token positions in speculative decoding do not route to sparse-MoE experts independently — they overlap substantially, and that overlap, not an independence-assumption worst case, is what actually bounds the activated-expert union a verification pass must pay for

TL;DR: This vault's speculative-decoding map has carried, since 2026-08, a standing finding that drafting "inflates the activated-expert union" under MoE targets, with three independent measurements confirming the direction and one proposed (untested-on-GPU) remedy — route per prompt rather than per token. This paper is the first to measure how much the union actually inflates against a principled baseline (independent per-token routing) rather than assuming the worst case, and the answer is: substantially less than independence would predict, especially for fine-grained MoEs with many small experts. Adjacent draft positions in a speculative window share 20-45% of their activated experts empirically, against 4-12% if routing were independent — so the real union is 21-34% smaller than an independence-based estimate. This is why fine-grained models like Qwen3-30B and Qwen3-235B break the field's predicted concave speedup-vs-batch-size curve and instead see their best speedup at batch size 1: the verification pass's expert-read cost is lower than the worst-case accounting implied, precisely in the regime (low batch, high sparsity) where that worst case would otherwise dominate. A maximum-entropy IPF model, fit on a model's own measured routing-overlap statistics, converts this into a usable practitioner tool: predict the unique-expert count a verification pass will touch, within 4-5% error, without running the GPU experiment first.

What it says. The gap this closes. This map's three prior independent measurements of "speculation fights sparse routing" established the direction of the cost (draft tokens inflate the expert union relative to single-token decoding) but not its magnitude relative to a principled reference point — each treated the union's size as an empirical fact to work around (e.g., route per prompt instead of per token), not as something explainable from routing statistics. This paper supplies that explanation: routing overlap is not incidental, it is large and systematic, and it shrinks as the speculative window deepens (35.1% at the first position, down to 26.4% by the third) — meaning the marginal cost of each additional draft token, in unique-expert terms, falls as the window grows, which is the opposite of what a naive "more tokens, more risk of touching a new expert" intuition would suggest. Mechanism, not just measurement. The IPF framework treats the observed per-token routing marginals as fixed and asks how much joint (multi-token) expert activation a maximum-entropy assumption predicts given those marginals plus the measured pairwise overlap statistics — this is what makes the 4-5% error figure a genuine predictive tool rather than a post-hoc curve fit; it is fit once per model from routing statistics alone and then scored against held-out batch/dataset configurations. Scope of the fine-grained-breaks-the-trend finding. Coarser-grained MoEs (Mixtral-8x7B, Qwen2-57B — fewer, larger experts) still show the originally predicted concave curve; it is specifically the newer, fine-grained design trend (many small experts, low k/N) where the routing-overlap effect dominates enough to flip the shape of the speedup curve entirely. One model, Qwen3.5-35B, breaks the concave pattern despite the paper's own account not fully explaining why — an acknowledged residual.

Caveats. All four authors are Cohere employees on Cohere-funded work — not a neutral third party, though the model set tested spans several labs' releases. Measured entirely on H100 datacenter hardware; the consumer/offloaded-MoE regime this vault's speculative-decoding and mixture-of-experts maps track separately — where the expert-union cost has previously been shown to bind much harder because weights must be fetched rather than merely read from HBM — is untested here, and nothing in this paper's mechanism obviously transfers to that regime without its own measurement. The IPF model predicts unique-expert count, which is a necessary but not sufficient input to a latency prediction; converting it to wall-clock speedup still requires the GPU bandwidth/cache measurements this paper reports separately (L2 hit rate, HBM utilization), which are hardware- and kernel-specific. No code or data release is stated.
