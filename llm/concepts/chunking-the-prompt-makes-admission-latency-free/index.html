The second bill for iteration-level scheduling

scheduling-at-iteration-granularity-unblocks-generative-serving records why continuous batching won: a generative request needs an unpredictable number of forward passes, so scheduling per request strands early finishers and blocks new arrivals. local-inference already carries one unintended consequence of that win — Orca's max_tokens KV over-reservation is precisely the fragmentation PagedAttention was later written to fix. This is the second, and it is the more expensive one.

If a request can join a running batch at any iteration, then at some iteration the engine must compute that request's prompt. A prompt of several thousand tokens is one compute-bound iteration lasting hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, and every request already generating is frozen for its duration. The authors call it a generation stall and measure one in vLLM lasting over several seconds for Yi-34B on two A100s. The metric it destroys is time-between-tokens, which is what makes a response feel fluid; the throughput-oriented schedulers of the day all traded it away, and the decode-prioritizing alternative traded away throughput instead by refusing new arrivals until the batch drained.

The fix has two halves and the ablation shows each is bad alone.

Chunked prefills split a prompt into fixed-size pieces spread over several iterations. This is only affordable because prefill-saturates-compute-far-below-a-full-prompt — a 512-token chunk is nearly as efficient as the whole prompt, at a measured penalty of at most ~25% (and near zero at chunk 2048) from re-reading earlier chunks' KV cache.

Stall-free batching sets the order: compute a token budget from the latency target, pack every running decode first, then any half-finished prefill, and only then admit new requests sized to whatever budget is left. Nothing waits behind a prompt. The bound is the paper's cleanest number — naively coalescing a whole prefill into a decode batch inflates the iteration by up to 28.3×; with chunking the same configuration is 3.7×, and the penalty shrinks as decode batch size and context grow.

Used together the gain is 2.6–6.3× more sustainable load at a fixed P99 time-between-tokens target, across four models from 7B to 180B. Used separately, chunking alone raises time-to-first-token (chunks are slightly less efficient than a whole prompt) and hybrid batching alone still admits long prompts whole; together they beat both on both metrics.

Two structural claims fall out that no single paper here states. First, the token budget is the knob, not the batch size — vLLM's capacity in these experiments is "largely identical" across max batch sizes 32, 64 and 128, because under a latency constraint its scheduler stalls before its memory manager runs out; paging enabled large batches that the admission policy then could not use. Second, this makes pipeline parallelism viable for inference: PP bubbles in serving not because stages are uneven but because micro-batches have wildly different runtimes depending on their prefill/decode mix (a 4K prompt at ~1150 ms against a batch-32 decode at ~200 ms — a ~950 ms bubble), and uniform-compute hybrid batches remove most of it, worth 3.6× capacity under strict targets.

The honest scope: this is a multi-tenant technique. It exists to stop an arriving request from hurting incumbents, and with one user there are no incumbents. It does not add a clause to this map's regime rule so much as supply the reason the rule has the shape it does — continuous batching's whole value, and its whole cost, is concurrency.
