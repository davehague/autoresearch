Speculation is not free after all — it is free per unit only while the unit is small enough

local-inference carries speculative decoding as the map's one free lever: it spends idle arithmetic, and under a lossless verification rule it provably does not move the output distribution (speculation-trades-arithmetic-for-latency-without-moving-the-distribution). That framing is correct about quality and this source does not touch it. What it corrects is the assumption riding underneath — that having established the lever is quality-free, more of it is better.

Write the speedup exactly. With speculation length k, acceptance rate α, verification cost C_v and per-token drafting cost C_d, one step emits αk + 1 tokens for kC_d + C_v, so

S = (αk + 1)/(kγ + 1) where γ = C_d/C_v, and ∂S/∂k = (α − γ)/(kγ + 1)².

The sign of that derivative is the sign of α − γ. If acceptance exceeds relative drafting cost, longer is monotonically better; if it does not, every additional drafted token makes the system slower. And α is not a constant — compounding autoregressive uncertainty makes the tail tokens progressively less likely to be accepted, so α_tail falls as k grows and eventually crosses γ from above. The paper calls the crossing an efficiency inversion.

The measurement is worse than the theory suggests. MagicDec with SnapKV on LongBench v2 peaks at 1.74× at k=2 and reads 0.36× at k=16 — nearly three times slower than plain autoregressive decoding — as acceptance falls 33.5% → 6.3%. The inversion also reproduces inside the paper's own winning method under a fixed k: 2.10× / 2.39× / 2.69× / 2.66× / 2.62× at k = 4 / 8 / 12 / 16 / 20, with acceptance sliding 93.4% → 67.9%.

Three consequences for this map.

A published speedup is a claim about a tuned k, not about a method. MagicDec's often-quoted 80.1% acceptance is achieved only by refusing to speculate far (k=2, 1.59 accepted tokens per step). Comparing two speculative methods at their own best k compares two different points on two different curves. This is the same shape as evaluation-contamination's flagged recipe-leakage mode — a preparation parameter that changes the number by more than the method does and goes unreported.

The right k is a runtime decision, and estimating it is cheap. The paper's controller tracks exponential moving averages of accepted and rejected draft-token entropies, scores each new draft token by its distance to those two centroids, and maximizes (1 + Σ_m Π_{i≤m} p_i)/(kC_d + C_v) over a small candidate set. It costs under 0.5% of step latency and beats every fixed k (2.79×) without manual tuning. Where the gain comes from is honest: on heterogeneous workloads (RULER needle-in-a-haystack) adaptivity is worth +17.3% throughput over the best fixed setting; on uniform ones (∞-Bench) it merely matches the best fixed k while removing the need to find it.

And this composes badly with the map's other levers, in the direction the map already suspects. γ = C_d/C_v is a ratio, so anything that makes verification cheaper — a fused attention kernel, quantized weights, a smaller batch — raises γ and moves the inversion point closer, shrinking the useful speculation horizon. That is the currency test again (speculation-and-quantization-compete-for-the-same-headroom) with an equation attached: optimizing the verifier partially disarms the speculator, and no source in-vault has measured the two together.
