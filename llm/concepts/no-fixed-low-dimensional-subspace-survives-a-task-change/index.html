The subspace you calibrated is a subspace of that dataset

The most useful paragraph in this source is in an appendix, describes an approach that failed, and would have been left out by most authors. They started where a systems engineer would start: find a fixed low-dimensional subspace during prefill, reuse it through decode, and collect the compounding prizes — a smaller KV cache, less memory traffic, a static kernel. It did not work. A subspace derived from WikiText was good on WikiText and "degrades sharply when evaluated on other tasks or distributions," and head dimensions that looked reliably inactive for one task were "not stable across tasks, layers, or inputs." Their conclusion is that hidden states admit no single globally valid low-dimensional subspace: information migrates across dimensions with the input, the layer and the decoding step.

That is a finding about the representation, not about their method, and it lands on structured-pruning as a scope condition.

This map's newest and most encouraging result is that structural importance is calibration-robust — SNIPER's layer-importance profile moves 0.85 points across Slim Orca, Alpaca and C4 where a competing pruner moves 11.6 (a-pruner-misses-the-compression-ratio-you-asked-for) — and this vault read that as evidence that the calibration confound recorded four separate times in post-training-quantization might not bind on pruning. The scope condition is now visible: those two signals are not the same object. A structural or layer-level importance score asks which blocks matter and is computed over an architecture; an activation-derived feature subspace asks which coordinates matter and is computed over a corpus. The first transfers across calibration corpora; the second, measured here, does not transfer across tasks at all. A practitioner reading "importance estimation is robust" and applying it to a channel-level or head-dimension selector is generalizing across a boundary the evidence does not cross.

The constructive half is that dynamic selection does work where static does not, and the margin is large: at retention 0.8 the input-adaptive method matches the dense baseline on summarization at every scale from 1B to 70B, while static and random pruning at the same retention "degrade severely." So the choice is not between good and bad pruning rules — it is between a rule fixed before it sees the input and a rule that re-decides per input, and that difference is worth more than any refinement within the static family.

This also gives post-training-quantization's calibration thread a mechanism it did not have. Four findings there record that a quantized model inherits its calibration corpus's domain, content, corpus and language (quantization-calibration-sets-leak-their-domain-into-the-model, calibration-content-is-the-dominant-term-below-four-bits, quantization-damage-has-a-language-term). The reason is stated here from the representation side: there was no stable subspace to calibrate against, so whatever a calibration set selects is a property of that set. And it sharpens the same map's live question of whether weight-space reconstruction error is the wrong objective — reconstruction-error-is-the-wrong-loss-for-a-mapped-cache and an-int4-proxy-ranks-channels-it-cannot-compute are the same complaint, that a signal computed once against one distribution is being asked to hold everywhere.

Graded suggestive and the reason is the format: this is a prose account of unpublished failed experiments with no table, no numbers and no ablation. It is a T1 paper and the authors have no incentive to invent a negative result about their own first approach, but nothing here is reproducible as stated. The controlled version is one experiment: one model, one selector, subspaces fitted on three corpora, all three evaluated on all three tasks — a matrix somebody could run in a day, and it would settle whether the structural / activation split above is real.
