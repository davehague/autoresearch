One knob, two mechanisms, and only one of them works

Lenience-based relaxation of the speculative acceptance rule decomposes into three regions by how the draft q compares to the target p. Where the draft underestimates (q ≤ p) it interpolates between the two. Where it moderately overestimates (p < q ≤ p/ℓ) it emits q unchanged. Where it severely overestimates (q ≥ p/ℓ) it caps at p/ℓ. Published implementations ship all three as one ℓ.

Run them separately on MBPP+ and they are not two halves of one idea; they are a good idea and a bad one soldered together.

The ceiling costs at most 0.7 points of Pass@1 and in the best cell is above the lossless number, at a small block-efficiency gain. The interpolation buys 1.67× block efficiency for 25 points. "Ceiling the overshooting region alone achieves task performance comparable to lossless verification, suggesting that overshoot ceiling is its primary contribution."

The theoretical grounding the authors cite is that overshoot tokens drive most low-quality generations while the rest of the distribution barely affects quality — so correcting the region where a small draft is confidently wrong is a targeted fix, and interpolating everywhere else is indiscriminate damage.

The usable rule for anyone running speculation locally: cap the draft where it severely overestimates the target, and leave the undershoot region alone. It is the only lossy relaxation in this study whose quality cost is bounded and small, and it is the one that comes bundled with the mechanism that is not.

Read alongside lossy-verification-silently-replaces-the-target-distribution: the λ = 0.2 interpolation row is simultaneously the fastest configuration in the paper by the metric a local benchmark would report and the worst model in it. The same table that supplies the good default supplies the clearest demonstration that a speed number cannot detect a bad one.

Bounded to MBPP+ for the ablation specifically, on the model families this source uses, with no consumer-hardware measurement anywhere.
