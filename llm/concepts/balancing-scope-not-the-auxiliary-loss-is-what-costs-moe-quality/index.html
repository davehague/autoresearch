The contribution is named after the wrong thing

mixture-of-experts belief (3) records that routing collapse is self-reinforcing and that the standard fix is an auxiliary balance loss, kept small at the expert level because over-constraining expert balance costs quality. The frontier recipe replaces that loss with a bias term: each expert carries a learnable-by-rule offset b_i added to its affinity score for the top-K selection only and excluded from the gating weight that multiplies the expert's output, so the bias decides which experts fire while the unbiased affinity decides how much each contributes. After every step, overloaded experts' biases fall by γ and underloaded ones' rise by γ. Balance holds well enough that no tokens are dropped in training or inference, which removes the token-overflow failure mode outright.

The ablation supports it at two scales — better bits-per-byte and better scores on most benchmarks, with HumanEval gaining 6.1 points at the larger scale — and the technique is named "auxiliary-loss-free."

A second ablation in the same section says the name misattributes the mechanism. The real distinction is batch-wise versus sequence-wise balancing scope. A sequence-wise auxiliary loss requires every sequence to spread its tokens evenly across experts; since a single sequence is usually one domain, that is an instruction not to specialize. A batch-wise loss imposes the same average constraint over a much larger sample and leaves individual sequences free. Measured:

1B MoE3B MoE
sequence-wise auxiliary loss2.2582.085
auxiliary-loss-free2.2532.080
batch-wise auxiliary loss2.2532.080

The loss-based and loss-free methods are identical once the scope matches. So the mechanism is not that auxiliary losses corrupt the training gradient — it is that a per-sequence balance constraint is too strong, and the bias-term method's real contribution is that it balances over the batch by construction. That reframes what a practitioner should take away: balance over the largest scope your parallelism allows, which is portable to anyone training an MoE with any balancing mechanism, and does not require adopting the specific technique.

Two efficiency risks come with batch-wise scope and the report names both. Imbalance within a small batch — handled here by expert parallelism guaranteeing large micro-batches, which a small-scale trainer will not have. And domain-shift-induced imbalance at inference, handled at serving time by duplicating high-load experts and re-planning the redundant set from live statistics every ten minutes. Neither remedy is available below cluster scale, so the finding is a mechanism this vault can use and a recipe it cannot run.

This also sits alongside router-z-loss-buys-moe-stability-for-free as the second instance of the same shape in this map: an MoE stabilizer whose careful version costs nothing, where the naive version costs real quality. The map's failure-mode inventory should now distinguish whether you balance from over what.
