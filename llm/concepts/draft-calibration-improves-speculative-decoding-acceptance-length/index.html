A drafter's own output distribution, not just its architecture or training data, is a lever on speculative-decoding speedup — and today's open drafters are systematically miscalibrated in a fixable direction

TL;DR: speculative-decoding has spent its whole history on what the drafter is (architecture, provenance, width, shape) and on how far verification can be relaxed. This source adds a third, orthogonal lever: whether the drafter's output distribution matches the target's, independent of either. Measuring across nine target-draft pairs, the authors find open-source drafters (EAGLE-3, DFlash, DSpark checkpoints) consistently get the top candidate token right but assign it less probability mass than the target would — underconfidence, not wrong predictions. A single scalar temperature applied to the draft distribution alone (the target and the verification rule are untouched, so the exactness guarantee this map treats as load-bearing is preserved) closes most of that gap: accepted length rises ~9-11% and throughput ~5-22% depending on pair and serving concurrency, at essentially the cost of one extra scalar multiply. A harder per-token-adaptive calibration shows more headroom exists in principle (oracle grid search beats scalar scaling by 0.44-0.69 accepted tokens per position) but the trainable methods the authors tried could not yet capture that headroom — so scalar temperature scaling is, for now, the only calibration method here that is both deployable and reliably positive.

What breaks the headline. The gain is not uniform: it collapses for a drafter trained by a different lab (DeepSeek-trained Qwen3-14B checkpoints) to near zero across all three drafter architectures tested, which says the underconfidence pattern this paper exploits is a property of how today's open drafters happen to be trained, not a universal property of speculative decoding. That makes "calibrate your draft model's temperature" a cheap thing worth checking per-drafter, not a fixed constant to adopt.

Relation to this map's existing temperature finding. a-drafters-acceptance-rate-depends-on-the-temperature-you-decode-at already established that acceptance rate is a claim about the target's sampling temperature — pooling results across temperatures hides a real confound in every reported acceptance number on this map. This source is a different claim about a different object: it calibrates the draft model's own distribution via its own temperature parameter, and reports it as a genuine, deployable speedup rather than a methodological warning. The two findings are adjacent (both turn the dial called "temperature") but neither tests nor resolves the other — a reader should not conflate "report your sampling temperature" with "calibrate your drafter's temperature."

Caveats. No code or project link found. No code-confirmed replication. The calibration proxy used to pick the scaling temperature (trajectory replay) has 1.83-2.64% mean relative error and sometimes misranks the true optimum. Training-based calibration is explicitly left open by the authors. Untested in composition with any of this map's architecture, provenance, or verification-relaxation levers.
