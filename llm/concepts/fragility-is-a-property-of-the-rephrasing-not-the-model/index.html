What breaks the answer is the rewrite, not the model

The useful consequence of phrasing-sensitivity-does-not-fade-with-capability-it-changes-sign is not the size of the range — it is that the range is attributable. Models differ in how much they drift overall, but they largely agree on the ordering of which rephrasings cost correct answers, which makes brittleness a property you can measure once and reuse rather than a fog around every score.

The spread is nearly 40 points on its own. Structural interrogative expansion — taking a word problem and splitting it into the explicit sub-questions it implies — costs 48.9% of correct answers. Adding whitespace costs 11.7%. Narrative-style rewriting costs 27.9%. Notice what the worst offender is: turning one question into a sequence of smaller questions is the shape of scaffolding, of chain-of-thought prompting, and of every agentic decomposition pattern, and it is the single most destructive meaning-preserving edit tested. That is a finding about deployment, not just about benchmarks — the transformation a practitioner is most likely to apply on purpose is the one the model handles worst.

Positive and negative drift are not opposites, which kills the natural simplification. Symbolic representation sits near the top of both lists (25.3% negative, 20.1% positive), and one transformation is top-8 on both at 41.2% negative and 21.6% positive. So a rephrasing is not "helpful" or "harmful" — it reshuffles which problems a model gets right, and reporting one direction misreads what it did. This is the same failure of a one-directional summary that averaging-a-suite-hides-its-weakest-dimension describes at the suite level, one layer down.

It is also not a confidence problem. Negative drift falls as baseline confidence rises — 38.1% in the low bin to 18.5% in the high — but never approaches zero, running 13–26% within every model tested. Rephrasing breaks answers the model was confident about, which rules out the comfortable reading that drift lives in the items a model was going to get wrong anyway and connects to stated-confidence-does-not-improve-with-scale from the calibration side.

The cheap protocol is the part to adopt. The full variation budget finds 67.2% of problems drifting; five variations recover 74% of that, eight 88%, ten 93% — and a single variation finds only 30%. So the audit is not expensive: ten meaning-preserving rewrites per problem on a 500-problem slice captures nearly all of the detectable brittleness, which is a periodic-audit cost rather than a per-run one. Against two prompt optimizers on the same problems, this returns a 73.3-point range that neither reports, because an optimizer searches for the best prompt and discards the spread — the spread being exactly what a reader of a benchmark number needs.

This map should now state the reporting rule in the form the evidence supports: a benchmark number is incomplete without a spread, and the cheapest honest spread is ten validated rephrasings. That sits alongside a-benchmark-score-should-carry-an-expiration-date (when it was measured) and a-detector-needs-a-no-verdict-branch (whether the instrument could tell) as the third thing a score has to carry to be readable.

Bounds: closed-form benchmarks only, largest model 34B, temperature 0, and the per-transformation ordering is reported as broadly consistent across model pairs rather than as a correlation coefficient — the agreement is qualitative in the text where the drift rates are quantitative.
