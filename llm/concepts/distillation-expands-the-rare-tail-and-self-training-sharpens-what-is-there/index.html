Distillation expands the rare tail; self-training sharpens what is there

distillation-small-models has held from its first ingest that you derive a small model from a large one rather than train it small, and its second sub-thread argues about what actually transfers — on-policy versus off-policy dynamics, distributional novelty over teacher rank, pass@K collapse. The recurring difficulty is that the arguments are about mechanism while the evidence is usually a benchmark average, which cannot distinguish "the student can now do things it could not" from "the student got better at what it could already sometimes do."

This separates them under a measured null, which is why it is worth a note despite confirming what the map already believed.

Four arms, matched in data stream, volume and evaluation. Three are self-training: STaR (+5.6 to +6.9 accuracy points), majority-vote SFT (−2.8 to +4.0, unstable across seeds), and a TTRL-style policy gradient that collapses into unbounded repetition on AIME across all three seeds. One is external distillation from a larger open model, gpt-oss-120b (+13 to +16 points).

On the 22 problems the base model rarely reaches — base solve rate ≤ 5 out of 1,408 pooled draws — distillation registers 8–11 expansions per seed under a per-problem exact test with FDR control. The three self-training arms register 0–2 (p < 10⁻⁶). The obvious objection is that distillation simply gained more overall and expansions come along for free; a regression rejects that at p < 10⁻⁸.

The boundary the paper draws on itself is the part to keep. On the 10 problems the base never reaches at all, the comparison is not significant (p = 0.35). So the licensed claim is about the rare tail, not the absent one: distillation moves the mass on problems the student could already occasionally reach, and whether it can install something genuinely absent is unresolved at this sample size. That is a narrower statement than "distillation teaches new capabilities," and it is the one the data supports.

What this map should update. The build-path ordering (strand 1) gains a control-grade confirmation with a mechanism attached — the advantage is specifically where in the difficulty distribution the gain lands, not merely how large it is. The transfer-mechanism argument (strand 2) gains a measurement instrument: the right way to argue about what transfers is a per-problem test against a pooled baseline denominator, not a difference of means, because the interesting effects live in a tail that means average away. And the negative half is the sharper contribution — three separate self-improvement recipes, including one RL-flavoured one, all fail to expand the rare tail, which is a stronger statement than any single failed replication.

Bounded, per the authors: one model family (Qwen3-8B), three rounds of rank-32 LoRA, 22 low-base problems on one benchmark. And every self-training arm here is iterated on its own outputs; nothing here speaks to self-training against an external verifier or a checker-based reward, which is post-training-rl's subject and a different loop.
