The bit-width is a memory claim; the speedup is a separate measurement that nobody prints beside it

This map scores quantization on quality against bit-width, and the whole vault has absorbed the habit: 4-bit is nearly free, x bits per weight, y points lost. Almost none of that literature states what the bits bought in wall-clock, and this source's contribution is to measure the other axis on the hardware scale this vault cares about and find it much smaller than the arithmetic implies.

Halving the weight bytes of the heaviest tensor group returns 1.15–1.34×, not 2×. Normalized against the theoretical maximum for a 2× byte reduction, that is S ≈ 0.24–0.26 — a quarter of the available speedup. The composite the author builds makes the asymmetry visible in one line: at α=0.5 on Q8 Gemma 3 1B the score is 0.497, of which the quality component contributes 0.808 and the speed component 0.187. The quality is nearly intact and the speed is nearly absent.

This is the small-model, single-card face of low-precision-stops-paying-when-the-accelerator-outruns-its-bandwidth, reached from the opposite direction: there the accelerator outruns its memory system, here the model is small enough that the weight-read term is only a fraction of a token's cost and shrinking it cannot buy more than that fraction. Both say the same thing about how this vault should read a quantization result — the bit-width tells you what fits, and nothing at all about what it will feel like.

The second contribution is that the shortfall is predictable without running the model. For a bandwidth-bound decode, t_FFN = W_FFN / BW with W_FFN = 3·H·I·2 bytes at F16; at Q8 the bytes halve, so bandwidth cancels and the analytical saving is a function of architecture alone. Anchoring on a single real F16 llama-bench measurement — which absorbs scheduler, CUDA-graph and dispatch overhead the roofline does not model — and subtracting the predicted saving gives per-model errors of 0.7% / 3.3% / 6.3% across Gemma 3 1B-it, LLaMA 3.2 1B and Qwen 2.5 1.5B, mean 3.4%. That is a genuinely useful procedure for a reader deciding whether a quantization run is worth the evening: one baseline benchmark plus arithmetic tells you the ceiling before you convert anything.

Two structural details worth carrying separately.

The realized fraction is a per-model number, not a constant. 1.175× on Gemma 3 1B-it against 1.344× on Qwen 2.5 1.5B, from the same halving — because per-block FFN weight volume and block count differ (Gemma 26 layers at 149.4 µs/block F16; Qwen 28 at 258.3). The recurring vault lesson applies again: size the expectation from the model's own architecture, never from a swept number on a different one.

Layer-wise heterogeneity is offered as a prediction and should be read as one. Keeping the middle 10 layers at Q8 (the SQNR-sensitive band) and the outer 16 at Q4 predicts η ≈ 1.254 against 1.183 for uniform Q8 — about +6%. The author states plainly that this was never measured, that Q4-versus-Q8 dequantization overhead may differ in ways the roofline ignores, and that verifying it needs graph-level control unavailable for Gemma 3 in this study. Six percent is also small enough that the unmodeled overhead could plausibly consume it.

Why this is graded T3. Single author, no venue, no peer review, no code, three sub-2B models on one Colab T4. The abstract claims better allocation than evolutionary search, specialized accelerators and Shapley-value approaches, and no head-to-head against any of them is run — the comparison is argued from cost, not measured. The SQNR quality side is an internal proxy validated against nothing external, and its two normalized values (0.819, 0.796) are close enough that the composite is effectively driven by the speed term alone. What survives the grade is the profiling table, the roofline procedure and its measured error, all stated in enough detail to re-run.
