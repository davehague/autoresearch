A weight merge is the baseline multi-teacher distillation must beat

distillation-small-models's build-path strand holds that deriving a small model from a large one beats training it small — strong-to-weak-distillation-is-the-small-model-build-path, replicated on several instruments. Multi-teacher consolidation is a different question wearing the same word: not compressing a parent, but merging peers of the same size into one deployable model.

On this benchmark, the distillation answer loses to the free one.

The results table, ranked by recovery of the gap between mixed-domain SFT and an oracle router:

  • ParamMerge-TA (task-arithmetic weight merging, no training at all) — 71.7%
  • mixed-domain RL — 49.3%
  • naive multi-teacher on-policy distillation — 35.6%
  • ParamMerge-Avg (simple weight averaging) — 32.9%
  • rejection fine-tuning — 12.6%

And after the paper's three repairs, full Open-MOPD reaches 83.4%. That is a real improvement over the merge — but the margin is 0.78 total points, bought with 300 steps of three-teacher on-policy RL on 8×A100-80GB, on top of having separately RL-trained the three experts first.

The paper reports this and moves on without comment. It should not be moved on from, because it reorders the map's practical advice on this question. Two things follow.

Naive multi-teacher distillation is worse than doing something simple. A practitioner who has three domain experts and wants one model, and who reaches for the obvious dense-reward consolidation, gets half the result of running a mergekit task-arithmetic command. That is a strong argument for the merge as the default first move, and for treating any multi-teacher distillation result that does not report a merge baseline as unevaluated.

And the repaired recipe's margin is thin enough to be a cost question rather than a capability one. 0.78 points for two orders of magnitude more compute is a trade most people should decline, and the honest framing of Open-MOPD's contribution is that it restores distillation to parity with a free baseline, not that it beats it decisively. The mechanisms are worth having because they diagnose a real failure (multi-teacher-distillation-fails-on-token-accounting-not-teacher-conflict); the headline recovery number is worth reading against the merge column rather than against naive distillation.

Graded suggestive and pushes-back rather than strong: this is one benchmark, one 3B student, one three-domain split, and merging is known to be sensitive to how far the experts have drifted from their shared initialisation — all three here start from the same mixed-SFT checkpoint and train for a bounded RL stage, which is close to the best case for weight arithmetic. Experts fine-tuned longer, or from different bases, would not merge this well. But that scoping cuts both ways: the case where merging works is also the common one, since most people building multi-expert systems fine-tune from a shared base.
