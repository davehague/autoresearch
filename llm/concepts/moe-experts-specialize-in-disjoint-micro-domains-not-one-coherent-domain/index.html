Why expert routing has resisted interpretation: not because specialization is absent, but because it is polysemantic at the expert level the same way individual neurons are polysemantic

This is the map's first entry addressed to interpretability of routing rather than its training dynamics, stability, or serving cost — distinct from the router-as-training-handle use in router-outputs-as-cross-lingual-alignment-target (2026-10-04), which trains against the router's output distribution without asking what that distribution means. RouterInterp asks directly: when an expert fires, what unifies the inputs that triggered it? The answer (a disjoint union of unrelated micro-domain features, not one coherent domain) explains a standing puzzle in the broader MoE interpretability literature — why prior work "analysing routing via token co-occurrence statistics" (the paper's own framing of Jiang et al. 2024, Lewis et al. 2021, Zoph et al. 2022) has struggled to recover clean, human-interpretable patterns: those methods implicitly assume the Domain Specialisation Hypothesis and look for one coherent explanation per expert, which this paper's evidence says does not exist to find.

Why graded open-question rather than confirms/extends. This map has no prior entry that directly addresses what an expert's activation means semantically — DivMoE's domain-specialized initialization (ingested same run, divmoe-fixes-fine-grained-upcycling-collapse-with-domain-specialized-experts-and-diversity-constrained-routing) is adjacent but orthogonal: DivMoE constructs experts to start from known domains and then forces diversity in what they end up being routed; RouterInterp measures, post hoc, what an ordinarily-trained expert's specialization actually looks like, on different models (OLMoE-1B-7B, gpt-oss-20b) than DivMoE's. The two findings are not in tension — DivMoE's experts are engineered to span multiple domains per the diversity constraint, which is broadly consistent with SSH's prediction that useful experts are polysemantic rather than monosemantic — but neither paper tests the other's object, so this is recorded as an open question the map should watch rather than a settled cross-link. The SSH finding is itself a measurement with an interpretation attached (the authors' own two theoretical arguments — interference minimization and load-balancing pressure — for why superposition would be favored), not a mechanism this vault has independently verified.

What's novel for this map. The paper's own baseline comparison places this result against the field's standing (apparently unsuccessful) routing-interpretability line, and the RouterInterp method itself (SAE features, contrastively grouped, LLM-synthesized explanations) is a concrete instrument this map did not previously have: a way to score whether a natural-language account of expert routing is accurate (F1 against held-out activation windows), rather than relying on qualitative inspection.

Caveats carried from the source. G(E_i) is measured at one layer per model for the DSH/SSH cluster-count test specifically (gpt-oss-20b layer 20, OLMoE-1B-7B layer 15), not swept across all tested layers. High G could in principle reflect inefficient or inexplicable routing rather than genuine superposed specialization; the authors argue against this via model performance and RouterInterp's own interpretable structure, but this is inferential, not a direct ablation. RouterInterp's advantage is substantially larger on OLMoE-1B-7B than gpt-oss-20b, attributed to SAE quality differences (32K vs 131K latents, better reconstruction on OLMoE). Evaluated on two MoE architectures only; ICML 2026 (TU Wien / UK AI Security Institute), code released per the source.
