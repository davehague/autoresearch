The bits should not be spread evenly, and the weights say where to put them

mixture-of-experts has held MoE × quantization as a gap with zero sources since coining. The premise for closing it is one this vault reached from the opposite direction three days earlier: moe-inference-cost-tracks-total-not-active-params-on-edge measured that on bandwidth-bound hardware the bill tracks total parameters because every expert must be resident. If they all must be resident and most are idle for any given token, the obvious question is whether they should all cost the same number of bits. They should not.

The importance signal is the interesting part because it needs no data at all. Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization theory holds that a well-trained weight matrix develops a heavy-tailed eigenvalue spectrum, and that the tail's shape predicts quality without access to training or test data. Fitting a power law p(λ) ∝ λ^(−α) to the tail and estimating α with a Hill estimator gives a globally-comparable score per layer: heavier tail → better trained → more bits. Allocation is then a Multiple-Choice Knapsack problem, solved to global optimality in seconds.

Three heterogeneities fall out, and the third is the one with predictive value:

  1. Within an expert, the up, gate and down projections score differently — so the natural unit of allocation is the layer, not the expert. That is a quiet echo of experts-specialize-by-syntax-not-semantics: there is no math expert to extract, and no math expert to protect either.
  2. Across blocks, distributions differ, so any fixed per-block budget is leaving quality on the table and the budget must be global.
  3. Across architectures, vanilla MoEs with few large experts (Mixtral) show small within-block variance while fine-grained MoEs with many small experts (DeepSeekV2-Lite, Qwen1.5-MoE) show much larger variance. Mixed precision therefore has more to exploit exactly where fine-grained-plus-shared-experts-reduce-expert-redundancy says the architecture is heading — and the 2.5-bit numbers confirm the prediction, with Qwen1.5-MoE gaining 13.2 points over uniform against Mixtral's 2.9.

The number to carry is Qwen1.5-MoE at 3.5 bits matching BF16 at over 4× compression. That is the most useful datum available for fitting an open MoE onto one card, and it sits comfortably beside the-gguf-quant-ladder-is-graded-not-a-cliff's dense-model finding that 4-bit is nearly free — with the refinement that on an MoE the average can go below 4 if the allocation is uneven.

Bound: weight-only and memory-only. No latency, throughput or energy figure appears anywhere in the results, so this closes MoE × quantization for footprint and leaves it open for speed — which matters because quantization-speeds-decode-and-can-slow-prefill shows those move independently. The five evaluation benchmarks are commonsense zero-shot tasks, precisely the family this vault has measured as least sensitive to quantization damage; GSM8K and MMLU appear only in the motivating figure, never in the main table, so "matches BF16" is established on the easy end of the ladder. Several method hyperparameters (γ, k, λ_min, the FARMS window, the {1,2,3,4} candidate set) are tuned by prior heuristics on a method sold as assumption-free, no head-to-head against AWQ exists, and the paper is a venue-less June 2026 preprint with no third-party reproduction.

Rebutted by: expert-bit-allocation-should-be-global-not-per-layer — contradicts finding (1) and confirms finding (2). An ICML 2026 result measures expert sensitivity as the trace of the empirical Fisher information matrix and reports that it varies substantially within a layer as well as across layers, so "the natural unit of allocation is the layer" does not hold; a global integer program over every expert beats the layer-local one in all twelve of its model-budget cells on perplexity. The disagreement is about the allocation unit only — both sources agree the scope must be global and that fine-grained MoEs have more to exploit than coarse ones. Two caveats keep this from being a clean overturn: the accuracy advantage is architecture-dependent (on Mixtral at 2.5 bits, uniform allocation posts the best zero-shot of the three), and the global method needs 128 calibration sequences, giving up exactly the calibration independence that is this note's most distinctive property. Also rebutted on the closing bound: the footprint-only limit is lifted by mixed-precision-moe-finally-has-a-throughput-number — 1.4–1.6× faster decode at batch size 1, Mixtral-8×7B at 12.0 GB and 88.6 tok/s.
