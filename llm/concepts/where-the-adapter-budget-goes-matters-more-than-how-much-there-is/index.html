A fourth knob on an adapter

This vault has been arguing about low-rank adaptation as though it had one dial. It has four, and only one of them has been varied in any in-vault comparison of adapters against full fine-tuning.

  • Rank — how much capacity the update holds. The dial everyone turns.
  • Initialization — where the adapter starts. adapter-initialization-determines-the-lora-ceiling (principal singular components of the base) and quantization-aware-init-extends-lora-below-the-4-bit-floor (the quantization error) disagree about which subspace, and agree it is load-bearing.
  • Scaling — the divisor applied to the adapter product. the-lora-scaling-factor-collapses-gradients-at-high-rank proves the field's default is wrong and the error grows with rank.
  • Placement — which of the model's weight matrices get the budget at all. This one.

The measurement is a single controlled sweep and it is larger than most effects on this map. Total trainable parameters pinned at 0.28M on a 183M-parameter encoder, MNLI-matched accuracy. Restrict the adapter to one weight type per layer and the two feed-forward matrices come out on top (89.99 and 89.91) with the query projection last (88.58). Restrict it to a band of layers instead and the top three score 88.6 against the bottom three's 77.87 — a 10.7-point spread from nothing but where the same budget was spent.

The practical consequence is that LoRA's usual configuration — one rank, applied uniformly to a chosen set of modules — is not a neutral default. It is a bet that importance is flat across depth and across module type, and this measurement says it is not, by a wide margin. Two follow-on readings:

The "which modules to target" question has an evidence-backed answer for the first time. The practice of adapting attention projections comes from the original LoRA paper, which targeted W_q and W_v. On this benchmark those are the worst two places to put a fixed budget. Applying adapters to every linear submodule, which is what current tooling defaults to, is a way of avoiding the choice rather than making it well.

Learned allocation recovers part of the spread, but less than the spread implies. Scoring each rank-one component by a smoothed gradient-weight sensitivity and pruning the weak ones to a decaying budget is worth roughly a GLUE point over uniform LoRA at matched parameters (89.31 vs 88.34), and more as the budget tightens — at 0.08% of full-fine-tuning parameters it reads 85.6/88.7 EM/F1 on SQuADv2.0 against LoRA's 84.7/87.5, which is also above full fine-tuning's 85.4/88.4. The gap between "10.7 points available from placement" and "1 point recovered by allocating automatically" is the honest headline: the sensitivity signal finds some of the structure, not most of it.

Two things bound this hard. Everything is encoder-scale and task-adaptation-shaped — a 183M encoder and an encoder-decoder summarizer, on GLUE, SQuAD and XSum. There is no decoder-only model, no instruction tuning, and no domain acquisition, which is the axis peft-methods is actually contested on. And the method inherits LoRA's alpha/r scaling by its own statement, which the-lora-scaling-factor-collapses-gradients-at-high-rank shows damps an adapter harder the more rank it holds — so an allocator that promotes a matrix is simultaneously shrinking that matrix's contribution. That is this vault's inference, not either paper's claim, and it means the reported gains are most likely a floor on what placement is worth.

Rebutted by: peft-does-not-reliably-reduce-peak-memory — extends this to a second axis the source never measured: placement decides peak training memory as well as accuracy, with a 2.6× inversion at matched batch size when trainable parameters sit in the earliest layers. Two groups, two axes, one variable, and no source reports both together — so the 10.7-point accuracy spread may be paired with a memory spread nobody has priced.
