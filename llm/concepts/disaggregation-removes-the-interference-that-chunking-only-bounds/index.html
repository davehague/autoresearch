Two cures for one interference, and they spend different resources

prefill-saturates-compute-far-below-a-full-prompt establishes the fact both cures respond to: prefill is compute-bound and saturates a GPU at around 512 tokens, decode is memory-bound and has arithmetic slack, and putting them in the same batch means the compute-bound one freezes the memory-bound one. chunking-the-prompt-makes-admission-latency-free is the first cure — split the prompt across iterations and fill each iteration with running decodes first, so nothing waits behind a whole prompt. This is the second: stop mixing them at all. Prefill instances and decode instances run on different GPUs, and a completed prefill ships its KV cache across the interconnect to whichever decode instance will continue it.

The head-to-head exists because one of the disaggregation paper's two baselines, DeepSpeed-MII, implements chunked prefill to a fixed token budget. Disaggregation sustains 1.6×–7.4× its request rate at 90% SLO attainment across three workloads. Read that with the partisanship discount — it is the disaggregation authors running it, and DeepSpeed-MII is not the strongest chunking implementation — but the mechanism critique underneath does not depend on who ran the experiment, and it is a dilemma rather than an overhead:

  • Set the chunk below the size that saturates the GPU and the prefill takes longer than it needed to, because it is now sharing the device rather than owning it.
  • Set the chunk at or above saturation and there is no room left in the iteration for decode tokens to ride along, which was the entire point.
  • Either way, chunk i must re-read the KV of all i−1 earlier chunks, so an N-chunk prefill performs O(N²) cache loads against O(N) unchunked — a cost the chunking source measured on itself (~25% extra prefill work at chunk 512, near zero at 2048) and accepted.

Separation has no such dilemma, and it pays a different bill instead: the KV cache has to move. Priced, that is 1.13 GB for a 512-token request on a 66B model, or ~90 Gbps at 10 requests/second. Over intra-node NVLink at 600 GB/s this vanishes into under 0.1% of request latency, which is why the system's placement algorithm exists specifically to keep corresponding prefill and decode segments on the same node. Over the same testbed's 25 Gbps cross-node link it does not vanish, and the constrained placement algorithm is the one used for nearly every experiment — the fifth appearance of this vault's recurring rule that a distributed technique is decided by the ratio of compute to interconnect.

The scope disclaimer is the authors' own and it is unusually direct: in "environments with only a few or even a single GPU, the design space… is significantly limited. It struggles and even fails." Disaggregation's whole mechanism is allocating different GPU counts and parallelism plans to two phases; one card has nothing to allocate. The same discussion concedes the other direction — where latency is not a constraint and only throughput matters, "chunked-prefill with piggyback may be preferred," because it keeps every iteration at the compute-bound threshold.

So the rule the pair yields, which neither paper states alone: disaggregation is for meeting two latency targets under concurrency; chunking is for keeping one device busy. A local single-user stack has one device and one target, and inherits the second answer.
