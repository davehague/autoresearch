Diffusion decoding's sampled regime gets a second trained, frozen-backbone scheduling controller — this one solving extension-length and early-finalization jointly, scoped to agentic tool use rather than general decoding

TL;DR: This map's only prior serving-side accelerator within the sampled regime, Early-Bird Decoding (2026-09-16), is a frozen-weight plug-in that groups tokens into variable-length blocks by uncertainty and reaches 3.53x-18.76x wall-clock speedup at roughly ±1-point accuracy on three dLLMs and four general benchmarks. Metacognitive Decoding is a close structural cousin — another small trained network sitting on top of a frozen diffusion backbone, deciding a decoding-schedule knob the fixed defaults waste computation on — but it differs in three ways that matter for how this map should read it: the controller is trained (via GRPO on trajectory-level reward) rather than reused frozen-weight, it controls two knobs jointly (how far to extend the response, not just when to finalize already-generated tokens), and it is evaluated exclusively on agentic tool-use and multi-hop-QA benchmarks rather than general-purpose decoding. It reports FLOPs reductions, not wall-clock throughput, which is a different and weaker currency than every other sampled-regime source this map holds.

What it says. The flux policy moves a per-block "active range" marker that bounds which positions are actively denoised; it can grow the range when a response needs more room but never shrinks it within a block — this is the extension-length knob diffusion LMs have that autoregressive decoders don't need (a diffusion block pre-allocates a canvas; the policy decides how much of it to actually use). The confidence-alignment policy decides which tokens to finalize at each step: it accepts whole recurring structural spans (e.g., tool-call syntax templates) as a group once a span-level score clears a threshold, and separately adjusts each individual token's base confidence threshold by a learned per-position offset. Both policies are driven by one small (two-layer Transformer) controller trained on-policy with GRPO: group-standardized trajectory advantages, reward [max(0,1-e/η)]^α for correct trajectories (zero for incorrect ones) where e is relative inference cost and η/α are unreported tuning constants. Training uses BFCL (non-live/live, 1:1 split) plus 1,000 questions each from HotpotQA/2WikiMultiHopQA/MuSiQue, 40 rounds per stage on 8×A100.

On the primary backbone (LLaDA-8B, SFT-adapted), the full controller beats the uncontrolled backbone on accuracy on four of five benchmarks (clearest on BFCL Live: +4.44 points) while cutting FLOPs 15.2-43.1% depending on benchmark. Ablating either policy independently costs FLOPs (flux ablation: +24.5% FLOPs on average; confidence-alignment ablation: +16.3%) without a consistent accuracy cost — several ablated configurations score marginally higher than the full controller on individual benchmarks, meaning the accuracy headroom these two policies buy is not where their FLOPs savings come from; the two effects are somewhat decoupled. A second backbone, Dream-7B, reproduces the FLOPs-saving mechanism but with a different cost profile: the two benchmarks with the largest FLOPs cuts (2Wiki 58.0%, MuSiQue 67.5%) are also the two with accuracy losses (−0.60, −2.20), the reverse of the LLaDA-8B pattern where the biggest FLOPs win (BFCL Live, −43.1%) came with the biggest accuracy gain. That reversal is the paper's most useful unremarked result: the cost/accuracy trade this controller learns is backbone-dependent, not a fixed property of the method, and nothing in the paper explains why.

Caveats. Every efficiency number is FLOPs; there is no wall-clock, throughput, batch-size, or hardware figure anywhere in the readable text, so it is unknown whether these savings survive contact with an actual decoding loop's real bottlenecks (this map's open question about consumer-hardware cost is untouched, and so is the general question of whether FLOPs reduction tracks measured speedup at all on diffusion decoders, which the map's other sources have found to be non-obvious). The abstract's "20.6% average FLOPs reduction" is quoted against "the most efficient baseline" per benchmark — which on some benchmarks is a stronger comparator (LLaDA2.0-mini) than the uncontrolled same-backbone figures reported above — and the paper's own aggregation does not reconcile with a plain unweighted mean of the numbers this ingest could extract (~29%), so the headline number should be read cautiously. Evaluation is agentic-only (tool-calling and multi-hop QA); no general diffusion-decoding benchmark is run, so this cannot be read as a general-purpose alternative to Early-Bird Decoding or the map's other sampled-regime sources — it answers a narrower question (agent decoding efficiency) than it is filed under here. No composition or comparison with Early-Bird Decoding's block-grouping router is attempted by either source. Code is stated as released on GitHub; no model/controller checkpoint release is mentioned. Single paper, no independent replication, reward hyperparameters unreported in the available text.
