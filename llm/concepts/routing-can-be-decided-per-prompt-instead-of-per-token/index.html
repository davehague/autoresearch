Route the prompt, not the token

Every mixture-of-experts result in this vault treats the routing decision as a per-token function of the hidden state, because that is what the architecture defines. experts-specialize-by-syntax-not-semantics and expert-routing-is-predictable-one-layer-ahead both take it as given and ask what the router does; ask-the-cache-not-the-router-when-experts-are-offloaded works around it by filtering the router's answer against residency. This source asks a different question: must the routing granularity be the token?

In prefill, it argues no. A small bidirectional encoder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, negligible cost against the target model) scores prompt tokens by CLS attention weight. The major tokens route at the model's original budget and their union becomes a shared expert set. Minor and trivial tokens get progressively more candidates but fewer activations, and are steered to reuse experts already in the shared set before activating a new one. The result is a 55.7% cut in prefill expert traffic at under 1% accuracy loss, with worst-case degradation of 1.0–3.1% across five benchmarks.

Two details are worth keeping. The encoder is deliberately bidirectional — the target model's own causal attention was a worse importance estimator, because a prompt token's importance depends on what follows it. And in decode the same idea reappears indexed by depth rather than by identity: candidates at the same draft depth co-activate 3+ of 8 experts 88.4% of the time and are mutually exclusive (at most one survives), so loading the union of their salient channels once and letting every candidate compute on all of it trades arithmetic — which is free here — for memory traffic, which is not.

Three things this means for mixture-of-experts.

It is a new lever, not a variation on the two the map holds. DraftExpert buys acceptance in resident parameters; AcceptMoE shrinks what the verifier may touch. Both take routing as given and manage its consequences. This changes the routing function itself, and it is the only one of the three that also attacks prefill, which the other two leave untouched.

Nothing about the prefill half requires custom silicon. A MiniLM encoder, a three-way token split and a shared expert set are ordinary software. local-inference measured expert loading at 87% of profiled decode time on an RTX 4090 and 49% on a Snapdragon NPU under llama.cpp — and no in-vault source has tried prompt-wise reuse on either. That is a cheap, unrun, directly premise-relevant experiment.

And it revisits an old finding from an unexpected angle. If experts specialize by syntax rather than semantics, a shared expert set derived from a prompt's semantically important tokens has no obvious reason to cover the routes its unimportant tokens need — and yet it does, at 1–3% cost. Either syntactic routes are far more concentrated within a prompt than the specialization result implies, or the reduced budget is doing more work than the shared set is. The paper does not separate them.

Graded suggestive: the 55.7% and the accuracy costs are measured, but the end-to-end latency and energy headlines come from an instruction-level simulator plus synthesis rather than from silicon, and the routing budgets need per-model calibration.
