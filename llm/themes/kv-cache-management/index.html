KV cache management (eviction, reconstruction, depth, reuse) — map

Coined 2026-08-03 (auto run) — three sources landed in one night on the same object, and the object had no home. The KV cache has been the vault's most-referenced un-mapped thing: it appears as a component on transformer-architecture (grouped-query attention shrinks it), as a format on post-training-quantization (2-bit KV, data-free rotation), as a serving structure on local-inference (paged allocation, prefix reuse, eviction economics), and as the bill on context-extension (extend the window and the cache grows by the same factor). Four maps hold pieces of one budget, and none of them can state the trade between the pieces. Auto-coined at emerging; flagged for David's review — the alternative was folding tonight's arrivals into local-inference, which is already 1,000 lines and whose own sub-thread declaration names "KV-cache budget" as a sub-item of on-device inference. The case for a map is that the question is not device-shaped: an eviction policy, a residual reconstruction and a depth-partitioned store are answers to the same question — given a fixed budget, what do you keep and in what form? — and they trade against each other in ways no serving map is organized to compare.

Status: emerging Status note (2026-08-03, at coining): emerging, deliberately. Five findings from three T1 preprints, all from the same fortnight, none replicated by anyone. Two of the three have no code link. Every measurement is on one datacenter card — an A100-40GB, an A40-48GB and a 96 GB H20 — at batch size 1, with no quantized base anywhere, so the compounding question this map exists to answer is untouched by its own founding sources. What it does have is coverage of a genuinely distinct axis and, unusually for a new map, one result that pushes back on the premise the other four share.

What we believe now:

  • What eviction deletes is a pair of sums, not a set of tokens — and a few slots of the same budget can keep them. Write attention with the normalization opened up: o = N_S(q)/Z_S(q), with Z_T = Σ_{p∈T} e^{a_p} and N_T = Σ_{p∈T} e^{a_p} v_p. Hard eviction drops (N_E, Z_E) for the evicted set E and renormalizes over what remains. So the loss is algebraic, and sums are approximable even when their terms are not storable: partition E in key space and keep, per group, a mean key, a mean value and a population count c_j, entering the same softmax through ā_j = ⟨q,k̄_j⟩/√d + log c_j + log g(q). Improves all 32 displayed LongBench configurations (+1.02 avg) and 63 of 64 RULER configurations (+3.38 avg) at identical retained budget, with the largest gains at the tightest budgets (+3.47 and +3.66 at 10% and 20% retained). The shared softmax is load-bearing — separate normalizations cost 3.00 points on RepoBench-P — and hard eviction is recovered exactly at zero residual budget, so the method strictly generalizes the family it beats. Gains concentrate on distributed-evidence tasks (variable tracking, multi-key retrieval, FWE, repository code), which is where aggregate mass is the missing signal and a small exact cache necessarily fails. [T1] See what-eviction-deletes-is-two-sums-not-a-set-of-tokens.
  • A KV-compression number is a claim about when you compressed, and the two settings differ by more than every method in the comparison. Query-aware construction appends the downstream question to the context before compressing; query-agnostic compresses the reusable prefix before the query is known. Same method, model, budget and benchmark: 72.20 against 26.92 on 32K RULER at 10% retained. The second setting is the one that describes deployment — a prefix cache is built once, before the queries that will hit it — and it is also the more discriminative: method differences are roughly double there (+4.54 against +2.22). Operating rule, the same shape as evaluation-contamination's recipe-leakage mode: a KV-compression result absent its construction setting is uninformative, and a deployment decision should be made on the query-agnostic number or not at all. [T1] See cache-compression-must-be-scored-query-agnostic.
  • The cache has a second axis, and it is cheaper than the one everyone compresses. The cache is tokens × layers, and every method above operates on tokens. Cache instead one intermediate residual tensor per token at a mid-depth split, retrieve a bounded set of chunks per query, and recompute only the upper layers over the retrieved pack. On Qwen3-8B at j=12 of 36 the stored tensor is 1/18 the bytes of full bf16 KV (8 KB vs 144 KB), and — the structural claim — at fixed retrieval breadth the model-side read is independent of stored-context length, roughly 6.7k tokens whether the store holds 8k or 128k. Measured at 128k: 18.26 GB against a full context's 89.36 GB, 7.83× prefill, with decode roughly a wash (0.89–1.07× of dense). Three controls make it non-obvious: retrieved chunks must attend to each other (block-diagonal reuse costs 36/62 points on multi-key retrieval), more retrieval is not better (a 17k pack loses to a 6.7k one from 16k onward), and depth is a quality-versus-query-cost knob rather than a free win. Two honest limits: the backbone is not position-extended, so the comparison is against a model past its own window rather than against a YaRN-enabled one, and the flagship configuration needs 4,000 self-distillation steps on 8 GPUs. [T1, one study] See the-kv-cache-can-be-compressed-along-depth-not-tokens.
  • PUSHES BACK — on retrieval-shaped work the bottleneck is choosing what to read, not preserving it faithfully. Run the selector as an isolated variable and, on a controlled single-needle sweep, an oracle and plain BM25 both hold 100 at 8k, 16k and 32k while recency falls to ~64 and reader-attention to ~78. Nearly every method on this map — and every eviction method in-vault — selects using an attention-derived signal, on the premise that the model's own attention says what matters. On this diagnostic it does not, and a lexical retriever from 2009 ties an oracle. The two results are compatible and the boundary is legible: when the evidence is one identifiable span, selection binds and lexical matching finds it; when it is diffuse across many low-scoring positions, there is nothing to select and aggregate fidelity is what is missing. Scope hard — RULER is synthetic and single-needle retrieval is the case most favourable to surface-form matching, the authors say so, and the comparison is not like-for-like (chunk selection from an external store has the raw text available; token scoring inside a resident cache does not). [T1, one study] See selection-not-cached-state-fidelity-is-the-long-range-bottleneck.
  • A cache you sparsify but never evict from turns the model into its own drafter, for free. Score historical positions per layer and head from the attention statistics the previous verification pass already materialized — no extra forward pass — draft over the sparse view, then verify over the complete cache, which preserves the output distribution exactly. 72.9% acceptance, 11.26 accepted tokens per step, 2.79× on long-context work where a downloaded trained drafter reads 1.95% and 0.89× — slower than not speculating. Because the dense tensors stay resident the index is a hypothesis, rebuilt every step, so nothing is irreversibly lost; that is the opposite trade from everything else on this map, and it means this is not a memory technique at all. It spends a cache you already paid for to buy latency, which makes it complementary to eviction and quantization rather than competing with them — and nobody has run it on top of either. [T1] See a-recallable-sparse-cache-is-a-free-drafter, a-downloaded-drafter-is-a-bet-on-its-training-distribution.

What the map inherits from its four parents is a set of findings it does not restate: the cache is the memory term that binds first on one card (local-inference), a data-free rotation puts it at 3.5 bits (post-training-quantization), sharing KV heads buys decode speed at near-zero quality cost (transformer-architecture), and extending the window multiplies the whole bill (context-extension).

Open questions:

  • Nothing here composes with anything, and composition is the entire point of the map. Five levers now act on one budget — eviction, residual reconstruction, KV quantization, depth partitioning, and sparse-view drafting — and no source measures any two of them together. The two obvious pairs are cheap and decision-relevant: residual reconstruction on top of a 2-bit cache (does the population count survive quantization? do the two errors add or cancel?), and depth-axis caching combined with token-axis eviction, which attack orthogonal dimensions of the same tensor and should multiply. Until someone runs a pair, this map is a list rather than a budget.
  • Every measurement is on a datacenter card at batch size 1. An A100-40GB, an A40-48GB, a 96 GB H20. The regime this vault's premise cares about — a 12–24 GB consumer card, where the cache is the term that actually forces a decision — has no number in any of these sources, and the 2026-07-31 eviction ingest already showed why that matters: at a small cache on a 7B model, eviction's measured benefit on an RTX 4090 was a 4% speedup at higher peak memory, because the cache was a rounding error beside the weights. The crossover where the cache dominates has still never been demonstrated on consumer hardware.
  • Is the selector finding real outside synthetic retrieval? BM25 tying an oracle is the most disruptive result on this map and the most fragile: needle-in-a-haystack inserts literal text, so surface-form matching is near-optimal by construction, and the source's own limitations flag exactly that. The experiment that would settle it is cheap — use a lexical or dense retriever as the scoring signal for token-axis eviction, against the attention scores every method uses, on a benchmark where the query and the evidence do not share vocabulary.
  • Nobody refreshes. Both compression methods here construct once after prefill and never update: the residual entries are fixed, and the depth-partitioned store is written per chunk and read thereafter. That matches reusable prefix-cache serving and mismatches generation-heavy workloads — reasoning traces, agentic loops — which are exactly the workloads long context is being bought for. The sparse-view drafter is the one entry that rebuilds every step, and it does so only because it never evicted anything.
  • What does the budget look like as a curve? Every result here is reported at a handful of retention ratios against a full-cache reference, and the honest reading of the best numbers is sobering — at 10% retained on 32K, the best query-agnostic score is 32.74 against an uncompressed 88.57. Nobody plots quality against cache bytes across methods on one axis, which is the single figure a practitioner choosing a budget needs and which every one of these papers has the data to draw.

See also:

  • local-inference — the parent for serving: paged allocation, prefix reuse, and the currency test that prices every local lever against the others. The eviction economics measured there on an RTX 4090 are this map's only consumer-hardware data point, and they are discouraging at small scale.
  • context-extension — the demand side. Extending a window multiplies this budget by the same factor, which is why that map's "cheap extension" claim and this map's compression ratios are two halves of one question nobody has costed end to end.
  • post-training-quantization — the fourth lever on the same budget, and the one with the most mature evidence: 2-bit KV via data-free rotation. It is also the lever most obviously composable with everything here and least obviously tested against it.
  • transformer-architecture — where the cache comes from, and where the architectural answer lives (grouped-query attention shrinks it by construction; a recurrent state removes it). semantic-content-is-assembled-by-mid-depth is the finding that makes the depth axis available at all.
  • evaluation-contamination — the query-aware/query-agnostic gap is a preparation-protocol failure of exactly the shape that map generalized on 2026-07-30: a comparison absent its protocol is uninformative.
