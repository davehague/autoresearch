What we believe now:

  • A local-inference number is a measurement of a runtime configuration as much as of a method, and the configuration terms can be larger than the method terms (new 2026-08-06). This map is built on comparisons: llama.cpp against vLLM, MLX against llama.cpp, a K-quant ladder against itself, an MoE against a dense model at matched active parameters. A year of Arm CPU optimization work says the floor under those comparisons is softer than assumed — the largest single win was replacing the process memory allocator (mimalloc for glibc malloc in PyTorch: 2.3× offline throughput, ~7× at low concurrency on Llama 3.1 8B), against 9% for an LSE-atomics OpenMP runtime, 16%/60% TPOT for oneDNN BF16 weight prepacking at warmup, and 12% end-to-end for BFMMLA paged-attention kernels with a vectorized softmax exponential. The rule is the same shape as perplexity-does-not-rank-quantization-formats and the query-agnostic construction rule on kv-cache-management: a runtime comparison absent its runtime configuration is uninformative, and no local-inference source in this vault reports its allocator. The pattern of the wins does corroborate the map's decode accounting from an unusual side — every one of them is largest at low concurrency, which is the single-stream regime where nothing hides memory-system latency. Separately, the shipped CPU quantization ladder is W8A8 then W4A8 (per-token activations, channelwise weights, SMMLA and KleidiAI INT4 micro-kernels) at up to 6.2× throughput and 7.8× TPOT over an October 2025 BF16 baseline — a format family llama.cpp cannot express, so this is a third measured local backend rather than an improvement to either existing one, with no quality number attached to any rung. [T2, one vendor post] See the-cpu-inference-bottleneck-was-the-memory-allocator, four-bit-weights-eight-bit-activations-is-the-arm-cpu-rung.
  • Linear-time sequence models are not a drop-in efficiency substitute at small scale on one card (new 2026-08-06, and it is the map's first evidence about a non-transformer family running locally). Measured on an RTX 4090 across six tasks: Mamba-1.4B takes 49.4 min on SST-2 where the larger Qwen3-1.7B takes 22.3, and 147.9 against 104.2 on QNLI, at roughly 18% fewer TFLOPs; inference is worse (484 s vs 75 s for TinyLlama-1.1B on the generative personalization task). At 128–512 token sequences the quadratic attention term never dominates, so there is nothing for linear scaling to beat, while the sequential selective scan is memory-access-bound and cannot use tensor cores as well as dense matmuls; Mamba-2's matmul-based scan recovers part of it (31.7 vs 49.4 min) and not on the generative tasks. The binding fact for this vault is the second one: there is no 4-bit path — no available library supports native quantization for these models, attributed to selective-scan feature-map sensitivity and activation outliers absent from attention modules, which is activation-outliers-are-the-barrier-to-w8a8 in an unfamiliar place where the standard remedy (rotating-a-transformer-deletes-its-outliers-for-free) has never been tried. The counterweight, and it is real: peak VRAM does not follow parameter count — Mamba-2-1.3B uses 7,680 MB where Qwen3-1.7B uses 19,098, and on STS-B the smallest model in the study peaks highest at 24,209 MB. So in a hard memory ceiling an SSM can be the model that fits; it will cost more time and energy, and you cannot quantize it. Every number here is a claim about kernels and libraries in 2026-08, which the authors flag and this vault expects to expire. [T1] See state-space-models-have-no-quantized-peft-path.
  • The currency test has its first hard boundary: on an offloaded mixture-of-experts model the scarce resource is expert movement, and speculative decoding's cost model breaks against it (new 2026-08-02). This map holds that single-stream local decode is bandwidth-bound and that its levers mostly contend for the same idle arithmetic (speculation-and-quantization-compete-for-the-same-headroom). Expert offload is the case where the bandwidth is not to VRAM but across PCIe or from Flash, and the accounting changes shape. On DeepSeek-V2-Lite, routed experts are 91.6% of the weights and every token touches top-6 of 64, so keeping the small resident part on the accelerator and streaming the rest is the natural deployment — and then expert loading is 87% of profiled decode time on an RTX 4090 and 49% on a Snapdragon 8 Elite NPU running llama.cpp with Q4_0 weights. Three consequences for the levers on this map. Speculation's founding assumption is dense-specific: verifying a block reuses the same FFN weights in a dense model, but in an offloaded MoE the verifier must load the union of every expert any drafted token routes to, so verification costs 2.4× a token-decode at block length 4 and 5.8× at 16 against a flat dense control — the standard advice to lengthen the speculation window is backwards here. The training-free version is a net loss: shared expert plus router top-r drafting runs at 1.19 tok/s against an unspeculated 2.19 on a 4090, because it pays expert movement for candidates that get rejected. And the fix spends a third currency — a small resident self-distilled draft expert per layer, bought in accelerator memory and offline training rather than in transfers, reaching 85% acceptance at essentially top-1 transfer cost for 1.45× average decode, output exact. That makes it the second lever this map has seen that stacks rather than contends, after feed-forward activation sparsity, and it is the first one indexed by memory hierarchy rather than by arithmetic. Bounded hard — single author, no venue, no code, two models of one MoE shape, one GPU and one phone at different precisions, and the draft experts' own footprint is never reported. [T1, discounted] See offloaded-moe-verification-is-not-one-forward-pass, a-distilled-draft-expert-buys-acceptance-without-buying-transfers.
  • The currency test makes its first positive prediction: feed-forward activation sparsity spends memory rather than arithmetic, so it stacks where speculation and quantization contend (new 2026-08-01, and it is the fourth lever on the local decode step). Feed-forward networks dominate a decode step's weight traffic; if you knew which SwiGLU intermediate channels mattered for this token you could skip reading the rest, but the signal that tells you is the dense computation you were avoiding. Prox breaks the circularity by noticing that sparse execution needs only the channel mask, and a mask is a ranking: build it from INT4 proxy weights on a magnitude-sparsified input, then compute the selected channels exactly. No training. Across ten models from six families: Qwen3-8B holds 74.6 aggregate at 50% sparsity against a dense 76.1, and at 70% reaches 1.82× decode throughput (46.7 → 85.0 tok/s) on an A6000 at batch size 1. The part that matters for this map is that it was measured in composition — on top of AWQ, FP8, W4A16 and W8A8, and on top of two sparse-attention schemes: RocketKV + Prox returns 1.92× at 16K and 2.62× at 32K at 60% FFN sparsity. That is the map's own yardstick paying off in the other direction, after two negative predictions (speculation-and-quantization-compete-for-the-same-headroom; moe-inference-cost-tracks-total-not-active-params-on-edge) — this lever buys bandwidth with memory (~12% extra weight storage for the proxies) and quality, and there is no contention for memory in the way there is for idle arithmetic. Read the quality column before adopting the headline: at 50–60% the cost is 1.5–2.8 aggregate points on the 8B/14B Qwen3 models, at 70% it is 7.5 on Qwen3-8B and 6.7 on Llama-3-8B. Bounded: single-batch decode only (the authors name large-batch serving as the limitation), thresholds calibrated on Alpaca text with domain-shift stability unexplored, six multiple-choice benchmarks plus WikiText and no generation quality, and every measurement is an A6000/A100/3090 — nothing in llama.cpp, so like AlphaQ and TurboQuant the local stack cannot express it.   • KV eviction is a quality-spending lever, and at the scale anyone has measured on a consumer card it returns 4% and costs memory (new 2026-08-01 — the map's first eviction source, and the honest read is more restrained than the abstract). The criterion is the best idea in the family: a past token that more recent tokens can already predict is redundant, scored by re-running the model over the cache in original order under a counter-causal (upper-triangular) mask so each position attends only to its future — in-distribution, tied to the cache's actual contents, no training. It beats heavy-hitter and sliding-window eviction on 3 of 4 models on MATH500 and leads every baseline on AIME at 25% retention, and it avoids the self-reinforcing bias of attention-mass scoring, where a retained token keeps accumulating attention and the policy's errors compound into degenerate output. Now apply this map's currency test. Eviction spends model quality — against the full cache the best arm still loses 5.0 points on Qwen2.5-3B, 5.6 on Qwen2.5-14B and 13.3 on AIME — and refresh compute, which the authors price honestly at "roughly doubles inference cost during pre-fill when refreshes are frequent" before a single-layer approximation cuts it 7–9× for 1.0–7.8 accuracy points. So eviction belongs in this map's ledger beside 3-bit quantization, not beside speculation-trades-arithmetic-for-latency-without-moving-the-distribution. And the efficiency table, measured on an RTX 4090, is the part to read twice: 10.2 s/sample against a full cache's 10.6 s, with peak memory 15.6–16.0 GB against 15.4 GB — higher than not compressing. Both facts are a scale artifact rather than a defect (a 512-entry cache on a 7B model is a rounding error beside the weights), which means the technique's value lives entirely in the long-context regime the source never measures: there is no memory-versus-context-length curve anywhere in it. [T1]
  • Speculative decoding's exactness is a flag, not a property — and no speed metric can tell you which side of it you are on (new 2026-07-31, and it revises the map's own yardstick rather than extending it). This map has treated speculation as the one lever that spends nothing but idle arithmetic. The "lossy verification" relaxations now shipping spend distributional fidelity: truncation-based schemes emit the draft's distribution restricted to an allowed set, collaborative ones emit a blend. The damage is under a point under plain single-draft speculation and 6.28 / 5.29 / 8.82 / 4.90 points (MATH / MBPP+ / INCLUDE / BFCL) under EAGLE-3 tree verification, average gap widening −0.32 → −6.32 — so the cost is set by a second, unrelated configuration choice. Block efficiency and tokens/second improve in every one of these configurations by construction, which makes self-benchmarking blind to the failure: the fastest cell in the study (BE 9.15) is its worst model (50.26 Pass@1 vs a lossless 75.84). Operating rule: run lossless, or report the verification rule with every acceptance-rate and throughput number. The one relaxation worth having is isolated by ablation — cap the draft only where it severely overestimates the target (≤0.7 Pass@1 for a small block-efficiency gain); the adaptive interpolation bundled with it in shipping implementations costs 25 points. [T1]
  • In a multi-turn conversation about an image, cache the encoder — not the text prefix (new 2026-07-30, and it is the first local vision-language serving measurement in the vault). A repeated 1024×1024 image query on Qwen3-VL-8B, M4 Max: 21.7 s cold → 1.15 s → 0.78 s, i.e. 28×. The ablation is the finding: vision embeddings alone are worth 7.8×, the KV cache alone 1.2×. In a text conversation prefix reuse is the lever (prefix-reuse-attacks-prefill-and-needs-no-concurrency); with an image in context it is a rounding error next to caching the encoder output, because an image's encoder pass is a fixed, large, non-incremental cost paid again every turn while text prefill was already cheap per token. 21.7 seconds for the first turn is the number to remember — that is the encoder, not the language model, and it is why local VLM use feels broken in a way local chat does not. The enabling trick is small and the naive version fails silently: identity is established by SHA-256 over decoded pixel values, so the same picture arriving as a URL, a base64 blob and a file path still hits the cache — kv-reuse-must-be-exact-prefix-to-stay-lossless's exact-match requirement relocated to where the obvious key misses identity that plainly exists. So the map's regime rule gains a clause: one user, multi-turn, about an image → cache the embeddings. It also composes cleanly in this map's own idiom, which is rare: the lever spends memory (156 MB per cached 1024×1024 image) to buy repeated encoder compute — a currency nothing else in the local stack competes for, which is why it adds where speculation and quantization did not. Entirely conditional on repetition: the cold start is unimproved and one-shot queries gain nothing. One model, one resolution, one image; no quality eval. [T1, vendor-measured]
  • The runtime is worth ~20% where it matters and 87% where it doesn't (new 2026-07-30). On identical 4-bit weights on one M4 Max (128 GB, 546 GB/s), an MLX-native vLLM-style engine beats llama.cpp by 21–87% — Qwen3-0.6B 525.5 vs 281.5 tok/s (1.87×), Qwen3-8B 93.3 vs 76.9 (1.21×), Qwen3-30B-A3B 109.7 vs 89.9 (1.17×). The useful part is the slope: the advantage decays monotonically with model size, and continuous batching scales 3.7× at 0.6B but only 2.6× at 8B, attributed to bandwidth saturation. Both curves confirm the mechanism this map already holds — single-stream decode on consumer hardware is bandwidth-bound, so runtime cleverness pays where the model is small enough to leave bandwidth unused. Deflationary consequence worth carrying: at the 7B–30B sizes this vault's premise concerns, an engine swap is worth about 20%, not 87%. Discounts: the author is the engine's vendor; MLX 4-bit and Q4_K_M are not the same quantization scheme, so part of the gap may be bit allocation rather than runtime, and the-gguf-quant-ladder-is-graded-not-a-cliff showed format choice can move GSM8K 3.4 points at constant perplexity; no quality benchmark appears anywhere; no prefill/decode split; no energy measurement — the axis where the vault's Jetson result found MoE's real cost. [T1, vendor-measured]
  • The levers do not stack, and the reason is that most of them spend the same currency (new 2026-07-29, and stated first because it conditions every speedup number below). Speculative decoding and weight quantization both convert idle arithmetic into saved memory traffic, and a decode step has a finite amount of idle arithmetic to sell. Quantization spends it first. Measured: applying EAGLE-2 to a 4-bit weight Llama-3-8B yields a 1.3× relative speedup where the FP16 model gets 2.2× on an A100, because the target's verification-to-decoding time ratio — ideally ~1, since verifying n tokens and decoding one read the same weights — stays below 1.2 for FP16 and W8A8 but reaches 1.8 for W4A16 on an A100 and ~2.3 on an RTX 3090. It is not an agreement problem: average accepted length under quantization tracks full precision within noise. Three consequences. The tuning advice inverts — bigger draft trees help FP16 and hurt 4-bit, so halving EAGLE-2's tree moves FP16(8B) 2.3× → 2.1× while moving W4A16(8B) 2.7× → 2.9×, and cutting draft passes from six to three takes it to 3.4×; taken to the limit, plain sequential speculation beats tree-based EAGLE-2 on a W4A16 70B (2.31× vs 1.80×) despite drafting eleven times slower, because per-iteration verification costs 29.7 ms against 45.8 ms. The consumer card is where it bites: across the whole grid of schemes × hardware, integration beats either technique alone in every cell but one — W4A16 on the 8B model on the RTX 3090, which is precisely what a reader of this map would run, because weaker silicon has less spare arithmetic per unit of bandwidth and saturates sooner. And the fix is a hierarchy, not a tuning — insert a mid-sized model to absorb the tree and hand the quantized target a flat sequence (2.78×, 98.19 tok/s on W4A16 Llama-3-70B, 1.31× over the best EAGLE-2 config). The generalizable rule, and the yardstick this map should now apply to every stacked claim: ask what currency each lever spends, and do not assume two levers spending the same one will add. [T1]
  • The quant ladder is graded, the damage is task-shaped, and perplexity will not choose for you (new 2026-07-24). Measured across 13 llama.cpp formats on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct: 8-, 6-, 5- and 4-bit K-quants all sit within about half an aggregate point of FP16 (Q4_K_M 69.15 vs 69.47, at 69.4% smaller). The only rung that moves is 3-bit, and it moves unevenly — at Q3_K_S, GSM8K falls 9.3 points and MMLU 4.2 while HellaSwag falls 0.6. Precision is load-bearing for multi-step arithmetic and instruction compliance, near-free for single-step commonsense. Within 3-bit it is a slope rather than a cliff: Q3_K_L recovers most of Q3_K_S's loss for four points of file size. Selection rule with teeth: do not pick from the perplexity column — the four 5-bit formats span 0.03 PPL and 3.4 GSM8K points, so PPL and task quality rank the menu differently. Converting costs 27–43 s, so measuring beats guessing. [T1]
  • Quantization is two speed knobs pointing opposite ways (new 2026-07-24). Decode is bandwidth-bound and gets monotonically faster as the file shrinks (2.83 → 9.91 tok/s, F16 → Q3_K_S, 3.5×). Prefill is compute-bound and does not: throughput peaks mid-ladder (Q4_0 at 97.35 vs F16's 79.57) and then falls to 57.39 at Q3_K_S — 28% below full precision, because dequantizing packed weights costs more than the smaller read saves. So the right quant level depends on your prompt-to-generation ratio, not only your memory budget: long-prefill RAG and long-generation chat should not pick the same file. Every throughput figure in this map now needs a fourth stamp field — which phase was measured — alongside kernel, card, and precision. [T1]
  • ...unless you stop keeping them resident, and then sparsity is exactly what saves you (new 2026-07-26). The bullet below is measured with every weight in memory, which is the configuration that makes the active/total distinction invisible. Offload the experts to host RAM and the same property inverts: Mixtral-8×7B is 96.6% expert parameters and decode touches two of eight per layer, so an LRU expert cache (k=2 at 12 GB, k=4 at 16 GB) plus speculative expert prefetch — run layer L+1's gating function on layer L's hidden states, recall ~0.8–0.9 one layer ahead — sustains 2–3 tok/s at batch size 1 on a free-tier Colab T4, an RTX 3060 and an RTX 3080 Mobile, against 0.58–1.06 for naive layer-wise offloading. The 12 GB 3060 nearly matches the 16 GB 3080 Mobile because both are bottlenecked by host-to-device PCIe, not the GPU. So the practitioner's rule is conditional on fit, and the two halves are complements rather than rivals: if the model fits, prefer dense at matched active params; if it does not, an MoE is the easier thing to offload, because a dense model of the same total has no idle 75% to leave in RAM. Note the prefetch is a different kind of speculation from the map's quality-free lever below — it guesses which weights, not which tokens, so it needs no verification and a wrong guess costs only the load you already owed. Bounded: a venue-less two-author report, one model family, quality evidence perplexity and 5-shot MMLU only at ~2.6 bits per expert parameter, and 2–3 tok/s is readable rather than comfortable. [T1]
  • On bandwidth-bound hardware with everything resident, inference cost tracks total parameters, not active ones (new 2026-07-24; scoped 2026-07-26). The MoE promise to a local user — 1.3B-active costs what 1.3B dense costs — does not survive contact with the devices it is aimed at. OLMoE-1B-7B (6.9B total) at Q4_K_M through llama.cpp is 10% slower than Llama-3.2-1B on an M2 Pro and 31% slower on a Jetson Orin Nano, where it also loses to Qwen2.5-1.5B — a model with more active params — at 2.1× the energy per token and 8.01 GiB peak RSS against an 8 GB ceiling. Node-level timing of the decode graph puts routing under 9% of MoE-block time, so this is not a kernel that will improve: to serve 1.3B of compute you must keep 6.9B parameters resident, and residency is what a bandwidth-bound device is short of. Sparsity buys FLOPs; FLOPs are not the constraint here. [T1]
  • The prefill/decode split is a property of the computation, and the admission layer needed a second generation to respect it (new 2026-07-27). Two facts, and the second follows from the first. The workload has two opposite bottlenecks. Prefill runs the whole prompt through the linear layers at once, so the cost of hauling each weight matrix out of memory is amortized over thousands of tokens: it is compute-bound, and batching more requests buys nothing because one request already saturates the arithmetic units. Decode runs one token per request through the same matrices, so it is memory-bound, and batching helps almost linearly. The gap has a memorable size — one decode token costs about what 128 prefill tokens cost — and two consequences: prefill throughput saturates around 512 tokens, so a prompt is not an indivisible unit of work, and a decode batch has compute slack that prompt work can be poured into nearly free. This is the same partition the map already held from llama.cpp measurement on a CPU (decode speeds up down the quant ladder, prefill slows), now derived from arithmetic intensity on A100s under vLLM — two unrelated stacks, one structure. Therefore iteration-level scheduling had a second defect. If a request can join mid-flight, its prompt executes mid-flight, freezing every running decode for the duration — a generation stall measured at several seconds in vLLM. Chunked prefills (split the prompt across iterations) plus stall-free batching (fill each iteration with running decodes first, then top up to a token budget with prefill chunks) bound it: naive coalescing inflates an iteration up to 28.3× over decode-only, chunked it is 3.7×, and the penalty shrinks as batch and context grow. Worth 2.6–6.3× more sustainable load at a fixed P99 time-between-tokens target across 7B–180B. Two riders. The token budget is the knob, not the batch size — vLLM's capacity here is "largely identical" at max batch 32, 64 and 128, because under a latency target the scheduler stalls before the memory manager runs out, so paging enabled batches the admission policy could not use. And the chunk size is not a free parameter: 512 costs at most ~25% extra prefill work and 2048 nearly none, but tile quantization makes a chunk of 257 cost 32% more than 256, a one-token cliff. Scope this honestly — it is a multi-tenant technique, and with one user there are no incumbents to protect; what transfers to the local stack is the token-budget idea, which llama.cpp already exposes as a physical batch size, and the tile-divisibility warning. That transfer is unmeasured. [T1]
  • Prefill/decode contention has two cures, they spend different resources, and the head-to-head now exists (new 2026-07-28). The map recorded on 2026-07-27 that it held two rival designs and zero evidence between them. Chunking bounds the interference by never letting a prompt monopolize an iteration; disaggregation removes it by running the two phases on separate GPUs and shipping the KV cache across. The comparison arrives from the disaggregation side — one of its two baselines is a chunked-prefill implementation — at 1.6–7.4× more sustainable request rate, and 2.0–5.7× over vLLM, at a 90% SLO-attainment target across chat, code-completion and summarization workloads. Discount the partisanship and the mechanism critique still stands, because it is a dilemma rather than an overhead: a chunk small enough to leave room for decode tokens is by construction too small to saturate the GPU, and a chunk large enough to saturate leaves no room — plus chunking pays O(N²) KV re-reads against O(N), a cost the chunking source measured on itself and accepted. Separation's own bill is that the cache must move: 1.13 GB for a 512-token request on a 66B model, ~90 Gbps at 10 requests/s, which disappears into under 0.1% of request latency over 600 GB/s NVLink and does not disappear over the same testbed's 25 Gbps cross-node link — a fifth appearance of this vault's interconnect threshold, for once on the permissive side. Underneath sits a sharper structural claim: each phase wants a different parallelism. Prefill's latency is execution time plus queueing delay (an M/D/1 queue), so tensor parallelism wins under tight targets and low arrival rates while pipeline parallelism wins as the rate climbs; decode inverts the pair. A colocated engine must answer once and over-provisions for the tighter SLO — proven by ablation, since searching parallelism without disaggregating matches the untuned baseline exactly. The scope disclaimer is the authors' own and it is blunt: with "only a few or even a single GPU" the design space collapses and disaggregation "struggles and even fails," while for throughput-only workloads chunking "may be preferred." So the rule for this map is the pair, not the winner — disaggregation is for meeting two latency targets under concurrency; chunking is for keeping one device busy, and a laptop has one device and one target.   • The serving stack has four layers, they stack in a dependency order, and only one of them pays for a single user (revised 2026-07-26). The fourth is reuse: every engine throws away a request's KV cache when it finishes, though the next request usually shares a prefix — a system prompt, a few-shot block, an image, a chat history. RadixAttention keeps them all in a radix tree with LRU eviction on leaves, reference-counts nodes against the running batch, holds no preallocated cache pool (cache and running requests share one pool, so a full cache is evicted for a bigger batch), and schedules longest-shared-prefix first — provably the optimal order, since it is a depth-first traversal, and measured at 96% of optimal hit rate. Worth up to 6.4× throughput and 3.7× lower latency vs vLLM v0.2.5 on Llama-7B, holding at 70B and on Mixtral under tensor parallelism. Two structural facts neither this nor any other paper here states. First the layers compose in one direction: RadixAttention needs the per-token page layout (paging), needs reference counting against a running batch (continuous batching), and its no-preallocation policy presumes iteration-level admission — each layer builds the substrate the next one needs. Second, and this is what corrects the rule below: reuse is driven by prefix overlap, not by concurrency, and the most reliable source of prefix overlap is one user having a conversation. A month of production service on Chatbot Arena, one worker per model, measured 74.1% hit rate on Vicuna-33B and 52.4% on LLaVA-NeXT-34B, cutting first-token latency 1.7×. So the map's rule gains a third clause — many users → batch continuously; one user, single-shot → speculate; one user, multi-turn → cache the prefix — and reuse is free when it does not apply (0.2 s of 74.3 s, under 0.3%, on a workload with no reuse to find). Its own null result is honest and matters: long outputs with no cross-session sharing show almost no speedup, because reuse cuts prefill and nothing else. [T1]
  • The serving stack's first three layers (new 2026-07-25). Placement, depth, and admission are separate problems: PagedAttention manages where the KV cache lives, speculative decoding attacks serial depth for one stream, and iteration-level scheduling manages when requests enter and leave. The third is the origin of continuous batching and the observation is specific to generation — a request needs an unpredictable number of iterations, so a request-granularity batch stays frozen until its slowest member finishes, and early finishers cannot return while new arrivals cannot join. Rescheduling after every iteration removes both stalls: 36.9× throughput on GPT-3 175B at a matched 190 ms/token median latency vs FasterTransformer, with the engine alone (scheduler disabled) measured as slightly worse than the baseline — so the win is entirely scheduling, not kernels. Two things this map must carry with it. The numbers do not compose: 36.9× is against FasterTransformer while PagedAttention's 2–4× is against this, so quoting a stacked multiple chains baselines that do not chain. And the regimes are opposite: continuous batching's one clear loss is max_bs=1 and its weak case is low load — precisely single-user local serving — while speculative decoding is measured at batch size 1 and erodes under concurrency. Many users → batch continuously; one user → speculate. Neither paper says this, because each measured only its own regime. (Superseded 2026-07-26 by the four-layer bullet above, which adds the third clause and the composition order.) [T1]

  • One lever in this map costs no quality at all, and it is the only one. Every other technique here — 4-bit weights, 2-bit KV, the GGUF quant ladder — buys speed by spending accuracy, and pricing that loss is this map's job. Speculative decoding is the exception: a small draft model proposes γ tokens, the target scores all γ+1 in one parallel pass, and a modified rejection rule (accept with min(1, p/q), resample the first rejection from norm(max(0, p−q))) makes the output provably identical in distribution to ordinary decoding. It spends arithmetic and a second model's memory instead — which is affordable precisely because decode is bandwidth-bound and the ALUs are idle. 2.8–3.4× at batch size 1 on T5-XXL (TPU-v4). The counter-intuitive tuning rule: the best draft is a deliberately weak one — T5-large agrees with the target most often (α=0.82) and is the slowest option (1.7×) because its cost eats the winnings, while T5-small (α=0.75) gives 3.4×. Use this as the map's yardstick: ask any claimed speedup what currency it spends, and note that this one spends the cheapest. [T1]

  • Does the offloaded-MoE verification penalty hold on a coarse-grained model, and does it interact with the quantization result from the same night? (new 2026-08-02.) The expansion curve is measured on two models with 64 routed experts and top-6 routing, where a block's tokens disperse widely. Mixtral's 8 experts at top-2 should expand far more slowly, which would make plain speculative decoding viable under offload with no trained drafter — and Mixtral is the substrate this map's other offload evidence uses. That is a profiling run, not a research project. The interaction is sharper: the same night's mixed-precision-moe-finally-has-a-throughput-number puts Mixtral-8×7B at 12.0 GB, i.e. resident on a 12 GB card, which would make the whole offload question moot for that model. The unrun comparison a consumer now faces is three-way — offloaded MoE, aggressively quantized resident MoE, and a dense model that fits — and no source runs any two of them together.
  • A phone appears in this map for the first time and nothing about it is reproducible (new 2026-08-02). The Snapdragon 8 Elite / Hexagon HTP v81 measurements run llama.cpp with Q4_0 weights, which is the local stack — but the speedup depends on trained per-layer draft experts with no released weights or code, and the baseline half (Flash-staged autoregressive at 10.18 tok/s against a resident-memory reference of 14.36) is the only part a reader could reproduce today. What this map actually wants from mobile is the boring version of that table: the staging penalty for a Q4_0 MoE on Flash-backed mobile storage, with and without expert caching, across a few models. Nobody has published it, and it is the number that decides whether MoE-on-phone is a format problem or a storage-bandwidth problem.
  • llama.cpp is no longer unsourced, but it is sourced as a measuring instrument, not as a system (revised 2026-07-24). Two sources now run it — across the K-quant ladder on CPU, and across Metal and CUDA backends on edge silicon — so this map finally has local numbers. What neither describes is llama.cpp's design: the ggml graph, the CPU/Metal kernel split, threading and batching behaviour, mmap and memory-mapping strategy, or Ollama's serving layer above it. The gap has narrowed from "no local evidence" to "no local architecture source," and the second is the harder one to close from papers, since the primary documentation is the repository.
  • Continuous batching landed 2026-07-25 and prefix caching 2026-07-26; the local half of both is still missing (revised 2026-07-26). Every serving number in-vault is datacenter, multi-tenant, unquantized — A100s, A10Gs, V100s — and continuous batching's own evidence shows it degenerating at max_bs=1. Prefix caching is the one layer whose mechanism predicts a single-user win, and the production Chatbot Arena hit rates (74.1% on Vicuna-33B) are the closest thing in-vault to evidence for it, but that is still one-worker datacenter serving rather than a laptop. The measurement this map wants is now very specific and nobody has published it: llama.cpp / Ollama, one user, a multi-turn conversation, with and without prompt-prefix reuse, at a GGUF quant level — because the two effects interact in opposite directions (reuse cuts prefill; quantization slows prefill and speeds decode, quantization-speeds-decode-and-can-slow-prefill), and nobody has measured them together. Chunked prefill landed 2026-07-27 and does not relieve any of this — it is A100 work, and it is a technique for protecting incumbent requests from an arrival, which is a situation one user never creates. Disaggregated prefill/decode serving landed 2026-07-28 (distserve-disaggregating-prefill-decoding) and the head-to-head against chunking now exists — disaggregation wins by 1.6–7.4× against a chunked-prefill baseline, and its authors state that on one or a few GPUs the technique "struggles and even fails" while chunking is preferable where only throughput matters. That resolves the rivalry and confirms neither answer is aimed here: one needs a cluster to partition, the other needs incumbent requests to protect. What replaces the gap is narrower — the vault now holds the datacenter answer to a datacenter question and still has no serving measurement at all on one card with one user. Also still unmeasured: KV-cache reuse across process restarts, which is the form a local user would actually want. PARTIALLY ANSWERED 2026-07-30 (vllm-mlx-apple-silicon-inference): the serving stack has finally been benchmarked on consumer hardware, and prefix reuse is measured on a single user's multi-turn conversation — but the reuse result that matters turns out to be about images, not text (vision embeddings 7.8×, text KV cache 1.2×), the machine is a 128 GB M4 Max rather than a 12–24 GB card, the engine is MLX rather than llama.cpp, and the paper reports no quality numbers and no prefill/decode split, which is precisely the decomposition the interaction above requires. So the exact experiment this item names is still unrun: llama.cpp / Ollama, one user, multi-turn, with and without prefix reuse, at a stated GGUF quant level, reporting prefill and decode separately. What is newly worth adding to it: do the same sweep with an image in the context, since tonight's result says that is where the order of magnitude lives, and llama.cpp's multimodal path has never been measured at all.
  • What does an image cost locally, and does llama.cpp cache it? (new 2026-07-30.) The 21.7-second cold-start encoder pass on an M4 Max is the first number the vault has for the fixed cost of a local vision-language turn, and it dwarfs everything this map measures about text. Three things follow that nobody has published. Whether llama.cpp / Ollama cache vision embeddings at all, or re-encode on every turn — the difference is 7.8× on a workload people actually run. What the cache costs on a memory-bound machine, since 156 MB per 1024×1024 image is free at 128 GB and is not at 16 GB, and the video numbers (12.1 GB for 64 frames) would bind well before that. And what the encoder does at a GGUF quant level, since every quantization result on this map is about the language model and the-vision-encoder-is-a-design-choice-not-a-frozen-import says the tower is no longer a fixed import.
  • The token budget is a knob local runtimes expose and nobody has tuned in public (new 2026-07-27). Chunked prefill's mechanism transfers even though its motivation does not: llama.cpp's physical batch size chunks prompt processing the same way, and the datacenter measurement says two things about it that a local user would care about. The chunking penalty is ~25% of prefill work at 512 tokens and near zero at 2048, so smaller is not safer; and tile quantization makes a non-tile-divisible chunk cost up to 32% more (257 versus 256), a cliff produced by a one-token change to a config value. Both numbers are A100-specific — the tile geometry and the FLOPs-to-bandwidth ratio that sets the saturation point differ on Apple silicon, a consumer GPU and a CPU — so what the map holds is the shape of the effect, and the sweep that would give it a local number is a few hours of work on hardware this vault's reader already owns.
  • The quant ladder is measured on one axis only (new 2026-07-24). Thirteen formats, but one model, one size, one CPU. Three extrapolations the map is now tempted to make and cannot: whether the 3-bit task-shaped damage holds at 70B (larger models are widely believed to tolerate aggressive quantization better, and nothing in-vault tests it), whether it holds on an MoE — where per-expert calibration coverage is already flagged as unsourced — and where the prefill/decode crossover sits on Apple silicon or a consumer GPU, whose arithmetic-to-bandwidth ratios differ sharply from a 96-core Xeon's. The IQ series and MXFP4 rungs of the menu remain unmeasured entirely.
  • Nobody has measured what quantization does to the KV cache in the local stack (sharpened 2026-07-24). The new ladder evidence is weight-only, at short context. This map holds that the KV budget is a product of three levers (architecture, precision, placement) and that a long window makes all three bind at once — and the one measurement it just gained deliberately avoids that regime. llama.cpp's own KV-quantization options are still ungraded here.
  • What an offload-aware runtime would recover on edge MoE (new 2026-07-24). The edge result measures full residency, and its own decomposition stops at "routing is not the problem" without separating weight traffic from expert-dispatch gather/scatter from KV pressure. Only the first is irreducible. Whether measured route locality (~28% consecutive-token expert reuse) is enough for an expert cache to close a 31% gap is the actionable unknown, and the offloading literature that attacks it is un-ingested.
  • What speculative decoding costs on the hardware this map is about — RESOLVED IN PART 2026-07-29, and the resolution reframes the rest. The three unknowns were: (a) batching erodes the win — concurrency already fills the arithmetic headroom speculation spends, and the crossover batch size is unreported; (b) the draft model competes for VRAM with the target's weights and KV on exactly the device where memory is the binding constraint — partly answered by multi-token prediction, which yields a draft that is one transformer block sharing the target's embedding and output head, at 85–90% acceptance for 1.8× tokens/s, though as a pretraining-time decision it selects checkpoints rather than tuning a deployment; (c) α is measured on full-precision pairs, so whether a quantized draft and a quantized target still agree at 0.6–0.8 is unknown. (c) is now answered at a finer grain than it was asked: a W4A16 8B drafting for a W4A16 70B reaches an average accepted length of 5.28 — higher than the FP16-headed alternatives — so 4-bit agreement is fine for a real draft model, and the reported fragility belongs to quantizing a single-transformer-layer draft head, which has no redundancy to lose. What replaces (c) is that agreement was never the binding constraint: the throughput arithmetic breaks first (see the first belief), and the sharpest missing datum is now the one this source's own design implies and never measures — the memory cost of the hierarchy on one card. HierSpec keeps a draft head, an 8B and a 70B resident to buy latency; nobody has priced that against simply running the target alone at batch size 1 on a card where the target barely fits. The llama.cpp-shaped version: does a two-model speculative setup ever fit and pay on 12–24 GB? Unknown, and now clearly the question. Also still open and now sharper: whether the "draft shallower and flatter on a 4-bit model" rule holds at GGUF K-quant levels rather than GPTQ-with-Marlin, since the mechanism is about the dequantization/GEMM balance and llama.cpp's kernels have a different one.
  • KV-cache quantization — shrinking the entries PagedAttention places — has a second source as of 2026-07-29 (turboquant-near-optimal-kv-cache-quantization, T1, joining KIVI on post-training-quantization), and the gap it leaves is narrower and more specific. What is now known: a data-free quantizer reaches 3.5 bits per channel at parity with a full 16-bit cache and 2.5 bits at −0.62 on LongBench-E. What is still unserved, and there are three distinct pieces: (i) the method's non-integer widths come from an outlier-channel split llama.cpp's K-quant menu cannot express, so neither of the map's two KV sources is reachable from the local stack — whether Ollama's and llama.cpp's own KV-quant flags land anywhere near 3.5-bit parity is unmeasured by anyone; (ii) no speed number exists for KV quantization at all in either source, which matters more here than for weight quantization because the KV quantizer sits on the critical path of every attention call and the decode/prefill effects may run opposite ways; (iii) a measured paging+precision combined result on one runtime.
  • The KV budget is now known to be a product of three levers — architecture (GQA, fewer heads), precision (KV quantization, fewer bits), placement (paging, less waste) — and no in-vault source measures more than one at a time. Since the models people actually run are already GQA, the headline numbers from the other two levers are upper bounds on what they add. The combined measurement is this map's sharpest missing datum.

What replaces it is narrower and listed above: the IQ/MXFP4 rungs, other scales, and MoE bases.
  • Every in-vault throughput number needs a hardware stamp (new 2026-07-19). The kernel evidence now shows tuning does not transfer across GPU generations, yet this map's figures (2–4× from paging, >3× from 4-bit kernels, 2.35–3.47× from 2-bit KV) come from different papers on different cards and are read here as if commensurable. They are not. A single-runtime, single-card measurement of the stacked levers would fix both this and the KV-budget gap above.
