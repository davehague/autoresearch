Status: consolidating (emerging → consolidating 2026-07-24) Status note (2026-08-26): holds consolidating, and the map gets its second consumer-accelerator answer in two days — the opposite sign from the first, and for the same structural reason. ane-placement-decode-measurement (T1, independent researcher; code, measurement harnesses and run ledgers released, every headline claim supported by at least two of three independent measurement paths) asks what gets a language model onto the Apple Neural Engine and what makes it fast there. Where the Intel NPU is a throughput null that buys placement (the-npu-shares-the-bandwidth-it-was-supposed-to-relieve), the ANE delivers a real 1.8–2.2× decode speedup at a fraction of the CPU's power — but only if the weight format lets CoreML's segmenter admit the graph at all, and that is a discontinuity, not a gradient. The same trained h512/L10 stack exports to 0% residency in fp16 (56.0 MB) and 82.5% in int8 (28.2 MB) or 2-bit LUT (7.1 MB); on trained checkpoints the byte counters record the fp16 model moving exactly 0.00 GB through the engine across ~5,750 consecutive inferences at 1.346 ms while its int8 sibling moves 255.6 GB at 0.765 ms and burns 1,655 mW against 4,929 mW of CPU rail. Replicated on an M3 by ANE energy (0 mJ in fp16, ~2,200 mJ in int8, 1.76× faster). Three findings transfer past the device. Placement is a property of how a computation is written: a fused RMSNorm is 100% ANE-eligible and its arithmetically identical pow-decomposition is 0%, every constituent op reporting CPU as its only supported device — a variable no prior ANE paper controls, which is why three of them give three different answers on whether normalization runs on the engine. And the telemetry lies silently: a sampler matching channel names alone reports a confident 0.0 mW for a model at 98.9% residency, because the channel lives in a different IOReport group on that chip. Status note (2026-08-25): holds consolidating, and the map gets the answer to a question it has carried implicitly since the seed ingest — does the NPU in every consumer SoC help? — and it is a clean null. precompiled-pipeline-shards-intel-ai-pc-fleet (T1, Not Community Labs + Intel; every table row backed by a committed script and raw log in a public reproduction directory) is the first source here where every machine is a machine a household owns: two ASUS Zenbooks and an HP OmniBook over WiFi, plus four Lunar Lake cloud instances for the large run. The NPU never wins on throughput. Per-stage decode latency ranks GPU < CPU < NPU — an NPU stage costs roughly 4× the iGPU per token on a 9B-class model — and an ILP placement solver fed real per-device profiles "never selects the NPU on throughput grounds," because it shares the same LPDDR bus as the integrated GPU and decode is bandwidth-bound. attention-is-memory-bound-not-compute-bound is the mechanism; this is the first measurement in the vault that applies it to the accelerator marketed as the on-device answer. What the NPU does buy is placement — a shard that fits nowhere else fits there, and it frees the iGPU at lower power — which is a scheduling argument, not a speed one. The second result raises this map's ceiling rather than lowering it: Llama 3.1 70B INT4 (~36 GB) served across four 32 GB laptops at 5.42 tok/s, bit-exact against the same topology's non-speculative path, while a monolithic 70B INT4 export OOM-killed at layer 71 of 80 on a 133 GB machine. Depth splitting is the only lever on this map that raises the size ceiling without trading quality — and it clears the paper's own 5 tok/s interactive floor by 8%, with a second concurrent stream pushing per-user throughput back under it. On 8B over a LAN the sign flips: two users at 21.99 tok/s each, aggregate 43.97, 1.79× the single-user monolithic baseline on the same hardware. What we believe now:

  • On a consumer neural accelerator the weight format is an admission ticket before it is an optimization, and the admission is discontinuous. The same trained graph in fp16 can be assigned entirely to the CPU — moving zero bytes through the engine, confirmed by memory-controller counters rather than by the compiler's plan — while in int8 or 2-bit it becomes ~83% resident and runs 1.8–2.2× faster at a third of the CPU power; there is no partial state between 0% and ~83%. Which side you land on is a joint function of architecture, size and encoding: within one conv-heavy family the fp16 cliff sits between 25.9M and 31.1M non-embedding parameters, yet an all-attention model at 22.29M — below that cliff — is 98.9% resident, so no parameter-count rule holds. The two directions reconcile through one cost expression: growing the model grows the work term until it amortizes the engine's per-dispatch overhead, and quantization shrinks the bytes and transfer terms so the engine wins at a size where it otherwise would not. Measured on M1 and replicated on M3 by energy. This is a scheduler decision inside a closed compiler, not a hardware limit — the engine can run the fp16 graph; CoreML simply prices it onto the CPU. Pairs with the NPU null: on an Intel AI PC the second accelerator buys placement and no throughput; on Apple silicon it buys real throughput, gated on placement. (the-weight-format-decides-whether-the-accelerator-runs-the-model-at-all, T1, 2026-08-26.)

  • Whether an operation runs on the accelerator is decided by how it is written, not by what it computes. Six formulations of RMSNorm compute the same function; the fused one is 100% ANE-eligible across twelve ops and the pow/reduce_mean/rsqrt decomposition is 0%, with every constituent op reporting CPU as its only supported device. 49 of 64 primitive shapes are fully eligible; the exclusions are the embedding gather at every index dtype, conv kernels ≥15, and that one decomposition. No FLOP count, parameter count or roofline model can express this, and CoreML exposes no runtime diagnostic saying which unit executed a call — so it was unobservable by construction, and the ANE literature contains three mutually contradictory answers about normalization placement, none citing the others. The actionable artifact is the per-op supported-device set rather than a residency percentage: one names the op to rewrite, the other only says the graph fell off. (placement-on-an-accelerator-is-a-property-of-expression-not-semantics, T1, 2026-08-26.)

  • A memory saving and a decode speedup are different quantities, and past a point they have opposite signs. Adding KV-cache compression to an already-quantized model lowers peak memory and drops decode to 0.61–0.63× at 32K context while barely moving prefill — and the penalty is context-sensitive (0.97× at 2K), because long-context decode repeatedly reads and dequantizes the cache. A 1-bit weight format uses half the memory of a 4-bit one and delivers 0.41× its prefill. On an M1 Max an 8-bit cache buys a 46% memory cut for a 46% decode loss, one for one. And the transfer is asymmetric: the same Q4_K_M format gives 1.36× prefill on an H100 and 0.97× — parity — on the M1 Max while cutting memory on both, so memory savings port across hardware and throughput gains do not. Expert pruning, which compresses least (~1.3×), is the lever whose speed survives the runtime (0.94–1.03× of baseline, preserving the BF16 execution path) where 4-bit scores half as well on hardware-acceleration efficiency. Batch size 1, one H100 and one M1 Max, one 30B-A3B model family. This is the widest confirmation yet of this map's currency test, and it replicates cache-compression-charges-throughput-and-pays-only-under-pressure from an unrelated group on different hardware. (a-memory-saving-and-a-decode-speedup-are-different-quantities, T1, 2026-08-26.)

  • Local accelerator telemetry fails as a confident zero, not as an error. The same sampler moved between chip generations reports 0.0 mW of accelerator power for a model demonstrably 98.9% resident, because the channel exists in a different IOReport group; the byte counters a name-only match reaches on one chip read INT64_MIN uninitialized, and on another they do not exist at all so the sampler returns 0 under load — indistinguishable from genuine non-residency. Match on (group, name, unit) jointly, enumerate the channels on the target silicon first, and validate the counter by behaviour: hold model, loop and machine fixed, vary only the requested compute units, and confirm it reads exactly zero when the engine is excluded while identical work is still being done. Every one of these failures reads as the pessimistic hypothesis a practitioner already holds. (accelerator-telemetry-fails-silently-as-a-confident-zero, T1, suggestive, 2026-08-26.)

  • At batch size one on a CPU, the ratio of cache-free layers to attention layers is the dominant deployable design variable — and it can be chosen from a byte-count argument before training. Six attention blocks in eighteen, with the rest carrying a two-timestep convolution state, halve cache bytes per context token (6,144 vs 12,288 B) against a parameter-matched all-attention twin and decode 1.76× faster at 2048 tokens of context (2.08× against an external peer), with the advantage near unity at empty context and growing monotonically with depth — the signature the mechanism predicts and a merely leaner model would not produce. Bought at a quality tie, not a quality win, and at a conceded 3.9-point gap to a 2T-token peer. The operator choice is subordinate to the ratio and was made for a deployment reason: a depthwise convolution over a selective-scan state-space layer, because it maps onto existing CPU kernels and exports to a stock runtime without new operators. [T1, strong — single author, single seed]

  • On bandwidth-poor local hardware the token rate is set by the linear and feed-forward layers, and a kernel speedup on attention barely reaches the user (new 2026-08-20). A rebuilt FlashAttention that runs 12–14× faster than scalar and ~2× faster than the shipping vectorized kernel on a physical RISC-V board converts to +4% end-to-end on the one configuration where it is positive and "comparable" everywhere else — because attention is ~50% of per-layer execution in prefill and ~10% in decode, and the linear and feed-forward layers are byte-identical between the two builds. The rule to carry: a component benchmark and a deployment benchmark are different quantities and the ratio between them is large and unpredictable, which this map has now seen in both directions (a-single-op-benchmark-measures-synchronization-not-dispatch overstated a cost by 20×; this understates a gain by ~3×). Demand the end-to-end number, and when a source gives only the component number treat the other as unknown rather than proportional. [T1] an-attention-kernel-win-does-not-reach-the-decode-user

  • File size does not predict deployed latency, and the platform spread is two orders of magnitude wider than the accuracy spread (new 2026-08-20). Three sub-1.1B models at 508–769 MB in Q4_K_M run at a mean 0.13 s/sample on GPU, 0.61 on CPU and 8.91 on a Raspberry Pi — ~4.8× and ~70× — while their accuracy under a controlled prompt stays within three points across GPU and CPU. "It runs on the edge" and "it is usable on the edge" are different claims and the artifact size answers neither. The same campaign supplies the harder warning about how these comparisons get made: a prompt-template swap alone reproduced 38 of a 39-point apparent GPU-to-Raspberry-Pi accuracy gap on one GPU over the same items (the-prompt-template-outweighs-the-hardware-it-runs-on, filed to evaluation-contamination), so any cross-runtime or cross-device number in this map that did not hold the prompt path byte-identical is measuring the prompt.   • Under 4-bit weights the memory footprint stops being a forecast and becomes a constant — and the number you have been reading off the file is the wrong one (new 2026-08-19). Across 1,920 instrumented agent trajectories on four Q4_K_M backbones served through llama.cpp, peak VRAM varies by 0.32–9.36% — Phi-4-mini's entire spread is 122 MiB on a 5.7 GB footprint — while the trajectories driving it span four orders of magnitude in generated tokens. A one-way ANOVA gives prompt family and retry count at most 12.6% of the variance against 87.4–100% residual; the deliberately-perturbed n_ctx/KV-dtype sweep, by contrast, explains 91.3–100%. So the dynamics exist and the weight term buries them, a per-backbone historical mean is a competitive predictor, and a learned prompt-feature regression buys 6–8% relative MAPE with overlapping confidence intervals — and is 57% worse on the flattest backbone. The corollary is the one that costs money: the closed-form model reaches 2.20–4.44% MAPE only when its two constants come from a live post-load GPU reading. Feed it the on-disk GGUF size and zero activation — which is exactly how every practitioner sizes a local model — and it underestimates 100% of runs by 16.66–53.25%, because the gap is llama.cpp's own buffers and graph allocations. Measure the loaded footprint once per (model, quantization, cache dtype) and pad; never size from the file. Riding along, on the same corpus: task success is set by the backbone and not by the memory — nvcc compile success runs 5.7% → 10.4% → 36.7% → 62.0% from Phi-4-mini to Qwen2.5-Coder-14B with peak VRAM uncorrelated with success inside three of four backbones.   • An arithmetic saving converts to wall-clock in proportion to sequence length, and at short sequence the conversion is nearly total loss (new 2026-08-16, and it is the currency test measured on the compute axis for the first time). Delete 20% of the contraction axis from every attention matrix product — a real 20% arithmetic cut, with the kernels rewritten in Triton to actually skip the work — and an A100 at batch size 1 returns 1.05× at 1024 tokens, 1.27× at 2048, 1.40× at 4096.   • The prefill/decode split is a property of the computation, and the admission layer needed a second generation to respect it (new 2026-07-27). Two facts, and the second follows from the first. The workload has two opposite bottlenecks. Prefill runs the whole prompt through the linear layers at once, so the cost of hauling each weight matrix out of memory is amortized over thousands of tokens: it is compute-bound, and batching more requests buys nothing because one request already saturates the arithmetic units. Decode runs one token per request through the same matrices, so it is memory-bound, and batching helps almost linearly. The gap has a memorable size — one decode token costs about what 128 prefill tokens cost — and two consequences: prefill throughput saturates around 512 tokens, so a prompt is not an indivisible unit of work, and a decode batch has compute slack that prompt work can be poured into nearly free. This is the same partition the map already held from llama.cpp measurement on a CPU (decode speeds up down the quant ladder, prefill slows), now derived from arithmetic intensity on A100s under vLLM — two unrelated stacks, one structure. Therefore iteration-level scheduling had a second defect. If a request can join mid-flight, its prompt executes mid-flight, freezing every running decode for the duration — a generation stall measured at several seconds in vLLM. Chunked prefills (split the prompt across iterations) plus stall-free batching (fill each iteration with running decodes first, then top up to a token budget with prefill chunks) bound it: naive coalescing inflates an iteration up to 28.3× over decode-only, chunked it is 3.7×, and the penalty shrinks as batch and context grow. Worth 2.6–6.3× more sustainable load at a fixed P99 time-between-tokens target across 7B–180B. Two riders. The token budget is the knob, not the batch size — vLLM's capacity here is "largely identical" at max batch 32, 64 and 128, because under a latency target the scheduler stalls before the memory manager runs out, so paging enabled batches the admission policy could not use. And the chunk size is not a free parameter: 512 costs at most ~25% extra prefill work and 2048 nearly none, but tile quantization makes a chunk of 257 cost 32% more than 256, a one-token cliff. Scope this honestly — it is a multi-tenant technique, and with one user there are no incumbents to protect; what transfers to the local stack is the token-budget idea, which llama.cpp already exposes as a physical batch size, and the tile-divisibility warning. That transfer is unmeasured. [T1]

  • Prefill/decode contention has two cures, they spend different resources, and the head-to-head now exists (new 2026-07-28). The map recorded on 2026-07-27 that it held two rival designs and zero evidence between them. Chunking bounds the interference by never letting a prompt monopolize an iteration; disaggregation removes it by running the two phases on separate GPUs and shipping the KV cache across. The comparison arrives from the disaggregation side — one of its two baselines is a chunked-prefill implementation — at 1.6–7.4× more sustainable request rate, and 2.0–5.7× over vLLM, at a 90% SLO-attainment target across chat, code-completion and summarization workloads. Discount the partisanship and the mechanism critique still stands, because it is a dilemma rather than an overhead: a chunk small enough to leave room for decode tokens is by construction too small to saturate the GPU, and a chunk large enough to saturate leaves no room — plus chunking pays O(N²) KV re-reads against O(N), a cost the chunking source measured on itself and accepted. Separation's own bill is that the cache must move: 1.13 GB for a 512-token request on a 66B model, ~90 Gbps at 10 requests/s, which disappears into under 0.1% of request latency over 600 GB/s NVLink and does not disappear over the same testbed's 25 Gbps cross-node link — a fifth appearance of this vault's interconnect threshold, for once on the permissive side. Underneath sits a sharper structural claim: each phase wants a different parallelism. Prefill's latency is execution time plus queueing delay (an M/D/1 queue), so tensor parallelism wins under tight targets and low arrival rates while pipeline parallelism wins as the rate climbs; decode inverts the pair. A colocated engine must answer once and over-provisions for the tighter SLO — proven by ablation, since searching parallelism without disaggregating matches the untuned baseline exactly. The scope disclaimer is the authors' own and it is blunt: with "only a few or even a single GPU" the design space collapses and disaggregation "struggles and even fails," while for throughput-only workloads chunking "may be preferred." So the rule for this map is the pair, not the winner — disaggregation is for meeting two latency targets under concurrency; chunking is for keeping one device busy, and a laptop has one device and one target. [T1]

  • The serving stack has four layers, they stack in a dependency order, and only one of them pays for a single user (revised 2026-07-26). The fourth is reuse: every engine throws away a request's KV cache when it finishes, though the next request usually shares a prefix — a system prompt, a few-shot block, an image, a chat history. RadixAttention keeps them all in a radix tree with LRU eviction on leaves, reference-counts nodes against the running batch, holds no preallocated cache pool (cache and running requests share one pool, so a full cache is evicted for a bigger batch), and schedules longest-shared-prefix first — provably the optimal order, since it is a depth-first traversal, and measured at 96% of optimal hit rate. Worth up to 6.4× throughput and 3.7× lower latency vs vLLM v0.2.5 on Llama-7B, holding at 70B and on Mixtral under tensor parallelism. Two structural facts neither this nor any other paper here states. First the layers compose in one direction: RadixAttention needs the per-token page layout (paging), needs reference counting against a running batch (continuous batching), and its no-preallocation policy presumes iteration-level admission — each layer builds the substrate the next one needs. Second, and this is what corrects the rule below: reuse is driven by prefix overlap, not by concurrency, and the most reliable source of prefix overlap is one user having a conversation. A month of production service on Chatbot Arena, one worker per model, measured 74.1% hit rate on Vicuna-33B and 52.4% on LLaVA-NeXT-34B, cutting first-token latency 1.7×. So the map's rule gains a third clause — many users → batch continuously; one user, single-shot → speculate; one user, multi-turn → cache the prefix — and reuse is free when it does not apply (0.2 s of 74.3 s, under 0.3%, on a workload with no reuse to find). Its own null result is honest and matters: long outputs with no cross-session sharing show almost no speedup, because reuse cuts prefill and nothing else. [T1]

  • The serving stack's first three layers (new 2026-07-25). Placement, depth, and admission are separate problems: PagedAttention manages where the KV cache lives, speculative decoding attacks serial depth for one stream, and iteration-level scheduling manages when requests enter and leave. The third is the origin of continuous batching and the observation is specific to generation — a request needs an unpredictable number of iterations, so a request-granularity batch stays frozen until its slowest member finishes, and early finishers cannot return while new arrivals cannot join. Rescheduling after every iteration removes both stalls: 36.9× throughput on GPT-3 175B at a matched 190 ms/token median latency vs FasterTransformer, with the engine alone (scheduler disabled) measured as slightly worse than the baseline — so the win is entirely scheduling, not kernels. Two things this map must carry with it. The numbers do not compose: 36.9× is against FasterTransformer while PagedAttention's 2–4× is against this, so quoting a stacked multiple chains baselines that do not chain. And the regimes are opposite: continuous batching's one clear loss is max_bs=1 and its weak case is low load — precisely single-user local serving — while speculative decoding is measured at batch size 1 and erodes under concurrency. Many users → batch continuously; one user → speculate. Neither paper says this, because each measured only its own regime.   • One lever in this map costs no quality at all, and it is the only one.
