Four ways a benchmark number lies: the state of honest evaluation

Synthesized 2026-07-20 from evaluation-contamination (status: consolidating — moved there this run, when the LLM-as-judge gap the map had escalated twice was finally closed). Five T1s from five independent groups. As of 2026-07 the four failure modes below are stable; the magnitudes are not — the judge-bias numbers are 2023-vintage and measured on retired models. Check the map's through-line before quoting the percentages.

The claim

This vault's premise is a comparison: open weights, plus your data and your compute, beating a frontier model on a specific task. That premise is a benchmark delta, which means it is only as good as the measurement behind it.

As of 2026-07 the vault can name four independent ways that measurement fails. Independent is the operative word — clearing one says nothing about the other three, and the modal case in this vault carries several at once. What follows is a checklist, in the order it is cheapest to run.

1. Leakage — the model already saw the items

Rebuild the benchmark from scratch and see what survives. GSM1k (Scale AI, NeurIPS 2024) reconstructed GSM8k from zero, difficulty-matched, and re-scored the field: several model families dropped up to 8%, and the drop correlated (Spearman's r² = 0.36) with how readily the model reproduced the original items verbatim. Memorization, measured rather than alleged. [T1]

Two things follow, and the second is the one people skip. Contamination is unevenly distributed — frontier models mostly generalized to the novel items, so "everyone's cheating" is as wrong as "leaderboards are fine." Per-family checking is the honest posture. And a fine-tune is deliberate exposure to task-adjacent data, which makes this the failure mode a fine-tuning practitioner is most likely to cause personally. [T1]

Do: score on freshly-authored, held-out, or post-cutoff items. Cost: building a parallel benchmark doesn't scale, and the cheap detectors (n-gram overlap, membership inference, perturbed evals) have no graded source in this vault yet — that gap is open.

2. Construct mismatch — the score is honest and still wrong

A number can be uncontaminated and measure a narrower surface than the claim it is quoted for. LoRA vs full fine-tuning found the two methods posting equal in-domain accuracy while having learned structurally different solutions — and the LoRA model generalizing worse out of distribution. Nothing about the score was dishonest. It just wasn't measuring the thing it was taken to measure. [T1]

This run added a fresh instance, caught as it entered the vault. YaRN extends LLaMA 2 to 128k context, evidenced by perplexity at 131,072 tokens (2.37 for 7B). Low perplexity means the model is not confused. It does not establish retrieval, reasoning, or instruction-following across that window — and "128k context" is invariably quoted as if it did. [T1]

Do: ask what surface the metric actually covers before repeating the headline. Suite averages hide shape: Molmo-72B tops an 11-benchmark average at 81.2 while sitting 15 points below GPT-4o on MMMU (54.1 vs 69.1). Any "beats frontier on task X" claim needs the per-task breakdown, not the mean. [T1]

3. Infrastructure non-equivalence — same score, different computation

The quietest one. PyTorch FSDP (Meta, VLDB 2023) states plainly that sharded training "cannot ensure… the same mathematical equivalence as local training" for the optimizer step. Because the optimizer works over flattened, evenly-chunked FlatParameters whose layout ignores parameter boundaries, anything needing a parameter's unsharded value — weight or gradient norms, hence norm-based clipping — is computing over the wrong object. [T1]

No quality delta is measured, which is exactly why it belongs on the list: the failure is silent. Two runs can post identical scores having optimized differently, with nothing in the output saying so. The paper calls fixing it an open research question.

Do: treat "we reproduced their number" as weaker evidence than it feels when the training topology differs. Open: which optimizers are affected and by how much is unanswered — and it is unanswerable from benchmark scores, which is the point.

4. Judge bias — the instrument has preferences

The gap this map escalated on 2026-07-18 and again on 2026-07-19, now closed. Judging LLM-as-a-Judge (LMSYS, NeurIPS 2023) cuts deliberately both ways, and the positive half matters as much as the negative.

The judge is legitimate. GPT-4's agreement with human preference exceeds 80% — the same rate humans agree with each other. There is no clean ground truth being approximated here, only a noisy human signal at the same noise floor. "LLM judges are unreliable" is not the correct conclusion. [T1]

The judge is also biased, in three named directions. Position bias (which response is shown first), verbosity bias (longer preferred regardless of quality), and self-enhancement bias — GPT-4 favors its own family's outputs by ~10% win rate; Claude-v1 by ~25% — plus weak math and logic grading. These are systematic and directional, so sampling harder does not wash them out. Position bias is mitigable: swap the order, require consistency. The other two are not cleanly fixable, and the paper says so. [T1]

The reach across this vault is the largest of any single source in it. Every method in the preference thread — DPO, ORPO, SimPO — is ranked by a GPT-4-family judge. So is LLaVA, whose training data and evaluator were both GPT-4, and which reports "85.1% relative to GPT-4." The ~10% self-enhancement figure is the measurement behind a closed loop this vault had flagged on suspicion.

The sharpest instance arrived the same week: SimPO's distinguishing mechanism is length normalization, and its headline win is scored on judges with a documented length preference. "Length-controlled win rate" is a correction the harness applies — not independence from the judge.

Do: when a result is judge-ranked, check whether the judge shares a family with the model, the training data, or the benchmark author. Prefer verifiable checkers where they exist — this is why R1's AIME pass@1 is sturdier evidence than any judged suite average, and it is the cleanest distinction the vault can currently draw between strong and weak results.

What this adds up to

A pattern worth holding as a prior rather than a curiosity: where the eval is honest, the margin shrinks. ORPO looks weakest in its own apples-to-apples table. Molmo's human ratings and its benchmark ranking disagree, and it publishes the disagreement. The vault has three instances now.

The positive model exists. Molmo (Ai2, CVPR 2025) ranks models by >325k human pairwise ratings (~450 matches per pair, Bradley-Terry), reports the automated suite separately, and then publishes where the two rankings disagree rather than quoting whichever flatters it. That is what honest measurement looks like when someone bothers. [T1]

What's still missing

Three things, and the first is the one that would help most.

The protocol. All four failure modes are named; no source gives a practitioner an ordered procedure for clearing them at their own budget. That synthesis is the single highest-value missing artifact in this area.

Whether they compound. A distilled model, evaluated by its teacher's own family, on a contaminated suite, trained under a sharded optimizer, is carrying all four at once — plausibly the modal case here. Every source isolates one.

Honest evaluation without a judge or an annotation budget. Molmo's 325k ratings are the lab-scale answer. The practitioner-scale answer does not exist in this vault, and the premise depends on it.

One dating caveat to carry: the judge-bias magnitudes above come from GPT-4-0314 and Claude-v1, both retired. The failure modes are structural; the percentages are 2023 numbers being applied as a discount on 2026 results, and nobody has re-measured them. Treat them as direction, not size.
