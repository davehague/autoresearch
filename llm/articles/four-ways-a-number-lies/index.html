Four ways a benchmark number lies: the state of honest evaluation

Refreshed 2026-07-27 from evaluation-contamination (status: consolidating). Seven dated bullets landed on the map since this article was written on 2026-07-20 and three of its sections were wrong or stale. The largest change is to failure mode one, which used to end with "the cheap detectors have no graded source yet": they do now, and the most widely deployed of them does not work. Mode three has gone from a warning to a graded ladder. A candidate fifth mode is flagged but not adopted. As of 2026-07 the four modes below are stable and the magnitudes are not — the judge-bias numbers are 2023-vintage, measured on retired models. Check the map's through-line before quoting percentages.

The claim

This vault's premise is a comparison: open weights, plus your data and your compute, beating a frontier model on a specific task. That premise is a benchmark delta, so it is only as good as the measurement behind it.

The vault can name four independent ways that measurement fails, and a fifth candidate under review. Independent is the operative word — clearing one says nothing about the other three, and the modal case here carries several at once. What follows is a checklist, roughly in the order it is cheapest to run.

1. Leakage — the model already saw the items

The mode everyone knows about, and the one where the standing remedy just collapsed.

The failure is real and measurable. GSM1k (Scale AI, NeurIPS 2024) rebuilt GSM8k from zero, difficulty-matched, and re-scored the field: several model families dropped up to 8%, and the drop correlated (Spearman's r² = 0.36) with how readily the model reproduced the original items verbatim. Memorization, measured rather than alleged. Contamination is also unevenly distributed — frontier models mostly generalized to the novel items, so "everyone's cheating" is as wrong as "leaderboards are fine." Per-family checking is the honest posture. [T1]

The standard defense detects verbatim copies and nothing else. This is new on 2026-07-27 and it is the most consequential thing on this map. Essentially every corpus builder decontaminates by n-gram overlap — strip any training document sharing a 13-gram (GPT-3's convention) or a 50-character span (GPT-4's) with a test item. Rephrased samples (Yang, Chiang, Zheng, Gonzalez and Stoica, the LMSYS group) measured that defense against paraphrase and translation. It scores an F1 of exactly 0.000 — in every subject and every language tested, against a random baseline of 0.500 — while scoring a perfect 1.000 on unrephrased items. It is not degraded. It has no signal on a reworded sentence. [T1]

The consequence is demonstrated rather than argued. Fine-tune Llama-2 13B on rephrased test items and MMLU goes 54.8 → 89.9 (GPT-4 scores 86.4) and GSM-8K goes 28.7 → 95.3; CodeLlama-13B takes HumanEval 36.0 → 81.1 (GPT-4: 67.0). Every one of those models passes decontamination clean. The obvious upgrade fails differently: embedding-similarity search works within a language (0.903–0.985 F1) and collapses across one — 0.179 once an item is translated to Chinese, 0.438 once the same problem is rewritten in C — and no single similarity threshold works across MMLU subjects, because their within-subject similarity distributions differ too much.

And it is not an attack. Pointing a stronger detector at corpora people actually train on returns, as a share of HumanEval: The Stack 18.9%, StarCoder-Data 15.9%, CodeAlpaca 12.8%, RedPajama-Data-1T 8.53%. The MATH training split contains 79 rephrasings of its own test split. And the most instructive case is synthetic: CodeAlpaca's 12.8% was generated, by an LLM asked for instruction-following examples from seeds, because that is what "produce problems similar to these" does. Read those numbers as a floor — they sit on top of whatever verbatim contamination the same corpora carry, which the Llama-2 report puts at over 10% of MMLU and the GPT-4 report at 25% of HumanEval. [T1, corpus figures extrapolated from samples]

There is a cheap screen, and it is a prior rather than a verdict. Min-K% Prob (2026-07-24) scores a passage by its lowest-probability tokens and recovers pretraining membership at AUC 0.72 with no corpus and no reference model — just the checkpoint. That gives the first two rungs of an ordered procedure: screen cheaply, rebuild what the screen flags. At 0.72 it prioritizes what to rebuild; it does not certify what to keep, and "we ran a contamination check" will be read as clearance by people who did not read the AUC. [T1]

The two tools do not compose, and the gap is where you are standing. The reliable rephrasing detector needs the training corpus — available to a corpus builder, and to nobody else. Min-K% Prob needs only the model — available to everyone, at the weaker number. So a model builder can use the strong tool and a model consumer cannot, and almost everyone fine-tuning open weights is a consumer auditing a corpus they cannot see, against a contamination class the builder's own pipeline probably missed.

Do: score on freshly-authored, held-out, or post-cutoff items — the authors of the rephrasing paper argue for one-time exams on the Codeforces model, and it is hard to disagree. Screen with Min-K% Prob where you cannot. Treat "we decontaminated" as uninformative unless it says how. Open: nobody has measured the dose — the inflation demonstration is 16 epochs on nothing but rephrased items, so "8–18% of HumanEval is in RedPajama" cannot yet be converted into a discount on any published score.

2. Construct mismatch — the score is honest and still wrong

A number can be uncontaminated and measure a narrower surface than the claim it is quoted for. LoRA vs full fine-tuning found the two methods posting equal in-domain accuracy while having learned structurally different solutions, with the adapter generalizing worse out of distribution. Nothing about the score was dishonest; it just was not measuring the thing it was taken to measure. [T1]

Perplexity is the vault's most-repeated instance, and it has now failed four times in four unrelated literatures. YaRN evidences a 128k context with perplexity at 131,072 tokens, which establishes the model is not confused and says nothing about retrieval or reasoning across the window. A 13-format llama.cpp sweep found four 5-bit quantization formats spanning 0.03 perplexity and 3.4 GSM8K points — perplexity does not rank them. LIMA's authors abandoned perplexity-based early stopping for hand-selection against a 50-example dev set. And rsLoRA is the sharpest case, because the rule splits a single paper: its gradient-collapse mechanism is a training-health claim backed by a theorem (valid), while its "higher rank gives a better model" claim is model selection resting on fine-tuning perplexity (discounted). The rule: perplexity is a training-health signal, not a model-selection criterion — and the useful precedent is that it is a reason to split a source, not to reject one. [T1 × 4]

Suite averages hide shape, and 2026-07-27 supplied the cleanest example yet. Emu3 (BAAI) reports that it "surpasses LLaVA-1.6" on vision-language understanding — a 12-benchmark average of 62.1 against 61.8. The paper also marks five of those benchmarks with an asterisk defined as "the images of related training datasets are observed during training," and those five are exactly where it wins by large margins (ScienceQA 89.2, ChartQA 68.6, DocVQA 76.3). On the unmarked benchmarks it loses MMBench by 8.9, MMMU by 3.5 and MM-Vet by 6.7, while winning two. The authors disclosed the exposure honestly and the summary statistic erased the disclosure. The generalizable rule: an average over benchmarks with heterogeneous training exposure is not a comparison — and the asterisk convention that makes the problem visible is precisely what aggregate leaderboards drop. [T1]

Do: ask what surface the metric covers before repeating the headline, and demand the per-task breakdown rather than the mean. The older instance still stands: Molmo-72B tops an 11-benchmark average at 81.2 while sitting 15 points below GPT-4o on MMMU (54.1 vs 69.1).

3. Infrastructure non-equivalence — same score, different computation

The quietest one, and since 2026-07-26 it is no longer a uniform warning. The vault now holds both ends of it, so it reads as a ladder — and the ladder is directly actionable, because it tells you which memory-saving levers to spend first.

Exact. Gradient checkpointing gives "equivalent weight gradient for training and can always be safely applied." It changes when a value is computed, never what it is. It never belongs in a results footnote, and it is the memory lever to reach for before any other. [T1]

Silently inexact. PyTorch FSDP (Meta, VLDB 2023) states plainly that sharded training "cannot ensure… the same mathematical equivalence as local training" for the optimizer step: the optimizer works over flattened, evenly-chunked parameters whose layout ignores parameter boundaries, so anything needing an unsharded value — weight or gradient norms, hence norm-based clipping — computes over the wrong object. No quality delta has ever been measured, which is why it belongs on this list. The paper calls fixing it an open research question. [T1]

Deliberately inexact. PipeDream's asynchronous pipelining is the worst case and the most honest about it: without weight stashing the update "is not a valid gradient of the loss function for any weight vector"; stashing restores only intra-stage consistency; and vertical sync, the mechanism that would restore data-parallel equivalence, ships off by default. [T1]

Do: rank your memory levers by exactness and spend the exact ones first. A results table mixing a checkpointed run with an unsharded one is comparable; one mixing a ZeRO-3 run with local training is not, and neither run will say so. Open: which optimizers are affected and by how much is unanswerable from benchmark scores, which is the point.

4. Judge bias — the instrument has preferences

Judging LLM-as-a-Judge (LMSYS, NeurIPS 2023) cuts deliberately both ways, and the positive half matters as much as the negative.

The judge is legitimate. GPT-4's agreement with human preference exceeds 80% — the same rate humans agree with each other. There is no clean ground truth being approximated here, only a noisy human signal at the same noise floor. "LLM judges are unreliable" is not the correct conclusion, and that finding now has a second group: LIMA measured crowd-versus-GPT-4 agreement at 78–79% against crowd-versus-crowd at 82%. [T1 × 2]

The judge is also biased, in three named directions. Position bias (which response is shown first), verbosity bias (longer preferred regardless of quality), and self-enhancement bias — GPT-4 favors its own family's outputs by ~10% win rate; Claude-v1 by ~25% — plus weak math and logic grading. These are systematic and directional, so sampling harder does not wash them out. Position bias is mitigable: swap the order, require consistency. The other two are not cleanly fixable, and the paper says so. [T1]

The reach across this vault is the largest of any single source in it. Every method in the preference thread — DPO, ORPO, SimPO — is ranked by a GPT-4-family judge. So is LLaVA, whose training data and evaluator were both GPT-4, and which reports "85.1% relative to GPT-4." SimPO is sharper still: its distinguishing mechanism is length normalization, and its headline win is scored on judges with a documented length preference — "length-controlled win rate" is a correction the harness applies, not independence from the judge.

Verbosity bias was caught in the act on 2026-07-27. CoT-Self-Instruct (FAIR at Meta) reports that its synthetic instructions produce longer responses, that preference training under a length-favouring judge therefore risks length explosion, and that it applies a length-normalized reward with a hand-chosen coefficient of 0.2 to contain it. Observed, named, and patched with a constant — which is roughly the state of the art. [T1]

Do: when a result is judge-ranked, check whether the judge shares a family with the model, the training data, or the benchmark author. Prefer verifiable checkers where they exist — this is why R1's AIME pass@1 is sturdier evidence than any judged suite average, and it remains the cleanest distinction the vault can draw between strong and weak results.

A candidate fifth: recipe leakage

Flagged 2026-07-25, not adopted, because it is one paper in one setting — but it is a different kind of failure from the four above, and that is why it is here. All four modes above are properties of the evaluation. This one is a property of undisclosed model preparation.

Hold the model, the method and the bit budget fixed, and vary only the calibration corpus used to quantize, and AlphaQ's sweep on Mixtral-8×7B at 2.5 bits reads HumanEval 29.3 / 13.7 / 25.1 — a 2.1× swing — with MMLU moving 10.8 points and no corrective rule (GitHub-Code calibration scores worse on HumanEval than generic C4). Two people can publish "Mixtral-8×7B, 2.5-bit," differ by 16 points, and both be honest and reproducible. Perplexity does not catch it. [T1, n=1]

The shape is worth naming even at n=1, because failure mode one now has the same shape: a decontamination claim without its method, and a quantization claim without its calibration corpus, are both reproducible numbers that cannot be compared to anyone else's, because a load-bearing step went unreported. As of 2026-07 that is the single most common way a published comparison in this field misleads without anyone lying.

What this adds up to

A pattern worth holding as a prior rather than a curiosity: where the eval is honest, the margin shrinks. ORPO looks weakest in its own apples-to-apples table. Molmo's human ratings and its benchmark ranking disagree, and it publishes the disagreement. Emu3 marks its own contaminated benchmarks and then wins on the average they carry. Four instances now.

The positive model exists. Molmo (Ai2, CVPR 2025) ranks models by over 325k human pairwise ratings (~450 matches per pair, Bradley-Terry), reports the automated suite separately, and publishes where the two rankings disagree rather than quoting whichever flatters it. That is what honest measurement looks like when someone bothers. [T1]

What's still missing

The protocol. All four failure modes are named; no source gives a practitioner an ordered procedure for clearing them at their own budget. That synthesis remains the single highest-value missing artifact in this area, and 2026-07-27 made it harder rather than easier — the strongest tool for mode one is unavailable to the people who need it.

Whether they compound. A distilled model, evaluated by its teacher's own family, on a contaminated suite, trained under a sharded optimizer, is carrying all four at once — plausibly the modal case here. Every source isolates one.

A demand-side detector for paraphrased contamination. Membership inference scores a passage you supply, and the whole problem with rephrasing is that you do not know which passage to supply. It is not obvious the tool can exist in that form, and nobody has tried in public.

The dose-response curve. Corpus contamination percentages cannot be converted into score discounts without one, and there isn't one.

Honest evaluation without a judge or an annotation budget. Molmo's 325k ratings are the lab-scale answer. The practitioner-scale answer does not exist in this vault, and the premise depends on it.

One dating caveat to carry throughout: the judge-bias magnitudes above come from GPT-4-0314 and Claude-v1, both retired, and the contamination survey is 2023-vintage corpora and models. The failure modes are structural; the percentages are old numbers being applied as a discount on 2026 results, and nobody has re-measured them. Treat them as direction, not size.
