Measured on your own machine: the state of local inference

Synthesized 2026-07-24 from local-inference, the map's first article, written the run the map moved emerging → consolidating. Refreshed 2026-07-29 after nine dated bullets landed in five days. Refreshed again 2026-08-10 after eight more, and that one changed the spine a second time: the largest terms in a local measurement are frequently not the method at all — a process memory allocator outweighed every kernel change on a CPU backend, an attention backend flipped the sign of a cache-compression result, a cache-refresh interval nobody documents controlled an accelerator's output where the documented knob did nothing. Refreshed 2026-08-18 after six more bullets, and this one adds a layer beneath even that. Before you can ask what configuration a number was measured in, you have to ask whether the artifact runs at all — a model card claiming wins over models three times its size shipped a checkpoint that could not be loaded on any device, and which, once patched, ran with its rotary position buffer silently zeroed. This refresh also retracts a claim the 08-10 version made: chunked prefill was described here as solving a multi-tenant problem a single user never has, and it turns out to be a capacity lever on a laptop. Refreshed 2026-08-24 after eight more bullets, and this one adds a third organizing idea rather than another caveat. Three of this map's levers now have measured interior optima — the offload cache budget, the batch size, the thread count — and the tuning procedure everybody uses on all three is monotone: raise it until it stops helping. That procedure finds the optimum and keeps going, and what is on the far side does not look like an over-tuned knob, it looks like the machine running out. Read the map's through-line before trusting any number here in 2027.

The claim

Running a model locally is four decisions — format, precision, runtime, hardware — and the honest state of the evidence is that each is well sourced in isolation, almost nothing is sourced in combination, and the configuration underneath all four is unreported almost everywhere. The format half settled early: GGUF won local distribution because it is a self-describing single file — typed key-value metadata, mmap loading, parseable without a library — so a fine-tuned model ships as a file copy and a conversion command. [T2]

The physics under everything else is one sentence, from FlashAttention: on modern accelerators arithmetic is nearly free and moving data is the bill. [T1] Every technique below is a way of moving fewer bytes, and most of them pay for it in arithmetic.

That sentence needs a qualifier as of 2026-08, and it is worth having early because everything below inherits it. "Decode is memory-bound, so shrinking the weights buys speed" is a fact about the kernel, not a law about decoding. A lookup-table kernel for ultra-low-bit inference that computes products from a table instead of dequantizing to FP16 first — with the column scale folded into the table so it never enters the inner loop — reports >99.8% GPU busy time against FP16's 67–87%, with correspondingly low memory-bandwidth utilization, and the observable tell is that its speedup persists at large batch instead of fading the way weight-only quantization's does. It has left the regime. Every runtime a reader of this article actually runs — llama.cpp, MLX, vLLM's common paths — is dequantize-then-GEMM, so the premise holds locally today, and it holds for a reason that could change. [T1]

That gives the article its first organizing idea. Ask what currency a speedup spends, and do not assume two techniques spending the same currency will add. A decode step has a finite amount of idle compute to sell. Quantization sells it. Speculation sells it. Sell it twice and the second sale fails — measurably, and worst on the smallest machine.

The second organizing idea is name the configuration your number was measured in, or the number is uninformative. Not the method's hyperparameters — the layer below: the allocator, the attention backend, the threading build, the batch size, the cache-refresh schedule, and as of this refresh the kernel. Every one of those has now been shown, in this vault, to move a local result by more than the methods being compared.

The third is new in this refresh and it is about procedure rather than about reporting. Several of the knobs on this page have interior optima, and the way you are tuning them will walk past. Three independent measurements now say the same thing. Raising an offloaded expert-cache budget past the point where the OS page cache still has headroom makes decode slower — a 90 GiB budget losing to a 24 GiB one on a 125 GB machine. Raising batch size on an SSD-offloaded mixture-of-experts goes 0.128 → 0.189 → 0.087 tok/s at B=1/8/32, so the peak is at 8 and B=32 is worse than serial. And thread count on a hybrid performance/efficiency-core part inverts with the cache-hit regime: more threads win while you are missing, fewer win once you are hitting. In every case the standard sweep — increase until the improvement stops — terminates on the far side of the knee, and the far side reads like a hardware limit rather than a mis-set parameter. [T1] Tune these three by bisection, and re-tune the thread count at steady state rather than during warm-up.

What to do

Verify the model runs correctly before you evaluate whether it is good. This is new, it costs a minute, and it is now the first step. A deployment study of a 3B agentic model on Apple Silicon found five independent bugs in a released checkpoint, each reproduced against the unmodified download and cited to a source line. Two are worth memorizing as a class. One fired during model construction, before device placement, so the checkpoint could not be loaded on any device — its model card nevertheless reported competitive-or-better results against Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B. The other is worse because it is silent: the rotary inv_freq buffer loaded as all zeros and was never repopulated, so attention received no positional information and the model produced fluent, positionally-incoherent text. No exception, no warning — the failure reads as a weak model rather than a broken one. The inference is not that vendors falsify results. It is that the benchmark number and the runnable artifact come out of different pipelines, and only one of them is routinely tested: the card's numbers were plausibly earned on an internal stack with its own loader and harness, while what ships is a checkpoint plus trust_remote_code files pinned against a library version that moves underneath them. For any model with rotary positions, check the position buffers are non-zero after load. [T1]

Budget activation memory at the architecture's effective depth, not its physical depth. The same study prices a trade this article had no evidence on. A Looped Transformer buys effective depth without parameters by feeding hidden states through the same layer stack twice — and prefill peak memory is dominated by materializing an O(prompt_len²) attention-score tensor per pass, so the loop doubles peak prefill memory against a non-looped model of the same parameter count. On a datacenter card that is noise. On unified memory shared with the OS and with no page-out path, it is binding: naive prefill of a 3B model in bf16 on a 32 GiB M2 Max cannot process an 8,192-token prompt at batch 1. A model chosen because 3B fits on a laptop could not read a prompt long enough to do the agentic work it was trained for, and nothing in the model card says so. This is the same shape as every other entry here — a gain measured in one currency reappearing as a cost in another — with parameter count as the currency bought and activation memory as the currency spent. [T1]

Start at Q4_K_M and only move if you measured a reason. The 13-format llama.cpp sweep on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct puts 4-, 5-, 6- and 8-bit all within roughly half an aggregate point of FP16's 69.47 across GSM8K, HellaSwag, IFEval, MMLU and TruthfulQA — Q4_K_M scores 69.15 at 69.4% smaller. Above 4-bit you are paying disk and bandwidth for nothing measurable. [T1]

Treat 3-bit as task-conditional, not as broken. The same sweep: at Q3_K_S, GSM8K falls 9.3 points (77.63 → 68.31) and MMLU 4.2, while HellaSwag falls 0.6. Precision is load-bearing for multi-step arithmetic and instruction compliance and close to free for single-step commonsense — so a quant level that costs nothing on a classification or chat workload can cost a tenth of a math benchmark. Within 3-bit it is a slope, not a cliff: Q3_K_L recovers most of Q3_K_S's loss for four points of file size. [T1] And it is size-conditional as well as task-conditional: a signal-noise decomposition of quantization error across Qwen3 0.6B–14B finds error is not merely introduced but propagated, with smaller models in sustained amplification where larger ones preserve or mildly attenuate. The models that most need the lever are the ones it damages most, and the disadvantage compounds at two stages — a noisier source and a multiplying network. A 14B at Q3 and a 1B at Q3 are not the same decision. The cleanest confirmation arrived from an unrelated direction: an ultra-low-bit LUT method holding algorithm, bit budget, calibration set and kernel constant across three sizes costs 7.92 average points at 7B, 6.21 at 13B and 4.20 at 70B. Read the blunt version: ultra-low-bit is for making a large model fit, never for making a small one smaller — at 7B it spends eight points to land below where the four-bit default already sits. [T1]

Test the build you are about to ship with a few hundred paired decisions — it is the cheapest honest instrument that exists, and it is new. Everything above scores a benchmark; the thing that breaks under compression is not what a benchmark reads. A study of quantization damage across 16 models, 8 families and three quantizers — including llama.cpp's own released GGUF builds — measures the margin of a two-way decision, the model's score for the option it picks minus the score of its best alternative, on the same items before and after quantization. Quantization does not add noise to that margin. It multiplies it, by a factor that collapses with bit-width: median 0.86 at 4 bits, 0.33 at 3, 0.00 at 2. Against three additive accounts by BIC, the multiplicative one wins 85% of damaged cells and every cell in the tool-calling and safety families. So a confident decision is not a protected one — everything contracts together, the ordering survives longest, and what fails first is anything reading the gap's size. Concretely, at 3 bits an agent stops calling tools in 96.3% of should-call cases while which tool it picks is untouched, and the same items flip identically under two different quantizers. The practical part: a few hundred paired items scored at the first token, no generation and no judge, predicts held-out flip rates within a median 1.8 percentage points. Run it per model and per bit-width — the fitted constants do not transfer between models (0.2–8.5 points of error at 4 bits, 18–33 at 3). [T1]

And when a build is damaged, spend a bit rather than a heuristic. The same study's cost-matched repair sweep, over 501 severely damaged conditions: one additional bit recovers a median 0.305 of flipped decisions, and five plausible repairs fail to beat it — importance-based weight protection in four variants including a random-weight control, full-precision trailing blocks, margin-gradient rounding, dithering, attention-only protection. Only three help, narrowly: an activation-aware quantizer at the collapse point specifically, non-uniform per-layer allocation, and taking the KV cache from 16 to 8 bits, which is free at 4-bit weights. [T1]

Do not pick a format from the perplexity column. The four 5-bit variants span 0.03 perplexity and 3.4 GSM8K points. An order of magnitude wider and from a different toolchain: a study of speculative decoding on quantized models reports one Llama-3-8B quantization ladder spanning 0.56 WikiText2 perplexity and 10.98 HumanEval points. Perplexity is a token-averaged likelihood, dominated by the many easy tokens and blind to the few hard ones — and multi-step reasoning and code fail on exactly the hard ones. Most community comparisons publish perplexity and nothing else. [T1]

Pick your quant for your prompt-to-generation ratio, not just your VRAM. Decode is bandwidth-bound, so it speeds up monotonically as the file shrinks: 2.83 → 9.91 tok/s from F16 to Q3_K_S, a 3.5× span. Prefill is compute-bound and does the opposite — throughput peaks mid-ladder (Q4_0 at 97.35 tok/s against F16's 79.57) and then falls to 57.39 at Q3_K_S, 28% below full precision, because dequantizing packed weights costs more than the smaller read saves. A long-prefill RAG workload and a long-generation agent workload should not run the same file. [T1] This was one CPU study until 2026-08-24; it is now two, from unrelated groups, on different instruction sets and three bits lower. A Graphcore/Arm sub-3-bit vision-language pipeline finds the same split at 2.68 bits per weight: its format wins batch-size-1 decode (36.8 tok/s against INT8's 26.4 on a Graviton4) and loses slightly at both text prefill and vision-encoder prefill, where the operation is compute-bound and unpacking is pure added work. The mechanism is the same one and the regime boundary is where the batch dimension stops being one. [T1]

Report your allocator, your attention backend and your threading build, or expect to be un-reproducible. A year of Arm CPU optimization work on vLLM found its largest single win was replacing the process memory allocator — mimalloc for glibc malloc in PyTorch, worth 2.3× offline throughput and about 7× at low concurrency on Llama 3.1 8B — against 9% for an LSE-atomics OpenMP runtime and 12% end-to-end for hand-written paged-attention kernels. In a different layer, the attention backend was worth 2.5× in the opposite direction on a KV-compression method, turning a +9.8% throughput result under eager attention into −59% under a fused kernel. Neither term appears in any comparison this article is built on. The pattern in the Arm numbers also corroborates the decode accounting from an unusual side: every win is largest at low concurrency, which is the single-stream regime where nothing hides memory-system latency. [T2]

On Arm CPUs there is now a third quantization ladder, and llama.cpp cannot express it. The shipped sequence is W8A8 then W4A8 — per-token activations, channelwise weights, SMMLA and KleidiAI INT4 micro-kernels — at up to 6.2× throughput and 7.8× TPOT over a late-2025 BF16 baseline. Read that as a third measured local backend rather than an improvement to llama.cpp or MLX, and read it with the caveat the post itself invites: no quality number is attached to any rung. [T2]

On Apple Silicon, size by GPU core count for batched encoder work and by nothing for decode. Across an M2, M4, M3 Pro, M4 Pro and M3 Ultra — 10 to 80 GPU cores, 16 to 512 GB — per-core throughput reads 922 / 1,341 / 1,189 / 1,348 / 1,048 tok/s at 97.7–99.7% accelerator utilization: a 1.46× spread against an 8× spread in width. That is the simplest sizing rule on this map and it is bounded precisely — the workload is compute-bound and batched, and autoregressive decode is the opposite regime. Pair it with the finding that the same 4-bit weights run faster under MLX than under llama.cpp (vLLM-MLX on Apple Silicon): core count predicts the ceiling, the runtime decides how much of it you get. [T1]

Read every "runs on a laptop" claim at p99. In the same on-device omni-modal study, the 16 GB M2 successfully indexes the largest corpus and posts a 13,261 ms p99 for a single-edit re-encode against the M3 Ultra's 2,916, plus 1.2-second p99 image queries, because image and audio paths do not batch. The small machine does not refuse the workload; it stops being usable, and only the tail shows it.

On a memory-constrained machine a 4-bit path can be the higher-quality path. The same study scans a 4-bit resident replica and rescores the top 4,096 candidates against exact vectors paged from disk: Recall@10 of 0.9828 / 0.9797 / 0.9672 / 0.9687 at 250K / 500K / 1M / 4M vectors against an exhaustive bf16 scan's 0.9812 / 0.9656 / 0.9609 / 0.9594. The quantized path wins at every size but the smallest and the margin grows. The mechanism is not fidelity — what 4 bits buys is keeping the working set in the fast path, and a full-precision scan degrades as it spills. The crossover is machine-dependent, at 500K–1M chunks on a 512 GB machine and by 250K on a 16 GB one. Read it as a systems comparison rather than a like-for-like precision one: it is a two-stage estimator against a one-stage baseline. [T1]

Pin and report which kernel ran, or your quantization comparison has an unlogged variable in it. This is new and it is uncomfortable, because it applies retroactively to most of the numbers above. A kernel-divergence study on an RTX 4090 holds the checkpoint, the quantization scheme, the prompts, the card, the engine (pinned by container digest) and greedy decoding fixed, swaps only which INT8 general matrix-multiply kernel the engine selects, and gets zero matching sequences out of 64 — while each arm reproduces itself bit-for-bit across cold restarts. Two deterministic machines that disagree, not one noisy one. The cause is localized rather than asserted: the integer accumulator is exact and order-independent under a verified no-overflow bound, so it is eliminated by proof, leaving scale application and output rounding — and rounding weight scales to powers of two makes the kernels bit-identical across every layer measured. The practical rule: a scheme-versus-scheme delta smaller than the kernel effect is unreadable unless the kernel is pinned, and several deltas on this page are in that range. The positive version arrived four days later and is the more useful half. A disk-streamed MoE serving study pinned its integer kernels bitwise-identical across aarch64 and x86-64 — proving exact lane-sum equivalence between the two instruction sets' dot-product formulations — and every behavioural fixture reproduced exactly on both, including one sitting on a decision knife-edge, while the transformer stack around those kernels still drifted 1.1% in perplexity from rounding in attention and normalization. Pin the integer kernel and behaviour crosses an instruction-set boundary; leave it free and behaviour does not survive a kernel swap on one card. [T1]

Match the serving lever to your regime, because the four do not overlap. Iteration-level scheduling (continuous batching) reschedules after every token — 36.9× throughput on GPT-3 175B at matched median latency, and its one clear loss is max_bs=1. PagedAttention pages the KV cache like virtual memory — near-zero waste, 2–4× throughput. Prefix caching keeps a radix tree over cached KV blocks and reuses any exact shared prefix — 74.1% hit rate on Vicuna-33B across a month of production serving, cutting first-token latency 1.7×, and costing under 0.3% when there is no reuse to find. Speculative decoding attacks serial depth for one stream. So: many users → batch continuously; one user, single-shot → speculate; one user, multi-turn → cache the prefix. Prefix reuse is the only one of the four whose mechanism predicts a single-user win. [T1]

If you need latency on one stream, speculate — but not the way the defaults tell you to, if your weights are 4-bit. Speculative decoding remains the only lever here that costs no quality at all: a small draft proposes tokens, the target verifies them in one parallel pass, and a modified rejection rule makes the output provably identical in distribution — 2.8–3.4× at batch size 1. On a 4-bit weight model, draft shallower and flatter than the defaults: halving a tree-shaped draft moves a 4-bit 8B from 2.7× up to 2.9× while moving the FP16 model 2.3× down to 2.1×, and cutting draft passes from six to three takes the 4-bit model to 3.4×. Taken to the limit, plain sequential speculation beats the tree-based state of the art on a 4-bit 70B (2.31× against 1.80×) despite drafting eleven times slower. [T1]

Budget for the KV cache separately, and know it now has four levers. Once weights are quantized the cache is what fills the card, and it grows with context and concurrency rather than parameter count. Architecture (GQA — 8 KV heads at roughly MHA quality), precision (KIVI — 2-bit KV, and TurboQuant — 3.5 bits per channel at parity with a 16-bit cache on a 13-task long-context benchmark with no calibration data), placement (PagedAttention), and now eviction. Two trade-offs worth naming. The models you actually run already ship with GQA, so KV-quantization gains measured on MHA models are upper bounds. And the 3.5-bit result is not reachable from llama.cpp — its non-integer widths come from splitting channels into outlier and non-outlier sets (32 channels at 3 bits, 96 at 2), which the K-quant menu has no way to express. [T1]

Treat eviction as a quality-spending lever, and read the eviction literature's own tables. A counter-causal surprise criterion genuinely improves on what it replaces — but the source is honest enough to publish the row that undercuts its headline, and at the cache sizes a consumer card actually runs, eviction is buying memory with quality rather than finding free redundancy. Spend the bits before you spend the tokens. [T1]

Do not assume a sparse model runs like its active parameter count on a small device. See below. The floor is now measured rather than feared: a router-locality study runs Qwen3-235B-A22B at Q4_K_M (134 GB) on an RTX 3070 (8 GB) at 0.44 tok/s warm, matching a bytes-per-token ÷ bandwidth model exactly over a hierarchy measured at ~448 / ~50 / ~2.4 GB/s. Two orders of magnitude below reading speed, and — the part worth internalizing — there is no mystery overhead to optimize away. The number is the hierarchy. [T1]

If the sparse model is the point, make the quantized block the same bytes everywhere. The one design that measurably improves that floor does not change the bits much; it changes how many times they are touched. A tied nine-level quantizer folds two ternary planes into a single 4-bit code plane and then makes that plane the persistent representation — the same 4.0625-bit blocks on disk, in the expert-cache slab, and at the integer kernel's input, with no transcode at any tier — so an expert-projection miss is one contiguous read by construction. Serving a 284B-parameter mixture-of-experts model from SSD on a 64 GB M1 Max against a 4.5-bit Q4_K baseline: −10.6% bytes read per round, +10.8% more experts pinned in the identical RAM budget (1,344 vs 1,213), 6.7% faster decode, 9% smaller files, with no detected fidelity difference against the model creator's own serving API. The returns track the bits-per-weight ratio almost exactly, which is the sign the mechanism is the one claimed. The same principle was measured one tier up (GPU, pinned host memory) by an expert-slot cache study; read the pair as pay the packing once and push "once" as far down the hierarchy as the artifact goes, including the file on disk. [T1]

Read the phone numbers as feasibility, not as usability, and check which alternative you are actually choosing between. The Arm sub-3-bit pipeline puts an 11B vision-language model in 3.73 GB at 2.68 bits per weight, decoding at a median 3.8 tok/s on a Pixel 8a — below reading speed. The comparison that matters is not 2.7 bits against 8: at INT8 that model does not fit in the phone's memory at all. On this class of device aggressive quantization is frequently not a quality trade, it is the difference between a workload and no workload, which is a different decision from the one the quant-ladder table above describes. [T1]

If you deploy for one language or one domain, most of your vocabulary is dead weight you can delete. This is the newest lever here and the least measured. A corpus-driven vocabulary pruning study keeps the union of token IDs appearing in a sample of the traffic it will actually serve, slices the embedding matrix and the language-model head by that index set, and resizes in place: 128,000+ tokens down to roughly 10,000–26,000, embedding layer down about 60%, no quality loss after a short fine-tune — and on two of three architectures, no fine-tune is needed at all (26.43 BLEU against 26.82 untouched; 24.95 against 24.86). The third collapses to 13.65, for a reason you can check before starting: a tightly tied decoder embedding and output head. Three deflations, all mine rather than the paper's. The evidence is 418M–600M encoder-decoder translation models on one language pair, so the transfer to a decoder-only model is an argument, not a measurement. 60% of an embedding layer is a much smaller share of an 8B model than of a 418M one, making this a gigabyte-scale constant rather than a proportional saving. And the failure mode is hard: a proper noun absent from the sampling corpus becomes unrepresentable, not merely worse. It composes trivially with the finding that the language-model head is the largest tensor 4-bit quantization skips — one deletes rows, the other shrinks bits — and nobody has composed them. [T1, discounted]

Where the evidence pushes back

The two levers everyone stacks do not stack, and a consumer card is where it shows. Speculative decoding and weight quantization both convert idle arithmetic into saved memory traffic. Quantization gets there first, because it changes the cost of the forward pass itself; speculation then finds a pass that is much cheaper in bytes and unchanged in FLOPs. Applying EAGLE-2 to a 4-bit Llama-3-8B yields a 1.3× relative speedup where the FP16 model gets 2.2× on an A100, because the target's verification-to-decoding time ratio stays below 1.2 for FP16 and 8-bit, reaches 1.8 for 4-bit on an A100, and about 2.3 on an RTX 3090. Across the whole grid, integration beats either technique alone in every cell but one: 4-bit weights on the 8B model on the RTX 3090 — precisely what a reader of this article would run. [T1]

The currency test now has a positive case, which is the reason to keep it. A negative rule that only ever forbids things is hard to trust. Feed-forward activation sparsity spends memory rather than arithmetic — an INT4 proxy ranks which SwiGLU intermediate channels matter for this token, and only those are computed exactly — so the test predicts it should stack where speculation and quantization contend, and it does. That is the first lever on this map measured in composition rather than isolation, and the first confirmation that the currency framing predicts rather than merely explains. [T1]

And it has a hard boundary: offload a mixture-of-experts model and the scarce resource stops being arithmetic entirely. On DeepSeek-V2-Lite, routed experts are 91.6% of the weights and every token touches top-6 of 64, so the natural deployment streams most of them — and then a self-speculative draft-expert study of offloaded MoE decoding puts expert loading at 87% of profiled decode time on an RTX 4090 and 49% on a phone NPU running llama.cpp at Q4_0. Speculation's founding assumption is dense-specific: verifying a block reuses the same feed-forward weights in a dense model, but here the verifier must load the union of every expert any drafted token routes to — 2.4× a token-decode at block length 4 and 5.8× at 16. The standard advice to lengthen the speculation window is backwards in this regime, and the training-free version is a net loss (1.19 tok/s against an unspeculated 2.19). [T1, discounted]

On the edge, cost tracks total parameters, not active ones. An empirical study on consumer and edge hardware ran the MoE pitch through llama.cpp at Q4_K_M: OLMoE-1B-7B is 10% behind Llama-3.2-1B on an Apple M2 Pro (114.5 vs 127.4 tok/s) and 31% behind on a Jetson Orin Nano (22.9 vs 33.4), where it also loses to Qwen2.5-1.5B — a model with more active parameters — at 2.1× the energy per token and 8.01 GiB peak resident memory against an 8 GB ceiling. Patching llama.cpp to wall-clock each decode-graph node puts routing under 9% of MoE-block time, so this is not a kernel awaiting optimization. Sparsity is an answer denominated in FLOPs, and FLOPs are not what an SBC lacks. The MoE decoupling itself is a pretraining-FLOPs claim and is untouched; what fails is the inference corollary a local user would draw. [T1]

That mechanism stands — but the footprint it was measured against turned out to be a variable, and by roughly half. The 8.01 GiB above is a Q4_K_M reading, and a K-quant compresses uniformly. About 93% of a sparse model's parameters sit in the routed expert bank, which is also the tissue an offloaded-MoE consumer-inference study found tolerates compression best — 2-bit experts cost 3.85 MMLU points where 2-bit attention, embeddings and gates cost 11.5. A complete-expert W4A16 runtime that quantizes only the routed bank and leaves the router, attention, embeddings, norms and head in BF16 puts the same OLMoE-1B-7B fully resident in 4.061 GiB at 31.923 tok/s — 47.4% above its own BF16 baseline — for −0.546 aggregate points across 12,450 paired multiple-choice questions (95% bootstrap [−0.964, −0.137], McNemar p = 0.0112, with the damage falling entirely on HellaSwag). That is an NVIDIA L4, not a Jetson, so the throughput comparison does not transfer and the edge deficit above is untouched. What transfers is the arithmetic: the footprint that was pressed against an 8 GB ceiling had about a factor of two of unclaimed headroom in it, and the practitioner ordering is shrink the bank before you build a streaming system — the resident 4-bit point beats every offloaded configuration of the same model on both throughput and latency. Two riders worth carrying. A matched-cache ablation says roughly half of what an offloaded 4-bit MoE runtime delivers is grouped fused execution rather than the bits (1.97×, identical weights and cache), so read end-to-end offloading numbers with that split in mind. And the transfer bill nobody else prints is large: 21.44 GiB host-to-device per 128-token request at 16 resident expert slots, on plain LRU with no prefetching. [T1] The whole scheme is also, incidentally, the coarse version of the per-role bit split this map has wanted — 4-bit routed experts, full precision elsewhere, decided purely by tensor name, which is the granularity llama.cpp's per-tensor overrides already operate on even though the K-quant menu cannot express per-layer assignment. Nobody has built it in GGUF. [T1]

One local lever can evict another, and the first measurement of it hits the lever this map recommends. Prefix reuse is the serving technique a single user actually benefits from — and it only pays while the prefix is resident. Tree speculation over a hybrid-attention backbone allocates transient state per proposal node, out of the same pool the KV cache lives in — up to 24.0 GB in one configuration. Cutting that by 90%, with no change to the drafter, the tree, or the accepted length, takes the prefix-cache hit rate on an agent trace from 58.3–69.7% to 90.8–92.6%, first-token latency down 67.6% and per-token latency down 49.9%. So speculation's bill is not only idle arithmetic; on a machine where memory binds it is paid to the prefix cache, and the two techniques this article recommends for the same single-user regime contend through a channel nobody had named. [T1]

Linear-time sequence models are not a drop-in efficiency substitute at small scale. Measured on an RTX 4090 across six tasks in a PEFT energy-and-memory study: Mamba-1.4B takes 49.4 min on SST-2 where the larger Qwen3-1.7B takes 22.3, at roughly 18% fewer TFLOPs; inference is worse (484 s vs 75 s against TinyLlama-1.1B on a generative task). At 128–512 token sequences the quadratic attention term never dominates, so there is nothing for linear scaling to beat, while the sequential scan is memory-access-bound. The binding fact for this vault is the second one: there is no 4-bit path — no library supports native quantization for these models, attributed to activation outliers absent from attention modules. The counterweight is real: peak VRAM does not follow parameter count (Mamba-2-1.3B at 7,680 MB where Qwen3-1.7B uses 19,098), so under a hard ceiling an SSM can be the model that fits — costing more time and energy, and unquantizable. Every number here is a claim about kernels and libraries in 2026-08. [T1]

A memory budget binds only if it reaches every allocator. What overflows a small machine is rarely the weights, which everyone counts. It is the union of transients nobody counts: a decoded video frame buffer, a precision copy of vision weights made once and never freed, a database compaction that doubles an index while rewriting it, a dtype conversion on a scan matrix costing +777 MB and unbounded until the budget is explicitly routed through it. The fix that generalizes is slab-based conversion — transform in bounded pieces rather than materializing the converted whole — plus re-propagating the budget during transient operations rather than only at startup. [T1]

Almost every serving number in this map is a datacenter number — but chunked prefill is not one of them, and this article said otherwise until now. The 08-10 version filed chunked prefill alongside disaggregated serving as A100-class work solving a multi-tenant interference problem — an arriving request's prefill stalling incumbent decodes — that a single user never creates. That reading of its origin is right and the conclusion drawn from it was wrong. The identical mechanism has a single-user purpose the serving literature had no reason to name: capacity. Processing the prompt in fixed-size chunks with an incrementally grown KV cache bounds the peak attention-score tensor to chunk_size × running-total, independent of prompt length, which converts a hard out-of-memory ceiling into a percentage throughput cost. Measured on a 32 GiB M2 Max over 50 LongBench-Pro samples with 256-token chunks, usable prompt length goes from 4,096 to 11,231 tokens (2.7×) plus 2–4× batch parallelism, for −22.8% throughput at 1,024 tokens and −40.9% at 2,048 — with outputs verified bit-identical against naive prefill, not merely close. On a machine where the alternative is not running at all, that trade is close to unconditional. Know the cost structure, because it explains an odd row: the per-chunk sub-call overhead is fixed per chunk regardless of batch size, so it amortizes at large batch and dominates at small, which is why a 4,096-token prompt at batch 4 runs slower (124.8 tok/s) than batch 1 at 11,231 (142.4). Fusing the sub-calls would remove most of it and nobody has. Disaggregation remains purely a datacenter answer. [T1] The two numbers this article already carried still stand and still have no local measurement: the chunking penalty is about 25% at 512 tokens and near zero at 2048, and tile quantization makes a chunk of 257 cost 32% more than 256 — a cliff from a one-token config change. [T1]

A model's measured capability is conditional on a rendering path, and your eval harness and your application do not share one. The same Apple Silicon study found the model's chat template branching on whether the caller supplied a system message: the auto-insert branch renders the model's own trained-in tool-use prompt with no trailing separator, the caller-supplied branch appends "\n\n". So any caller system message — including "You are an assistant with MCP tools", which every application adds — silently replaces the trained default rather than extending it, and multi-tool output degrades into malformed text. Re-supplying the original text explicitly does not fix it: the two rendered strings differ by exactly two whitespace characters, and the checkpoint's tool-calling reliability is calibrated to the byte sequence rather than the meaning. What lifts this above one vendor's bug is independent corroboration — the same paper reports that llama.cpp PR #26324 documents a mechanically distinct failure in the same pipeline for the same model — <tool_call> emitted with a trailing space instead of a newline for roughly 25% of calls — found by a different party in a different runtime, and notes the template structure is shared with Qwen3-Coder and Qwen3.5-4B. Testable rule: render your production prompt through the model's template and diff it byte-for-byte against the no-system-message output. And when comparing tool-use scores, check whether each model was scored through its own default path — the one that looks worse may be the one whose harness took the other branch. [T1, one checkpoint]

Some of the sharpest numbers on this map were never measured on a machine, and that is a discount rather than a disqualification. An adaptive expert-prefetching study is peer-reviewed, rigorous and entirely simulated — RTL synthesis in TSMC 28 nm, PrimeTime power characterization, Ramulator memory timing, cycle-accurate co-simulation against a platform modeled as RTX-3090-comparable, with the authors stating no prototype exists. Its 26% lower per-token latency and 41% better energy-delay product describe hardware nobody can buy. But simulation is the only instrument that can isolate idle and leakage energy, which turned out to be the entire win (I/O energy rises 48 → 56 mJ while idle/leakage falls 57 → 8 mJ, total 340 → 299), and no consumer-GPU experiment can separate that term. The rule to carry: a simulated result is a mechanism claim, and its magnitudes are conditional on a machine that may never exist. The mechanism — waiting costs static power, so overlap pays even when traffic grows — is the part worth stealing for anything running on a battery. [T1]

Every throughput number here was measured on different silicon, and they are not commensurable. FlashAttention-2 showed kernel tuning does not transfer across GPU generations, and FlashAttention-3 confirmed it by measurement: the A100-tuned kernel reaches 35% of an H100, while a Hopper-native rebuild restores 75%. A tok/s figure is a claim about a kernel, a card, a precision, and which phase was measured. Comparing two runtimes benchmarked on different machines is comparing nothing. [T1]

What is still unsettled

The composition question is now two-for-three and still mostly unasked. Feed-forward sparsity stacks. Speculation and quantization contend. Speculation and prefix reuse contend through memory. That is three pairs measured out of a dozen anyone would try: prefix reuse × quantization (they push opposite ways — reuse cuts prefill, quantization slows prefill and speeds decode), KV quantization × weight quantization, speculation × KV quantization, vocabulary pruning × anything. The currency test predicts which pairs are at risk. Nobody has run the rest.

The measurement this map actually wants remains unpublished, and it is cheap. llama.cpp or Ollama, one user, a multi-turn conversation, with and without prefix reuse, at a GGUF quant level, on one card, reporting first-token latency and decode throughput separately — now with the allocator and attention backend named, which the map did not know to ask for in July. Every ingredient is public and free.

Nobody has written the reporting checklist, and by now the list writes itself. Allocator, attention backend, threading and OpenMP build, batch size, sequence length, the quantization scheme's internal bit allocation, any cache-refresh or state-staleness schedule, and — after this refresh — the generation cap and the exact rendered prompt. max_tokens earns its place on evidence: a budget-dependent ranking study varying nothing but the cap across seven levels, four models and three reasoning benchmarks (56,476 inferences at temperature 0) finds the best model changes on all three, with several crossings significant under McNemar, and 3.6–25.8% of items going from right to wrong as the budget grows after truncation is controlled. It is a setting a local practitioner picks by feel on the assumption that generosity is free. Every item on this list has been shown in this vault to move a local result, and not one comparison here reports even half of them.

Vocabulary pruning has never been run on a decoder-only model. The mechanism has no obvious reason to be architecture-specific, the disqualifying condition (tied embeddings) is checkable in one line, and the composition with language-model-head bit-compression is arithmetic nobody has done. It is plausibly the cheapest unrun experiment on this map.

A new artifact class arrived and this map cannot say anything about it. DiffusionGemma ships open weights under Apache 2.0 at 25.2B total and 3.8B active — call it 13 GB of 4-bit weights, over a 12 GB card and under a 24 GB one — with GGUF builds, and it does not decode autoregressively at all. Its throughput claim (~1,500 output tok/s) is an H100 number at low batch size, and the vault's own MoE evidence predicts the offloaded case is where it breaks, because a 256-token denoising canvas activates the expert union over 256 positions rather than one. Tokens per second and peak memory on one consumer card is the single most useful number this map could acquire next.

llama.cpp is sourced as an instrument, not as a system. Several studies run it; none describes it. The ggml graph, the CPU/Metal kernel split, threading and batching behaviour, mmap strategy, Ollama's serving layer — no graded source, and that gap is harder to close from papers because the documentation is the repository.

The quant ladder is one model on one CPU. Whether the task-shaped 3-bit damage holds at 70B, whether it holds on an MoE base, and where the prefill/decode crossover sits on Apple silicon rather than a 96-core Xeon are all open. The IQ series and MXFP4 rungs are unmeasured entirely, and the provenance is the weakest T1 in this article — a single-author preprint with no venue.

Neither KV-quantization source reports a single speed number, and none of the cache work reports a decision-level quality number either. Both are footprint-and-quality-as-accuracy, which now looks like the wrong instrument: the margin study's whole finding is that argmax-scored accuracy survives compression while the margin behind it does not. Running a paired margin set against an aggressive cache compressor is GPU-minutes and would tell us whether the cache budget and the weight budget damage a model in the same currency.

The Arm CPU rung had no quality numbers at all, and now has one — for a rung below the ones in question. W8A8 and W4A8 are still reported as throughput ladders by their vendor, in a format family llama.cpp cannot express, with nothing said about what either costs on GSM8K or IFEval. The sub-3-bit pipeline supplies a quality number two rungs lower — 0.083 average degradation across four visual-QA benchmarks at 2.68 bits — but on a vision-language model, against a vector format nobody else ships, with a quantization-aware training run behind it. It shows sub-3-bit Arm inference is viable; it does not tell you what the vendor's W4A8 costs, which remains the open question.

Below three bits the format and the procedure stop being separable levers, and nobody has swept the floor. The same pipeline reports the widest same-rate quality spread this vault holds: at roughly 2.7 bits, a rate-matched uniform integer grid under GPTQ scores 0.018, a vector format under GPTQ 0.340, and that format under quantization-aware training 0.661 — 37× at one bit budget. The scalar arm's collapse is not a procedure failure; it is a procedure with nowhere to put the model. What is missing is the equivalent sweep at 2 bits and 1.58 bits with format and procedure each held fixed in turn, which is the experiment that says whether "train into it" has a floor of its own. This strand now has its own map — training-time quantization was forked out of post-training quantization on 2026-08-24 — and none of it has been measured on a consumer card.

The two cache-budget results point opposite ways and nobody has reconciled them. The offload literature on this map says more resident state is better, because a resident expert is one you do not fetch. The disk-streamed measurement says the expert cache and the OS page cache compete for one pool of RAM, so past a knee more budget is worse. Both are right in their own configuration and the crossover is where a practitioner lives. The missing measurement is a budget sweep with page-cache occupancy instrumented alongside hit rate — a day's work on hardware anyone reading this owns, and it would turn a knee that currently has to be discovered by bisection into a number you can compute.

The trajectory

As of 2026-08, this map spent its first six weeks reasoning about local deployment from datacenter evidence, spent two replacing it, and has spent the last two discovering that the replacement was measured on sand. The 4-bit default survived translation into K-quants intact and has now been re-derived from decision behaviour rather than from a loss. The MoE inference story did not survive contact with an 8 GB device. Two of the four serving layers turned out to solve a multi-tenant problem a single user does not have. The assumption that these techniques compose failed its first test and has since passed its second and failed its third. And underneath all of it, the terms that actually moved the biggest numbers this month were an allocator, an attention backend, and a refresh interval.

The 08-18 refresh adds a step underneath the whole checklist and retracts one claim. The step: a released checkpoint may not run, or may run silently wrong, while its card reports honestly-obtained wins — so "measured on your own machine" now begins with does it load, and are the position buffers non-zero. The retraction: chunked prefill was filed here as a multi-tenant answer irrelevant to one user, and it is a capacity lever that turns an out-of-memory ceiling into a 20–40% throughput tax on a laptop. Two smaller corrections point the same way — the MoE footprint that failed on an 8 GB device had half its headroom unclaimed in the quantization scheme, and the memory-bound premise underneath every recommendation here is a property of the kernels we happen to run rather than of decoding.

The 08-24 refresh does not retract anything, and adds one thing and takes one away. What it adds is a procedural finding rather than a technical one: three of this map's levers have measured interior optima — the offload cache budget, the batch size on an offloaded sparse model, the thread count on a hybrid core part — and on all three the standard monotone sweep terminates past the knee, where the result reads like a hardware ceiling rather than a mis-set knob. That is a different kind of error from the ones this article has been cataloguing. An unreported allocator makes a number uninformative; a monotone sweep on a non-monotone knob makes it wrong in a predictable direction, and the fix is bisection rather than disclosure. What it takes away is some of the comfort in "pin your configuration": a kernel-divergence result shows two INT8 kernels agreeing on zero of 64 sequences with everything else pinned down to the container digest, so the configuration list this article has been growing all month was still missing its most load-bearing item. The consolation, from a disk-streamed serving study four days later, is that the fix is tractable and narrow: pin the integer kernel bitwise and behaviour survives an entire instruction-set change, even where the model around it does not reproduce to the last bit.

None of that makes the stack worse. It makes the arithmetic legible, which is the only thing that lets you predict a result instead of benchmarking your way to it. Three rules to carry forward. Name the currency each lever spends, and be suspicious of any two levers that spend the same one. Name the configuration your number was measured in — including the kernel — because the gap between "this technique works" and "this technique works on the machine you own, with the allocator you happen to link against, alongside the other thing you already turned on" is where every remaining open question here lives. And assume a knob is non-monotone until you have seen its far side, because on this map three of them already are, and the sweep that would have told you goes one step too few.
