Rubble below three bits: the state of training-time quantization

Synthesized 2026-10-23 from training-time-quantization (status: consolidating), forked 2026-08-24 from post-training-quantization on the night the question of when a model adapts to a quantization grid became too thick to keep filed as an allocation question. The parent asks where the bits go and what a frozen checkpoint lost under a fixed budget; this map asks what the model is trained against while it adapts to the grid — teacher choice, loss shape, which tensors stay frozen, when training stops, and now, as of tonight, which format family the grid itself belongs to.

Train into the format, not into a conversion afterward

Imperative 1 [T1, strong]: below roughly three bits, format and procedure stop being separable levers and start multiplying each other. On Llama 3.2 11B Vision at ~2.7 bits per weight, a rate-matched uniform integer grid under GPTQ scores 0.018 average across four visual-QA benchmarks; the same rate under a vector format scores 0.340; the same vector format under quantization-aware training scores 0.661 — a 37× spread at one fixed bit budget. Above three bits this is a modest efficiency argument; below it, it is the difference between a shippable model and rubble. The format decides whether there is enough representable structure left for the training procedure to do anything with it.

What the model trains against, not just what grid it lands on

Imperative 2 [T1, single-study]: in a multi-step compression pipeline, the teacher is a free variable, and the obvious choice — the intermediate checkpoint you already have — is usually the worst one. Under compound compression (a structural step then a quantization step), the intermediate full-precision checkpoint was never independently trained; it is itself a distillation-recovered approximation carrying the compressed architecture's ceiling. Supervising the quantized student from that checkpoint caps the student there. Supervising from the original uncompressed model instead — architectures need not match, since distillation only needs logits — lets a 4-bit artifact match or beat the bfloat16 checkpoint it was quantized from on 7 of 9 benchmarks, at roughly 4× less weight memory. The decisive head-to-head against distilling from the recovered checkpoint has never been run, so this is a demonstration attached to a motivation, not a closed comparison.

Imperative 3 [T1, strong]: quantization-aware distillation (QAD) recovers short-form QA well below three bits but leaves reasoning badly broken, and the fix is training on the model's own quantized rollouts rather than fixed corpus text. On-policy low-bit reasoning recovery (T1) finds QAD alone holds 82% average BF16 retention on short-form QA across four models but only 35% on MATH-500 — traced to quantization-amplified exposure bias, since QAD trains on fixed prefixes while the quantized model's own errors compound along the trajectories it actually generates at deployment. Adding an on-policy stage — sample through the deployment-quantized path, score against a frozen full-precision teacher plus verifier rewards — lifts MATH-500 retention to 70% and HumanEval to 91%, at 14-23× fewer GPU-hours than the QAD stage it follows.

Imperative 4 [T1, suggestive, narrows but does not close]: that on-policy fix's benefit shrinks sharply as bit-width falls, and the shrinkage repeats in a from-scratch QAT setting, not just the post-hoc QAD setting that discovered it. Adding the same self-rollout reverse-KL recovery stage after block-wise QAT beats fixed-completion recovery by 2.90 points average at W3A16 (57.28 vs. 54.38) but only 0.44 points at W2A16 (32.52 vs. 32.08), trailing the baseline on three of six benchmarks at the lower bit-width — the exact regime where the strand's core claim promises the most. Whether this is a hard quantization ceiling, a reverse-KL-specific limitation, or a token-selection problem was this map's sharpest open question as of 2026-10-09.

Imperative 5 [T1, strong, newest]: loss design — not more compute — is the better bet so far, and a candidate-wise correction wins cleanly at exactly the bit-width where plain on-policy recovery nearly vanished. OnQ (T1) replaces a single symmetric reverse-KL loss with a Dynamic KL that weights forward- and reverse-KL per candidate token by the teacher/student probability ratio — correcting under- and over-prediction as separate problems. At 2-bit, across Qwen3-1.7B/4B/8B, it beats every tested baseline on every benchmark at every size, improving the Qwen3-8B average by 10.26 points over the strongest baseline. This favors the token-selection/loss-design explanation over a hard bit-width floor without the controlled ablation (same recipe, swap only the loss term) that would prove it — the open question from Imperative 4 is narrowed, not closed.

Curve shape, frozen tensors, and a numerical bug masquerading as a trade-off

Imperative 6 [T1, single-study]: choose a recovery recipe by the shape of its training curve, not by its peak score. Two healing recipes reaching 54.9 and 54.6 look indistinguishable on a benchmark table and are not remotely equivalent in a release process: a KL objective against a frozen teacher peaks in roughly 100 steps and stays within about two points of that peak for the next 1,100, while a cross-entropy objective takes roughly 700 steps to an equal peak and then loses nearly 19 points by step 1,200. One can be run to convergence and shipped as-is; the other needs correctly-tuned early stopping against a held-out signal, or the artifact that ships is post-collapse.

Imperative 7 [T1, suggestive]: some tensors must stay frozen while the rest of the model adapts to the grid. Unfreezing layer norms and embedding projections during the quantization stage — especially at higher learning rates — produced checkpoints worse than the unhealed quantized model, across an eleven-configuration sweep and under both objectives tested. The failure is cliff-shaped, not graded, which is exactly the regime where a stopping rule is a liability rather than a safety net.

Imperative 8 [T1, strong]: an apparent precision-versus-quality trade-off in native low-bit training can turn out to be a plain numerical bug, worse at larger scale. Delta-Matching (T1) traces naive FP8 attention training's failures to a stale backward-pass delta that breaks the softmax gradient's zero-row-sum invariant — mild at 569M parameters, a 0.48-point validation-loss gap at 1.67B, outright collapse at 5.29B (1.93 vs. a 1.33 reference). A closed-form correction restores the invariant exactly, matching BF16/FP32 loss and downstream accuracy (RULER-4K 61.4% vs. the broken baseline's 24.8%) at every scale tested, with no architecture changes. Carry the lesson past this one format: a training-time quantization failure that looks like physics may be an unverified gradient derivation.

The format family is widening, and the training-time cost that gated it is an engineering problem

Imperative 9 [T1, strong, extends to MoE scale]: native 4-bit pretraining now reaches MoE scale, but needs depth-wise precision placement, not just a better gradient estimator, to avoid diverging. NVFP4 at scale (T1, NVIDIA/IST Austria) trains NVFP4 natively to 1T tokens on both a dense 8B and a 30B-A3B MoE, reaching under 1.2%/0.81% relative training-loss gap to BF16 — but an unbiased backward-pass gradient estimator alone is not sufficient: quantizing every layer diverges the 8B run past 300B tokens, and the fix is keeping the last 15% of layers in BF16, after which the choice of de-biasing technique underneath makes only a small further difference.

Imperative 10 [T1, strong, newest]: "train into the format" now covers vector-quantization grids, not just scalar and floating-point ones, and the per-step cost that kept VQ-QAT small-scale for years turns out to be an engineering bottleneck rather than a fundamental one. SQuAT (T1) splits each weight block into a unit-norm direction (quantized on a spherical E8-derived lattice codebook) and a scalar magnitude, each with its own straight-through estimator, and makes the expensive nearest-neighbor codeword search nearly free by distributing it — each worker searches its own slice of weight blocks and only integer indices are exchanged across workers. That alone cuts LLaMA-3.2-1B training time from 62.5 to 4.9 seconds per iteration (12.8×), close to plain BF16's 4.3 s/it, and is what turns VQ-QAT from a toy-scale demonstration into a method run at 10B training tokens for the first time. At 2.25 bits/weight, it matches or beats scalar integer QAT at the same budget (Qwen3-4B MMLU: 69.5 vs. 67.8, both starting from a 54.2 post-training-quantization floor) — the strand's "train into the format" claim no longer depends on which family of format you chose.

What still doesn't compose

  • Is there a bit-width below which even training into the format fails? The 37× spread at 2.7 bits is this strand's most dramatic number and it is one point on a curve. Nobody has published the equivalent sweep at 2 bits and 1.58 bits with procedure and format each held fixed in turn — the experiment that would say whether "train into it" has a floor of its own.
  • Nothing in this strand has been measured on a consumer card, on the training side. The healing runs are 8×H200-class; the sub-3-bit VLM pipeline's QAT compute is unreported; SQuAT's per-iteration speedup has no accompanying GPU-count or total-cost figure. The on-policy recovery result comes closest — its recovery targets are 0.6B-4B, consumer-card-sized at inference — but the recovery training itself still ran on an unspecified multi-GPU setup.
  • Does the teacher-choice result survive a non-proprietary compression operator? The only demonstration uses a proprietary tensor-network operator. The claim is operator-agnostic in principle — it needs only logit access — but layer pruning, SliceGPT, and low-rank decomposition produce differently-damaged students, and nothing says the recovered-teacher ceiling is the same height in each.
  • Does precision-masking of weight deltas affect how this strand's own results are read? A separate finding on distillation-small-models shows that on a Qwen3-1.7B student, roughly 97% of parameters move from initialization in FP32 but only 7-11% differ after BF16 rounding — most of a training run's real signal is invisible at the precision it's typically evaluated in. None of this strand's results (on-policy recovery, native-FP8 training, healing curves) carry a parallel FP32 delta check.
  • The decisive comparison behind this strand's boldest claim has never been run. "A 4-bit artifact can beat its own 16-bit source" rests on one training run against one baseline choice; the head-to-head against distilling from the recovered checkpoint remains unrun.

If you are choosing today

If you can afford a training pass at all, spend it on distribution, not just on bits: fixed-corpus recovery leaves reasoning broken well below three bits in a way short-form benchmarks will not show you, and on-policy recovery against your own deployment path is the cheapest lever this map has for closing that gap, at least down to roughly 3 bits. Below that, loss design (weight the divergence per token rather than applying it uniformly) is the more promising lever right now than throwing more on-policy compute at a plain reverse-KL objective. Freeze your norms and embeddings during healing regardless of which objective you pick, and if you're using a task loss rather than a KL objective, instrument the curve past its peak before you ship a checkpoint from early in training. And the format choice itself is no longer scalar-versus-nothing: a lattice vector-quantization grid, trained into rather than converted into, is now a demonstrated option at a training-time cost that no longer requires a research budget of its own to amortize.
