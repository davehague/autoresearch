Someone else's optimum: the state of tokenization & vocabulary

Synthesized 2026-10-04 from tokenization-and-vocabulary (status: emerging), coined 2026-08-14 and now past ten dated findings. The map exists because the embedding/unembedding pair — the tensor every tokenizer decision eventually becomes — was scattered across three other maps, each able to see one of its bills (storage in local-inference, bits in post-training-quantization, the component in transformer-architecture) and none able to ask the question that actually matters for a laptop: how big should V be, and who decided? The findings below treat V as one object with several currencies — bytes, bits, bandwidth, construction method, and editability — none of which substitutes for the others.

The vocabulary is a bet placed before the bet is needed

Imperative 1 [T1, one study]: price your vocabulary against your serving batch size, not against a training-time default. The unembedding matmul's arithmetic intensity works out to exactly the batch size B in fp16 — V itself cancels out of the ratio — so whether you are bandwidth-bound at decode time is entirely a function of how many sequences you serve at once, and the vocabulary decides only how much data moves once you are. Lifecycle-Optimal Tokenization (T1) measures the consequence directly: at B=1 the full V×d output matrix streams from memory on every decoded token — 15.1 μs at V=8k, 760.8 μs at V=1M on an A100 — while at B=4096 the same reads cost 143-377× less, amortized away almost entirely. Composed into a lifecycle cost that weighs training against a projected inference volume, the size that minimizes total cost moves 16× across realistic batch regimes: 32k at B=1, rising through 65k and 131k to 262k at B≥256, against a training-only optimum of 16k everywhere. The crossover is measured, not just predicted, landing at 128-256 on an A10G and 256-512 on an A100.

Imperative 2 [T1, scoped]: inside the range anyone actually trains at, quality will not tell you which size to pick — but the instrument that tells you that is blind in a specific, predictable way. Bits-per-byte spreads only 0.9-1.8% across an 8k-to-262k sweep, so there is no accuracy argument for picking a larger vocabulary than the batch-size formula above wants. The catch is what bits-per-byte cannot see: it is deliberately tokenizer-neutral, which is exactly why it misses the two failure modes vocabulary edits are known for — rare entities breaking silently and permanently when their tokens are pruned, and non-English scripts, where the whole English-FineWeb-Edu evidence base this imperative rests on simply does not apply.

Whoever shipped the model already made this bet, and it wasn't for your deployment

Imperative 3 [T1 x3]: on a single-user local deployment, a shipped vocabulary is oversized in bytes, in bits, and in time — three independent measurements of the same tensor, not one. In bytes: most of a 128K-256K multilingual vocabulary is dead weight for a single-language deployment, and corpus-driven pruning (T1) down to 10K-26K tokens cuts the embedding layer roughly 60% with no quality loss after a short fine-tune — and on two of three architectures tested, with no fine-tune at all. In bits: checkpoint inspection finds lm_head is the one tensor both AWQ and bitsandbytes leave in BF16 under 4-bit quantization — 1,188 MB on Qwen3-8B-Base — and a head-specific residual compressor (T1) recovers most of that for a fraction of a point of cross-entropy. In time, Imperative 1's bandwidth cost applies per decoded token regardless of footprint. Qwen3 ships 151,936 tokens; Gemma ships 262,144 — both defensible choices for a lab serving many languages at large batch, neither transferring to a laptop. The vocabulary you inherit is someone else's optimum, not an open question about yours.

Imperative 4 [T1, one study, translation models]: whether you can even edit the vocabulary safely is a property you can check before you start. Pruning without retraining is quality-neutral on two architectures and collapses on a third (mBART-50), traced to how tightly the decoder embedding and output head are tied — weight tying is inspectable in the checkpoint config, no experiment required. The second precondition is a data one, and it is this map's clearest link to data-curation: whichever tokens survive pruning were decided by a sampling corpus, so anything absent from that sample is unreachable by construction, not merely degraded — the same regime argument that map makes about training data, arriving in the deployment stack.

Imperative 5 [T2, single-study]: the edit runs in both directions, and a shipping team has already done the expensive one in public. A vendor's 3B edge vision-language model (T2) extended its trained backbone's vocabulary to 128,000 tokens for non-Latin script coverage by in-place tokenizer extension, not retraining from scratch. Paired with Imperative 3's deletion result, vocabulary size is now a post-hoc editable property in both directions — extend for the scripts you need, delete the rows you never reach — which puts it alongside context length as a structural hyperparameter that turned out not to be fixed at initialization after all. The grade here is deliberately low: the vendor states the mechanism in one clause, with no ablation, no before/after quality number, and no statement of how the new embedding rows were initialized. The claim to carry is existence; the price is still unknown.

Construction and encoding are their own lever, independent of size

Imperative 6 [T1]: how the vocabulary is built and used can buy compression and downstream accuracy together, without touching size at all. Every finding above holds the tokenizer's algorithm fixed and varies V. CNF-MCE (T1) varies the algorithm instead — a counting-based vocabulary construction (CNF) replacing BPE's greedy highest-frequency merge, and a min-cost segmentation algorithm (MCE) replacing greedy encoding — and wins on both axes this map tracks at once. At a matched 250K vocabulary it beats comparable BPE tokenizers by 26.7-49.7% token efficiency depending on domain; at 1M it exceeds 60%, while vocabulary utilization — how much of the tokenizer's own learned vocabulary its encoder actually uses — rises from BPE's 52.9% to 96.9%. An 11-benchmark pretraining comparison shows the compression win traveling with a downstream-accuracy win rather than trading against it: at 8B scale, GSM8K improves 17.9 points and LAMBADA 7.8 points over a comparable Qwen BPE tokenizer.

Imperative 7 [T1, one architecture at 1.27B, n=29 tokenizers]: that pairing is not guaranteed, and the map has a controlled study proving it. Hold vocabulary size fixed at ~128K and vary only algorithm, pretokenization, and training mixture, and TokEval (T1, EPFL/Apertus 2, COLM 2026) finds the two axes this map now tracks — compression and task accuracy — are answered by different metrics that do not predict each other. Information-theoretic measures (Rényi efficiency strongest at ρ=-0.80) predict bits-per-byte; structural measures (digit-boundary F1, AST boundary alignment) predict task accuracy; compression rate, the field's default heuristic, predicts the former and not the latter. So Imperative 6's result — a method that wins on compression and on task accuracy together — is worth flagging rather than generalizing from: the two sources differ in what they hold fixed (TokEval varies design at one size; CNF-MCE varies the method across sizes), and this map should not let one result imply the dissociation TokEval found no longer applies generally. TokEval's own operational limit matters too: leave-one-family-out validation puts most held-out predictions inside a calibrated interval while putting LLaMA-3's tokenizer at predicted rank 1 against an observed rank of 21 — intrinsic metrics screen candidates, they do not rank the field.

Imperative 8 [T1]: a tokenizer quality metric's corpus and normalization unit are part of its definition, and getting that wrong flips the sign of the result, not just its noise level. The same TokEval study found the Gini fairness coefficient correlating at ρ=-0.58 with multilingual bits-per-byte when measured line-normalized on a parallel corpus, and flipping to a non-significant +0.38 when measured byte-normalized on a web corpus. Fertility's sign reverses too — positive within trained languages, -0.57 aggregated across all 215 FLORES+ languages — because cross-entropy on unseen languages is scoring byte-fallback calibration, not encoding quality. Report the corpus and the unit as part of the metric, and analyse per-language rather than in aggregate; pooling hid the largest effect in the study.

The vocabulary as a boundary between models, not just a cost inside one

Imperative 9 [T1, vendor-self-evaluated]: a different tokenizer is a small tax on cross-model transfer, not a wall. The vault's working assumption had been that token-level knowledge transfer between two models requires a shared vocabulary. SimpleOPD (T1, Shanghai AI Lab) tests that assumption by distilling in shared text space instead, supervising only positions where the student's and teacher's tokens cover identical character spans and leaving the rest unsupervised: the alignable fraction starts near 90% and rises to 96-98% during training, across three students from three families against a byte-level BPE teacher. The cost tracks segmentation algorithm rather than vocabulary size — meaning the best teacher for a student is a question about capability, not about which tokenizer it happens to use.

Imperative 10 [T2]: even a declared-compatible stopping signal isn't interchangeable at the probability level. A model's EOS behavior is not one token but probability spread across a set of declared-equivalent tokens, and a study of on-policy distillation (T1) found two models can declare identical stopping sets while placing their mass on different members of it — one Qwen3 checkpoint assigning ~0.8 probability to <|endoftext|> where its post-trained counterpart assigns that same token ~10⁻¹¹. The fix is specific to distillation's training signal, but the underlying fact — a tokenizer's stopping-token set is not interchangeable at the probability level even when it is interchangeable at the set-membership level — is a vocabulary-design property this map had not named before CNF-MCE's and SimpleOPD's arrivals put the vocabulary-as-interface question on the table at all.

What still doesn't compose

  • Nobody has run the three deployment levers on one model. Row deletion, head-specific bit allocation, and batch-matched vocabulary sizing are orthogonal by construction and have never been run together, or even pairwise, on a decoder-only checkpoint. The obvious experiment — a Qwen3-8B pruned to a code-only vocabulary, head-compressed, benchmarked at B=1 for tokens/second and peak RSS — uses only pieces that already exist in-vault.
  • The map is measured on the wrong models for its own audience. The pruning evidence is 418M-600M encoder-decoder translation models; the cost-curve evidence is ≤2.3B decoder-only GPT trained for 5k steps; the bit-allocation evidence is the only source on a modern 8B open-weights checkpoint. There is no measurement of vocabulary cost on a model this vault's readers actually run.
  • V in int4 is unmeasured where it matters most. The bandwidth term scales directly with bits per parameter, so quantizing the head should move the B=1 optimum rightward — the founding cost-curve source extends its model to int4 analytically and never measures it, in exactly the precision regime this vault's premise lives in.
  • Where do CNF-MCE's and SimpleOPD's gaps live? Span-based cross-tokenizer alignment leaves 2-10% of a student's tokens unsupervised, and nobody has checked whether that's scattered uniformly or concentrated on the numerals and symbols where it would matter most. CNF-MCE's 1M-vocabulary utilization gain (52.9% to 96.9%) is likewise not yet connected to a downstream number at that same size — the 8B/250K comparison is the only downstream evidence so far.
  • Does the flat-quality result survive a non-English corpus? Everything behind Imperative 2 is English FineWeb-Edu. If per-character compression is worse for non-Latin scripts, as the source's own authors suspect, every curve moves, and the multilingual vocabularies that look oversized at B=1 may be correctly sized for the languages they were actually built for.

If you are choosing today

Run Imperative 1's batch-size formula before benchmarking anything: at B=1, which is most of this vault's readers most of the time, the formula wants something in the 32k-65k range regardless of what your base model ships with. Check weight tying before attempting any pruning — it is the cheapest predictor of whether deletion will be free or will collapse the model. If you need scripts or vocabulary your base model lacks, in-place extension is now a demonstrated (if unpriced) option rather than a retrain-from-scratch decision. And if you are choosing a tokenizer rather than editing one you inherited, treat compression rate as a screen, not a ranking — TokEval's own instrument put the field's most popular tokenizer at a predicted rank its intrinsic metrics could not reach, and CNF-MCE's construction-method win is evidence that the compression-versus-structure dissociation is not universal, not evidence that it has reversed.
