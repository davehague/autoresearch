Buy parameters, not FLOPs — until the weights don't fit: the state of mixture-of-experts

Synthesized 2026-07-21 from mixture-of-experts and refreshed 2026-08-06 after twelve dated arrivals on the map — the largest drift in this vault and its oldest article at sixteen days. The map is still consolidating: independent tiers agree on the core decoupling, effect sizes are large, and nothing has rebutted it. The title moved because the thesis needed a condition. "Buy parameters, not FLOPs" is a claim about pretraining compute, and between 2026-07-24 and 2026-08-06 the map acquired six sources about what happens at inference, where the currency is bytes rather than FLOPs and the answer inverts twice. As of 2026-08 the strongest open-weights models — DeepSeek-V3/R1, Qwen3's large variants, Mixtral, Kimi K3 — are all MoE, so this is the architecture under the weights you are most likely to fine-tune, quantize and serve. Check the map's through-line before trusting this in 2027.

The claim, and the condition it needs

A mixture-of-experts layer replaces the transformer block's feed-forward network with N expert feed-forward networks behind a sparse router: each token is processed by k of them (k=1 or 2 in the classic recipe), so parameter count and per-token compute become separate dials. Quality tracks the parameter dial when training data is large; each token's arithmetic cost tracks the compute dial.

That is a FLOPs claim, and it is well established. The lineage:

  • The sparsely-gated MoE (Shazeer et al., Google Brain, ICLR 2017) made conditional computation real — noisy top-k gating, >1000× capacity at minor efficiency loss, 137B parameters on 2017 hardware — and named the three problems everything since has been solving: routing collapse, the shrinking batch, and the bandwidth ratio. The third one is the whole second half of this article. [T1]
  • Switch Transformers (Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, JMLR 2022): route each token to one expert and expert count becomes a fourth scaling axis — 7× pretraining wall-clock to T5-Base quality at identical FLOPs per token, gains monotone from 2 to 256 experts. [T1]
  • ST-MoE (Zoph et al., 2022) is the owner's manual: most stability fixes trade quality away; the router z-loss doesn't. And sparse models fine-tune differently. [T1]
  • Mixtral 8x7B (Mistral AI, 2024, Apache 2.0) is the open-weights proof: 47B total / 13B active, matching or beating Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 (MMLU 70.6 vs 69.9 / 70.0), instruct variant above both on human Elo. [T1]
  • DeepSeekMoE (DeepSeek-AI, 2024) sets the design point the open flagships inherited: slice the experts finer and pin a shared always-on expert — within noise of the dense same-total-params upper bound. [T1]
  • DeepSeek-V3 (2024-12) is the recipe at scale and the frontier generation the 2026-07 version of this article said it was one step behind: 671B total / 37B active, auxiliary-loss-free balancing via a per-expert bias term rather than a gradient-carrying loss, and FP8 mixed-precision training through the whole run. [T1]
  • Kimi K3 (2026-07) is the largest sparse model this vault holds: 2.78T total, 104.2B activated, 16 of 896 routed experts per token — a sparsity ratio of 56, against Mixtral's 8-of-8. It ships three named fixes for things that break at that scale (below). [T2/T1, vendor report, no component ablations]

The condition. Every number above is a training-compute or quality number. The moment you ask what a sparse model costs to run, the accounting changes, and it changes differently depending on whether the weights fit.

What to do

Reading a model card. Separate the two parameter counts. "8x7B = 56B" is wrong twice — Mixtral is 47B total (experts share attention) and 13B active. Pretraining cost, per-token arithmetic and latency track active; memory tracks total. The headline win is per-FLOP, not per-byte. [T1]

Sizing hardware — and this is the rule the last two weeks rewrote. The question is not "how many parameters are active," it is "do the weights fit?"

  • If they fit, prefer a dense model at matched active parameters. On bandwidth-bound consumer and edge silicon the sparsity is invisible, because inference cost tracks total parameters: an instrumented consumer/edge study puts OLMoE-1B-7B (6.9B total, 1B active) 10% behind same-active Llama-3.2-1B on an M2 Pro and 31% behind on a Jetson Orin Nano, at 2.1× the energy per token and 8.01 GiB against an 8 GB ceiling — losing there even to a larger-active dense model. Node-level decode timing puts routing under 9% of MoE-block time, so this is structural, not a kernel awaiting optimization. [T1]
  • If they don't fit, an MoE is the easier thing to offload. Decode touches two of eight experts per layer, so most of the weights can stay in host RAM: Mixtral offloading reaches 2–3 tok/s at batch size 1 on a free-tier Colab T4 and on a 12 GB RTX 3060 (3.2× over naive offloading) via an LRU expert cache (hit rate ~0.8 at k=4) plus speculative expert prefetch — run layer L+1's gating function on layer L's hidden states, recall ~0.8–0.9 one layer ahead. A dense model of the same total has no idle 75% to leave in RAM. [T1]
  • Small-total MoE is the local-friendly shape. DeepSeekMoE 16B (~2.8B active, ≈LLaMA2-7B quality) runs on one 40 GB GPU unquantized. [T1]

Quantizing one: allocate the bits unevenly, and know which unit. Experts differ in quantization sensitivity and the difference is exploitable. Two methods, and they disagree usefully. AlphaQ ranks layers by the power-law tail exponent of their weight spectra — no calibration data at all — solves a global integer program, and puts Qwen1.5-MoE at an average 3.5 bits matching BF16 (68.04 vs 67.93) at >4× weight-memory compression. GEMQ (ICML 2026) argues the unit is the expert, not the layer, because sensitivity varies within a layer too, and a global program over experts beats a layer-local one in all twelve cells it tests; it also supplies the throughput number AlphaQ could not — 1.4–1.6× faster batch-1 decode, Mixtral-8×7B at 12.0 GB and 88.6 tok/s where FP16 will not fit an H100 at all. Two rules that survive both: fine-grained MoEs have far more exploitable variance than vanilla ones (13.2 points over uniform at 2.5 bits, against Mixtral's 2.9), and protect the shared weights before the experts — 2-bit experts cost 3.85 MMLU points; 2-bit attention, embeddings and gates cost 11.5 from a thirtieth of the parameters. [T1 ×2]

And budget for the router moving. GEMQ measures something nobody in this vault had: quantization reroutes tokens — over 40% of them at 1.5 bits. Re-fitting the router afterwards costs under 0.04% of parameters and 3.5% of quantization time, and is the single largest lever in that paper. If you quantize an MoE aggressively and the quality is worse than the bit-width predicts, the router is the first thing to check. [T1]

Speculative decoding on an offloaded MoE: assume it is a loss until you measure. This is the most counterintuitive practical finding on the map and it is now replicated by two groups. Speculation's founding accounting — verify a block of k draft tokens in one target forward pass — is a dense-model property. On a sparse target the verification pass must load the union of the experts every draft-tree node routes to, even though at most one path is accepted. DraftExpert measures verification at 2.4× a token-decode at block length 4 and 5.8× at 16, with the training-free MoE self-draft running 1.19 tok/s against an unspeculated 2.19 on an RTX 4090. AcceptMoE reaches the same place in SGLang on an RTX 5090: standard speculative decoding moves 2.3–2.8× more expert bytes per output token than not speculating, spends 65–78% of wall time stalled on expert fetches, and loses to plain autoregressive decoding on seven of eight Qwen model–task pairs. The standard advice to lengthen the speculation window is backwards here. [T1 ×2, independent groups]

The two fixes spend opposite currencies, and both work. Buy acceptance in resident parameters — a small self-distilled draft expert per layer, 85% acceptance at essentially top-1 transfer cost, 1.45× average, output exact. Or spend nothing and shrink what verification may touch — size the eligible expert set per block from the effective rank of commitment-weighted router demand, then prune non-resident members against the current cache rather than a predicted route: 2.06× over standard speculative decoding under offload, host-to-device traffic −73.6% to −77.1%, cache hit rate +10.8 to +14.2 points. Nobody has composed them. [T1 ×2]

Fine-tuning one: change the protocol, not just the checkpoint. Sparse models overfit small tasks and want smaller batches and higher learning rates — the opposite of dense; with dense-tuned hyperparameters, "virtually no fine-tuning gain over dense" survives. [T1]

Consider freezing the experts. Updating only the non-expert dense skeleton matched updating all parameters (≈86 SuperGLUE both ways); updating only the experts — ~80% of the model — lost ~3.5 points. The caveat has now survived a year of adapter papers and is stated in the tension section below. [T1]

Don't plan around a "domain expert." Two labs independently find routers learn syntax and position, not topics — near-uniform expert selection across content domains, no French expert, no math expert. That finding has since acquired an unexpected corroboration from the quantization side: the right bit allocation unit turns out to be the layer or the expert, never "the maths experts," because there is no such set to protect. [T1]

Building one on a budget: upcycle rather than pretrain. Every source above presupposes a pretraining run. Sparse upcycling does not: copy each MLP of a dense checkpoint into 32 experts, add a randomly initialized router, and continue training — beating the dense original for ~50% of its sunk pretraining cost, where MoE-from-scratch needs ~120% to catch up. Copying the MLP is load-bearing (random experts never catch up), and there is an initial dip below the source checkpoint that a naive checkpoint-selection policy would misread as failure. [T1]

At 10³ experts per layer, three things break and each has a published fix. From Kimi K3, and relevant mostly as a forecast of where the open weights are going: expanding the pool the vanilla way makes communication scale with routing multiplicity, fixed by routed experts in a latent space of half the model width (sparsity buys FLOPs; latent width buys bandwidth). Activations explode in the routed branch, fixed by an RMSNorm plus a bounded gated FFN — motivated explicitly by low-precision overflow, since SwiGLU is unbounded in both factors. And auxiliary-loss-free balancing stops adapting, fixed by Quantile Balancing, which replaces the fixed-step bias update with a closed form recovered from a histogram of per-expert margins in one all-reduce. [T2, vendor, no ablations]

The trade, named — twice

At pretraining time, MoE buys quality per training FLOP with serving memory, fine-tuning fragility, and systems complexity. That is the trade the labs take, and it is why the open flagships are sparse.

At inference time, the trade is conditional on fit, and this is the part the 2026-07 version of this article could not state. Sparsity buys FLOPs, and FLOPs are not what a single-board computer is short of. Resident on bandwidth-bound hardware, cost tracks total parameters and the sparsity is invisible — worse than invisible, since you carry the whole footprint for a fraction of the compute saving. Offloaded, the sparsity becomes the entire mechanism, because two of eight experts per layer is a transfer budget rather than an arithmetic one. Same architecture, opposite verdicts, and the discriminator is whether the weights fit in memory.

A third framing has since landed on top of both: under offload the accounting unit is the expert, not the FLOP — which is why per-expert bit allocation, expert caching and verifier-side expert selection all pay, and why FLOP-denominated intuitions about sparsity mislead in this regime specifically.

Live tensions

MoE × LoRA is still open, and two papers declined it in one night. The freeze-the-experts result was demonstrated full-rank, never with adapters, and the vault's question is what a low-rank update does when the frozen substrate underneath is a routed sparse layer — whether rarely-visited experts get any gradient at all. MoEGen looks like the answer and is its mirror image: it puts the mixture inside the adapter on dense backbones throughout. SALT names a sparse base as explicitly out of scope. The two setups have opposite failure modes — adapter-side MoE inherits routing problems on small matrices where the router can be regularized freely; base-side MoE inherits frozen, unevenly visited expert weights, where an adapter's effective training-set size differs per expert by a routing distribution nobody chose. Stated sharply: train an adapter over a routed expert layer of a sparse base and report per-expert gradient coverage alongside the task score.

Does quantizing the experts make speculation profitable under offload? AcceptMoE reports, without pursuing it, that standard speculative decoding loses on seven of eight pairs whose offloaded experts are BF16 and wins on all four whose experts are packed MXFP4. If that survives a controlled test it inverts the vault's standing position that speculation and quantization compete for the same headroom — where the scarce resource is PCIe bytes rather than idle arithmetic, quantization would be the precondition for speculation rather than a rival for it. As published, precision is confounded with the model. One card, one afternoon, one variable.

Quantized MoE that fits versus dense model that fits, on one 12 GB card. GEMQ puts Mixtral-8×7B at 12.0 GB — the exact card the offloading paper used to demonstrate running it at 2–3 tok/s. If an aggressively quantized build fits outright, the entire offload apparatus is unnecessary for that model. The unrun comparison is 1.5-bit Mixtral against a dense 7B–8B at 4 bits on the same card, on GSM8K and MMLU, at matched footprint. Neither paper ran it.

Almost none of this is reachable from the local stack. The quantization throughput numbers need custom fused kernels for 1/2/3-bit dequantization; the offloaded-speculation results need either a trained per-layer draft expert with no released weights or a modified serving engine. llama.cpp's K-quant menu cannot express per-expert bit assignment, and no local runtime ships an expert-level speculative decoder. This map now holds several demonstrations that the speed exists and no path for a reader to run any of them. Closing that is a tooling question — a GGUF-side representation for per-tensor mixed expert precision — not a research one.

Hold loosely

  • The Google trio shares authors and codebases — lineage, not independent replication. The independent replications (Mistral, DeepSeek, Moonshot) don't disclose their full training recipes; no neutral third party has trained or compared these architectures. [T1, provenance caveat]
  • Mixtral's benchmark table is Mistral's own re-runs; its Elo is a Dec 2023 snapshot; MT-Bench is GPT-4-judged, so evaluation-contamination's biases apply.
  • Kimi K3 is a vendor report with no component ablations — nothing isolates the latent-width MoE, the bounded FFN or Quantile Balancing, its 2.5× scaling-efficiency headline aggregates architecture, data and recipe, and the licence is CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0: open weights, not an open licence. At ~1.4 TB of weights it is also the point at which the open frontier left consumer reach.
  • The edge and offload results are each one paper on one model family. The consumer/edge study is one MoE on two devices; the offloading study is a venue-less two-author report on one model family with perplexity and 5-shot MMLU only, at an effective 2.6 bits/param, no reasoning benchmark, no batch above 1, and no head-to-head against simply running a dense 7B that fits — the choice a 12 GB owner actually faces.
  • The bit-allocation results are footprint- and throughput-measured, quality-checked on benchmarks least sensitive to quantization damage. Read them beside post-training-quantization's finding that a quantized model's published score is partly a property of an undisclosed calibration corpus — a hazard that is small at four bits and enormous below.
