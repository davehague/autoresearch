The image becomes a token sequence: the state of open vision-language models

Written 2026-08-03 from vision-language-models (status: contested). Thirteen dated bullets and no article — the whole [article-due] queue this run. The map is contested twice over: about which architecture family is right, and about where the supervision comes from. Neither is resolved here, because neither is resolved anywhere. What is settled is more than it looks, and it is the part a practitioner acts on. As of 2026-08 the interface claims below are stable, the architecture question is 3-vs-2 with no matched-cost comparison in existence, and one assumption underneath the cheap recipe moved last week.

The one thing everybody agrees on

An image becomes a token sequence the language model reads. That is the whole interface, and it survives every disagreement below.

LLaVA hangs a single linear projection off a frozen CLIP encoder and feeds the result into Vicuna [T1]. Molmo uses an MLP-projection-plus-pooling onto OLMo/OLMoE/Qwen2 [T1]. Qwen2.5-VL uses a vision-language merger onto Qwen2.5 [T2]. Three independent groups, three bridges, one shape — no cross-attention surgery, no joint pretraining, no architectural change to the language model.

The competing family agrees, from the opposite direction. Chameleon and Emu3 delete the encoder and the projection entirely and tokenize images into the text vocabulary — and the result is still a token sequence, just produced by a VQ autoencoder instead of a ViT [T1]. Emu3 is structurally a Llama-2 block: 32 layers, grouped-query attention at 32 query and 8 KV heads, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE. The only difference from a text model is a 184,622-entry vocabulary — text tokens, a 32,768-entry vision codebook, and five special tokens [T1].

The practical consequence is larger than it sounds: your serving stack already works. Put image tokens in the same paged pool as text and key the cache on a hash of the decoded pixels, and a variable per-image token count stops being a special case — SGLang measures 0.18 → 1.15 images/s on LLaVA-v1.5-7B (6.4×) against the model authors' own reference implementation, with a 52.4% production cache hit rate on LLaVA-NeXT-34B over a month of Chatbot Arena traffic [T1].

What is settled

Budget the fine-tune, not a cluster. [T1] LLaVA-1.5 trains fully on ~1.2M public pairs in about one day on a single 8×A100 node — the same order of resource as the text fine-tunes in peft-methods. Adding a modality is a fine-tuning-economics problem, not a pretraining one.

Do not treat the freezing schedule as load-bearing. [T1, two groups] LLaVA stages two unfreezes (align the projection with encoder and LLM frozen, then instruction-tune projection + LLM). Molmo runs two stages as well — caption pretraining, then SFT — but updates all parameters in both, with no frozen components and no RLHF at all. Two different policies, both working, which makes the staging a resource convenience rather than a requirement.

Pick the encoder by whether a fixed grid binds on your data. [T1 ×2 + T2] LLaVA and Molmo both import a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 at a fixed 336px. Qwen2.5-VL trains its own ViT at native resolution and aspect ratio — resize only to a multiple of 28, 14×14 patches, so token count varies with the image rather than the image being distorted to fit the tokens — made affordable by window attention in all but 4 layers, so encoder cost is linear rather than quadratic in patch count. The gains land exactly where a fixed 336px grid binds: documents, charts and OCR (Qwen2.5-VL-72B InfoVQA 87.3 against GPT-4o's 74.3; DocVQA 96.4). If your target is documents at native resolution, no projection will fix the encoder. Otherwise the cheap recipe still holds. The middle option — unfreezing an off-the-shelf CLIP rather than replacing it — is the only one of the three a practitioner could afford and is measured by nobody.

Spend tokens, not pixels, when the image is large. [T1] Kimi K3 applies a 2×2 pixel-shuffle downsample before projection, cutting visual token count fourfold and keeping inputs up to 3584×3584 affordable. Same lever as Qwen's window attention, different stage. Worth reaching for before you reach for a smaller model, because the bill you are paying is sequence length.

Discount any VLM headline by how it was scored. [T1] LLaVA's "85.1% relative to GPT-4" comes from GPT-4 judging a model trained on GPT-4-authored data against a GPT-4-authored benchmark — a closed loop. MT-Bench put a number on the defect: GPT-4 favours its own family's outputs by about 10% win rate, Claude-v1 by about 25%, and the bias is directional rather than noise. Molmo's headline instead rests on >325k human pairwise ratings (~450 matches per model pair, Bradley-Terry), reports the automated suite separately, and publishes where the two rankings disagree. Read LLaVA as "the pipeline works." Read Molmo's ranking as a measurement.

And read a benchmark average only after checking training exposure. [T1] Emu3's "surpasses LLaVA-1.6" is a 12-benchmark average of 62.1 to 61.8. Split by the authors' own marking for benchmarks whose related training data was observed during training, the five marked benchmarks are where it wins big (ScienceQA 89.2, ChartQA 68.6, DocVQA 76.3) and the unmarked ones are mostly losses (MMBench 58.5 vs 67.4, MMMU 31.6 vs 35.1, MM-Vet 37.2 vs 43.9), with two genuine unmarked wins (SEEDBench-Img, OCRBench). To their credit, the marking is theirs. The rule generalizes: an average over benchmarks with heterogeneous training exposure is not a comparison.

Contested — which family, and nobody has priced either

Two architectures, both delivering what they claim, and no source compares them at matched cost.

Late fusion (LLaVA, Molmo, Qwen2.5-VL) reuses a pretrained vision prior, costs about a node-day, and does understanding only. Early fusion (Chameleon, Emu3) discards the CLIP prior and is much harder to train, and buys native image and video generation from the same weights that do perception — which the adapter stack has no mechanism for at all. That advantage stopped being a promise on 2026-07-27: Emu3 is preferred over SDXL by human voters (70.0 vs 66.9 over 100 prompts), scores GenEval 0.66 against Chameleon's 0.39 and SDXL's 0.55, reaches DPG-Bench 81.60, and tops VBench at 80.96 against OpenSora 1.2's 79.76 — by predicting the next token in a video sequence [T1].

The bill for that is legible and unpaid. One 512×512 image is 4,096 tokens, which is why Emu3's second pretraining stage runs at a 131,072-token context with tensor, context and data parallelism composed. Late fusion spends compute in an encoder once and hands the language model a short sequence; early fusion spends nothing on an encoder and pays in context forever after — through the KV cache, through attention, through every cost kv-cache-management and local-inference track. Neither side of that trade has a latency, throughput, memory or quantized number attached, on any hardware. That is the gap, and it is the one this vault's premise cares most about: whichever family is cheaper to run at 4 bits on one card is the one that matters, and that is not the axis anyone has published on.

Two sub-questions have sharpened rather than closed. Does early fusion's understanding hold up outside its authors' own evaluation — the two clean cross-family cells in evidence are one paper's unmarked benchmarks, on which it wins two and loses three. And is the missing CLIP prior what costs it, or the tokenizer's lossiness? A 32,768-entry codebook reconstructing at LPIPS ~0.1 puts a fine-detail ceiling on perception before any model sees anything, and no benchmark isolates that from the architecture choice.

Contested — where the supervision comes from

The two founding papers solved "there is no multimodal instruction corpus" in opposite ways and disagree about whether one of them was legitimate.

LLaVA fed symbolic proxies — COCO captions and bounding boxes, never pixels — to a language-only GPT-4, which wrote 158K conversations. A teacher that could not perceive the modality it was supervising [T1]. Molmo names that entire lineage as the field's blind spot: the strongest open-weight VLMs "rely heavily on synthetic data from proprietary VLMs… effectively distilling these closed models into open ones," leaving the community "missing foundational knowledge about how to build performant VLMs from scratch." Its answer uses zero VLM-generated data — annotators speak for 60–90 seconds per image and the audio is transcribed and cleaned by a language-only LLM that never sees the image (PixMo-Cap: 712k images, ~1.3M captions), plus 2.3M human question-point pairs [T1].

Feasibility is settled: Molmo-72B tops its 11-benchmark academic table at 81.2 average — above Qwen2-VL-72B (79.4), GPT-4o (78.5), Gemini 1.5 Pro (78.3) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (76.7) — and ranks 2nd on human-preference Elo behind only GPT-4o [T1]. Portability is not. Most practitioners cannot run a 712k-image annotation operation, so the transferable content is the question — is there a channel where ground truth already exists? — rather than the protocol. And nobody has compared the two approaches at a fixed budget, which is what a practitioner actually needs to know.

Read the suite average carefully in either direction: Molmo wins on perception and counting (Flickr Count 85.2 vs GPT-4o's 59.6) and trails badly on knowledge (MMMU 54.1 vs 69.1). Whether that reflects the backbone, the absence of teacher-distilled reasoning traces, or the benchmark, is exactly the kind of question a suite average is designed not to answer.

New, and it moves the cheap recipe's foundation

The two-family framing has been conflating two independent choices, and Kimi K3 (2026-07-27) takes one without the other. It is architecturally late fusion — a 401M vision encoder with a lightweight MLP projector — and procedurally early fusion: language and vision jointly optimized from the start of training, visual and textual tokens interleaved inside one next-token objective, with no post-hoc alignment stage [T1].

The finding that matters is narrower. Prior practice, including this team's own previous model, initializes the vision tower from a contrastively pretrained encoder such as SigLIP. K3 trains its tower from scratch under next-token prediction, reports that it matches the SigLIP-initialized baseline on vision evaluations, and says the reason they switched was training stability — the SigLIP-initialized tower shows persistently higher gradient norms with frequent spikes under joint optimization while the from-scratch tower stays smooth.

If that holds, the frozen CLIP prior was buying convenience, not capability — and convenience is what the whole node-day economics above rests on. The practitioner rule needs a scale condition attached rather than a reversal: import CLIP because you cannot afford to train a tower, not because the contrastive objective contributes something a language objective cannot.

Two reasons not to act on it yet. There is no table comparing the two encoders anywhere in the report — "matches the baseline" is asserted, with a gradient-norm plot as the visible evidence. And "at scale" is load-bearing: a 401M encoder trained jointly with a 2.78T backbone, on a corpus including programmatically generated multimodal data (SVG, 3D assets, webpages, games, CAD schematics paired with the code that produced them). It says nothing about whether a 7B model fine-tuned on one node should unfreeze its CLIP, and the honest prediction is that it should not.

What this article does not cover

Cost on hardware you own. That strand forked to vlm-on-device on 2026-08-02 because it had stopped sharing instruments with the architecture argument — everything here is scored on capability benchmarks, everything there in milliseconds, megabytes and bits. Three findings from it are worth carrying anyway, because they change what you would buy. At a fixed memory budget, take the larger quantized model — the study of VLM quantization and uncertainty signals finds Qwen2-VL-7B at 4-bit dominating Qwen2-VL-2B at fp16 on accuracy, error-detection AUROC and selective risk, and finds that 4-bit costs the 2B arm 1.6 accuracy points against 15 points of error-detection AUROC, so an accuracy-only evaluation would have called it free [T1, one study]. Allocate the precision budget by modality — vision at 4 bits, text at 8 — per the quantized adaptive VLM at the edge, though below 4 bits a CLIP-class encoder falls off a cliff where an 8B language model degrades gradually [T1, one study]. And the tower, not the language model, is what makes local use feel broken: vLLM-style inference under MLX on Apple Silicon measures 21.7 s on the first turn falling to 0.78 s by the third on one 1024×1024 image, with vision-embedding caching worth 7.8× of that 28× [T1, vendor-authored].

If you are building one this month

  1. Late fusion, frozen CLIP, MLP projector, one node-day. The interface is replicated three ways and the economics are the reason this is reachable at all. Do not train a tower.
  2. Unless your data is documents, charts or OCR at native resolution — then the fixed 336px grid is your ceiling and no projection will lift it.
  3. Downsample visual tokens before the projector (2×2 pixel shuffle, or window attention in the encoder). Sequence length is the bill in both families.
  4. Evaluate on something that is not a language model of the same family as your teacher, and check training exposure before quoting any suite average.
  5. Do not pick a family on generation you do not need. Early fusion's advantage is real and demonstrated; it costs the CLIP prior, much harder training, and 4,096 tokens per image forever.
  6. Price it yourself before you commit, because nobody has published the number: prefill and decode throughput, peak VRAM, and a real benchmark for a 3B–8B VLM at 4 bits on a 12–24 GB card, with and without vision-embedding caching. That single table would settle more of this map than any paper currently in it.
