Keep everything badly: the state of KV cache management

Synthesized 2026-08-14 from kv-cache-management (status: contested), eleven days after the map was coined and on the night it crossed ten dated findings. The map is contested for a specific reason, and it is the reason to read this before choosing a compressor: the metric almost every result below is scored on cannot see the damage it is scored against. As of 2026-08 every number here is attached to a card, an attention backend, a construction protocol and a context length, and most published figures are missing at least one of the four.

The budget

Attention needs every previous key and value. Keeping them is the KV cache, and it grows linearly with context and batch while the weights do not — so past a certain length the cache, not the model, is what you cannot fit. Paged allocation (vLLM, T1) fixed the fragmentation half of that a few years ago and exact-prefix reuse (RadixAttention, T1) fixed the redundancy half. What is left is the part neither can fix: at 256K on an 8B model the cache is tens of gigabytes, and something has to give.

The literature's answer has been to pick a byte budget and decide what to keep inside it. Five families do this: evict tokens, quantize states, reconstruct what you dropped, partition by depth, or allocate per head. This article is organized around the two findings that cut across all five — check whether the budget is real before you spend it, and at a fixed budget, coverage beats fidelity — plus the measurement rule that the map is contested over.

First: is the budget real?

Before compressing anything, check whether you are storing one cache or N copies of it. Tensor parallelism partitions the KV cache by attention head, which works cleanly only up to num_key_value_heads. Past that the cache duplicates on every rank, and multi-head latent attention — one shared latent vector across all query heads — replicates in full at any TP size (vLLM's decode context parallelism, T2).

The fix shards the cache along sequence position instead: AllGather the queries, attend on each rank's local slice, then AllGather and ReduceScatter the partial outputs, merged by online-softmax reweighting. That merge carries each rank's running max and denominator, so the result is arithmetically the full-cache attention — not an approximation, and there is no quality term to trade. Measured on 8×B200 serving Kimi K2.6 in NVFP4: baseline tensor parallelism saturates KV memory at concurrency 64 and plateaus at 1,863 tok/s/GPU; sharded, it reaches 6,091 tok/s/GPU at concurrency 512 while still at 82% KV memory. Flags today: --decode-context-parallel-size N, with TP % DCP == 0 for latent attention and (TP // num_key_value_heads) % DCP == 0 for grouped-query. [T2, one configuration]

The reason this belongs at the top rather than in a serving footnote is that it reframes everything under it. Compression is the right answer when the memory is genuinely absent — one card, one machine. Sharding is the right answer when it is present but replicated. Every compression number in this article was measured in the first regime, and none of the papers says so, which means their baselines are systematically the harder case.

Two things this does not settle, and they matter for anyone reading it as a general win. Throughput is measured at high concurrency, exactly where two extra collectives per decode step per layer amortize; per-token latency at low concurrency is unmeasured. And the post is silent on interaction with prefix caching and chunked prefill, which is where re-partitioning a cache is most likely to break something.

There is also a bill attached, payable by the architecture. Grouped-query attention buys roughly MHA quality at 5.4× decode speed (GQA, T1) by cutting KV heads — and the head count is the same number that caps how far the cache can shard. A KV-head reduction is a single-device win that becomes a multi-device constraint, and latent attention, the most aggressive version of the idea, is the worst case.

What is settled: six imperatives

1. At a fixed byte budget, approximate every token rather than keep a few exactly. Store a small set of exact anchors per KV head, express every other token as a projection onto its nearest anchor plus a 2-bit Hadamard-rotated residual for the positions whose error most moves the attention output (AnchorKV, T1). At 20× compression that retains 99.3% of full-cache RULER-32K on Llama-3.1-70B, and at 10× it beats every byte-matched eviction baseline — the same quality for half the memory. The decisive number is not the average: on needle-in-a-haystack at 5× it reads 0.94 against an uncompressed 0.99 while AdaKV reads 0.18. Eviction fails hard, because a deleted needle is unrecoverable; approximation fails soft, because an under-valued position is stored coarsely and is still there. The trade: decode latency is 1.3× higher at 32K, reaching parity near 64K. [T1]

2. Spend bits before you spend tokens. On reasoning workloads 2-bit KV quantization matches the uncompressed model exactly on AIME (67.4 vs 67.4) where the best eviction method reaches 50.0, and degrades a matched-correct reasoning chain in 4.2% of cases against eviction's 47.9–64.8% (answer–evidence gap, T1). Read with the fact that 3.5 bits is reachable with no calibration data at all (TurboQuant, T1; 2-bit per-channel grouping is older still — KIVI, T1) and that a measured eviction study on an RTX 4090 bought a 4% speedup at higher peak memory (counter-causal surprise, T1), the ordering follows. Honest limit: the comparison is not byte-matched, and composing the two families is still the run nobody has done.

3. Do not delete irreversibly when you can demote cheaply. About 6.18% of long-inactive cache windows re-enter the important set later in decoding, against an oracle's 0.98% — the fraction a one-shot decision gets wrong by construction. Partition the budget three ways instead: full precision for the top band, an INT2 tier attention can still read for the middle, deletion only for the bottom, re-ranked every 8 tokens with promotion back out of the quantized tier (QEvict, T1). The attention-mass accounting moves from 33.3% exact / 64.8% gone to 19.7% exact / 42.6% quantized / 35.7% gone, and it beats the best pure-eviction baseline at every budget on Llama-3.1-8B (RULER-32K at 20% retained: 87.6 vs 86.4), with the margin widest at the tightest budgets. Note what this is and is not: the two levers act on disjoint window sets, so it shows a budget can be partitioned between precision and deletion, not that quantizing an evicted cache is safe. [T1]

4. Read the head budget off the frozen weights before you spend a calibration run. Form a head's query-key matching operator M_h = W_K^{h⊤}W_Q^h, normalize its singular values, take the exponentiated Shannon entropy: a concentrated spectrum marks a retrieval head that needs the full cache, a diffuse one marks a streaming head servable from a 128-token sink plus a 256-token recent window (Autonomy of Heads, T1). At 50% head sparsity that retains 96.5% of full-attention LongBench across four models and halves KV memory at 256K (34.362 → 17.206 GB). It beats gates trained on data at the identical budget, and the controls are brutal — random head selection reads 30.89, inverting the criterion reads 23.31. By Sylvester's determinant theorem the whole diagnosis is about 4.6×10⁷ operations, i.e. seconds on the checkpoint, once. [T1]

5. Report the attention backend with every efficiency number, or the number means nothing. The same method, model and workload under two attention implementations: eager SDPA gives +9.8% throughput at flat peak memory; FlashAttention-2 gives −59% throughput and −29.7% peak memory (29.54 → 20.78 GB). The compressed FA2 configuration is slower than the uncompressed eager baseline. This is structural, not an implementation slip — a policy that scores tokens by attention needs the probabilities materialized, and the fused kernel family exists to avoid writing them down. The memory saving and the speed saving live in different configurations, and no source on this map states its backend. [T1]

6. Score compression query-agnostically, or not at all. Appending the downstream question to the context before compressing versus compressing the reusable prefix before the query is known: same method, model, budget and benchmark, 72.20 against 26.92 on 32K RULER at 10% retained (ResKV, T1). The second setting is the one that describes deployment — a prefix cache is built once, before the queries that hit it — and it is also more discriminative, with method differences roughly double. A KV-compression result absent its construction protocol is uninformative. [T1]

Why the map is contested

Everything above is scored on accuracy, and accuracy is an asymmetric diagnostic for a compressed cache. Under a controlled fixed-trace replay protocol — an uncompressed model writes the reasoning trace once, then every method replays the same text through its own compressed cache — eleven methods on Qwen3-8B separate on two axes that were assumed to move together. Spearman ρ between accuracy rank and reasoning-quality rank: AIME26 −0.95, GPQA-Diamond −0.20, MedCalc +0.31, RULER +0.61 (answer–evidence gap, T1).

The operating rule is the asymmetry itself: a collapse proves damage; a preserved score proves nothing. A compressed cache can keep the answer and lose the reason for it, and on matched-correct cases the chain becomes invalid for SnapKV 64.8% of the time, AdaKV 63.2%, TOVA 50.6% — against KIVI-2bit's 4.2%. More budget fixes the answer first: accuracy recovers across 512→8,192 retained tokens while chain consistency stays degraded at all of them.

Two riders. Coverage beats precision — the gap tracks losing access to parts of the trace, not reducing KV memory, which is why the quantization control is barely affected and why imperatives 1 and 3 point the way they do. And salience is not trust: sink/window policies over-preserve early claims (StreamingLLM fidelity 94.0 → 28.9 under wrong-chain span replacement) while attention-scored policies over-preserve late conclusion-shaped tokens, so each family has a position-shaped vulnerability rather than a uniform loss.

A second, narrower pushback runs alongside it and the boundary between them is legible. On single-needle retrieval an oracle and plain BM25 both hold 100 at 8k, 16k and 32k while recency falls to ~64 and reader-attention to ~78 (CoMem, T1) — so nearly every method here selects using an attention-derived signal, and on that diagnostic a lexical retriever from 2009 ties an oracle. Read the two together: when the evidence is one identifiable span, selection binds; when it is diffuse across many low-scoring positions, there is nothing to select and aggregate fidelity is what is missing.

Two results that change the shape of the problem

Eviction deletes a pair of sums, not a set of tokens. Write attention with the normalization opened up — o = N_S(q)/Z_S(q) — and hard eviction drops (N_E, Z_E) for the evicted set and renormalizes over what remains. The loss is algebraic, and sums are approximable even when their terms are not storable: keep a mean key, a mean value and a population count per group of evicted tokens, entering the same softmax (ResKV, T1). Improves all 32 displayed LongBench configurations and 63 of 64 RULER configurations at identical retained budget, largest at the tightest budgets, and recovers hard eviction exactly at zero residual budget — so it strictly generalizes the family it beats.

The cache has a second axis, and it is cheaper than the one everyone compresses. The cache is tokens × layers, and every method above operates on tokens. Cache one intermediate residual tensor per token at a mid-depth split instead, retrieve a bounded set of chunks, recompute only the upper layers (CoMem, T1): on Qwen3-8B at layer 12 of 36 the stored tensor is 1/18 the bytes of full bf16 KV, and at fixed retrieval breadth the model-side read is independent of stored context length. At 128k: 18.26 GB against 89.36 GB, 7.83× prefill, decode roughly a wash. The flagship configuration needs 4,000 self-distillation steps on 8 GPUs.

What nobody has run

  • The composition this map was coined to want. Quantizing the states a token-eviction policy has already selected — where eviction has concentrated attention onto fewer retained states and a coarse quantizer's error is least tolerable. QEvict partitions rather than composes, so the interference never gets a chance to appear.
  • The evidence check on anything but eviction. The replay protocol is released and the run is a day's work. Anchor-residual compression and learned restoration (RestoreKV, T1 — eight learned restore tokens over a LoRA-adapted pass, KVzip 38.2 → 73.2 at a 5% budget, but 42.0 vs 73.2 when trained on one evictor and run on another) both preserve coverage by construction and should land near the quantization end. If they do, the practitioner ordering stops being "prefer bits" and becomes prefer coverage, in whatever currency buys it.
  • Margins, not rankings. Every quality number here is an argmax-scored aggregate. Margin shrinkage (T1) shows aggregates survive compression while the decision margin behind them contracts, and supplies a paired instrument that would answer this in GPU-minutes. Until somebody runs it, "99.3% of full-cache RULER at 20×" is a claim about rankings and is silent about the size of the gap.
  • A curve instead of a table. Every result is reported at a handful of retention ratios against a full-cache reference. Nobody plots quality against cache bytes across methods on one axis — the single figure a practitioner choosing a budget needs, and one every paper here has the data to draw.

If you are choosing today

On one card, at batch 1, with memory binding: quantize the cache before you evict from it, prefer a method that stores every token coarsely over one that stores a few exactly, and read the head split off the weights for free before spending anything else. Measure on your own workload with your own attention backend, because the sign of the speedup depends on it — a fused compressed path costs 0.5% of prompt throughput against llama.cpp where an unfused one costs 44% (RotaryQuant, T1). And if you are on more than one GPU, check whether you have a memory problem at all before you accept a quality trade to solve it.
