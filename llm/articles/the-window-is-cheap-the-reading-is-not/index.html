The window is cheap; the reading is not: the state of context extension

Written 2026-08-02, refreshed 2026-08-22 from context-extension (status: contested, nineteen dated bullets). The map is contested, and that is a reason to write it rather than to wait: the settled parts are settled and immediately useful, and the open part is a single well-posed question with a named, unrun experiment attached. As of 2026-08 the mechanism claims below are stable and the effective-length numbers are not — the field's measuring instrument changed in 2024 and most published window claims predate it.

What changed in this revision: the contest is unmoved and the map is unmoved, but three things arrived that change how to act. The training window turns out to have an interior optimum, which the extension literature never considered because it only ever argued about the deployed one. A frontier model shipped a 1M-token window with no position encoding at all, which makes this article's central argument a contest inside one architectural choice rather than the whole space. And two serving-side results say a long window is mostly unread at inference by design — measurably 4.6% of it at 128K — and that how you fine-tune and how you evict are one decision, not two.

The claim

You can take a released open-weights checkpoint and give it a longer context window after the fact, cheaply. YaRN takes LLaMA 2 7B and 13B to a 128k window with roughly 600 fine-tuning steps on under 0.1% of the original pretraining data — 10× fewer tokens and 2.5× fewer steps than the methods before it [T1]. That is the most budget-asymmetric lever in this vault: a capability normally associated with a pretraining run, bought for a fine-tuning afternoon on one or two cards.

The reason it works is architectural rather than clever. Since rotary position embedding stores position as a rotation rather than a learned lookup table, the frequencies are a continuous knob you can turn after training and then fine-tune back into alignment. There is no position embedding table to resize, so there is nothing to retrain from scratch [T1].

Everything above survives. What has come apart in the last month is the assumption sitting underneath it — that a window you can open is a window the model can read.

What is settled

Treat the position encoding as heterogeneous. [T1] Position Interpolation squeezes every RoPE dimension by the same factor, which destroys the high-frequency dimensions carrying local token order. YaRN's NTK-by-parts scheme decides per dimension by how many rotations it completes over the training context (r = L/λ): long-wavelength dimensions (r < α = 1) encode absolute position and are fully interpolated, short-wavelength ones (r > β = 32) encode relative offset and are left untouched, with a ramp between. Uniform rescaling is the failure mode, and the rule generalizes past this one method.

Expect the fine-tune to teach a scaling relationship, not a length. [T1] YaRN's s=32 model was trained only at 64k and evaluated cleanly at 128k (perplexity 2.37 at 131,072 tokens for the 7B, 2.24 for the 13B). The Dynamic-YaRN variant sets s = max(1, l'/L) at inference and gets more than 2× extension with no fine-tuning at all, degrading gradually rather than falling off a cliff. If your extension collapses precisely at its training length, something is memorized that should have been learned.

Fold the attention-entropy correction into the embedding scale. [T1] Attention entropy grows with sequence length; YaRN's temperature term (√(1/t) = 0.1·ln(s) + 1) folds into the embedding scale rather than the attention kernel, so it is a uniform perplexity improvement for no architectural change, no extra parameters and no custom kernel.

Do not assume a longer training window is free. [T1] Every other claim on this page is about the window you deploy. Sweep the window you pretrain at — token budget, update count, data, seed and optimizer all held fixed — and quality rises and then falls: on the information-abundance sweep (Johns Hopkins, 2026-08), SuperGLUE and closed-book multiple-choice knowledge peak near 2048 tokens and language-modeling loss bottoms near 8192, degrading past both. The two obvious escapes are closed — the same inverted U appears at all four scales tested (20M–750M) and under RoPE, ALiBi and LongRoPE alike, which matters because everything in this article is a positional-encoding argument operating one layer above the effect. The account offered is economic: context and weights are alternative carriers of the same information, so a longer window lowers what the weights must hold and the optimizer takes the discount, visible as a falling feed-forward-to-attention gradient-norm ratio. The fine-tuning half is causal and immediately actionable: fix the document budget at eight and vary only how many are task-relevant, and going from zero to eight relevant documents improves supporting-context accuracy while significantly reducing no-context accuracy and increasing vulnerability to conflicting context, the supporting-minus-conflicting gap opening from 22.1 to over 42 points across Qwen3 0.6B–14B. Tune feed-forward modules alone and no-context robustness improves; tune attention alone and context reliance rises. The bound is large — the controlled sweep tops out at 750M, the fine-tuning arm is LoRA, and no arm tests a curriculum (short window first, lengthened later), which is what most real recipes do.

Read a long-context result against an unextended baseline as a measurement of the baseline. [T1] The CoMem authors state this against their own headline: their RULER 97.05 at 128k is measured on a Qwen3-8B whose native window is 40,960, so everything above that is "an unextended stress reference, not a substitute for a length-extended model." They decline to claim superiority over a YaRN-enabled full context. Adopt the discipline: a method that beats a model past that model's own window has measured the window, not its own reach.

Never quote perplexity as evidence that a window works. [T1] This is the single most transferable lesson on the map, and the vault has now watched it fail in four unrelated literatures. On PG-19, competing extension methods land within 0.5% of each other (Jet-Long 11.71 against DCA 11.77 at 1.7B) while the same pair on a capability benchmark reads 73.93 against 67.80 — six points. Same ordering, wildly different magnitude. Perplexity is a training-health signal; it will tell you your extension did not break the model, and it will not tell you the model can use the window.

Budget the KV cache separately, and know that nobody has priced it at length. Extending the window is the move that makes the KV wall bind hardest, and the vault holds three levers on that budget: grouped-query attention as an architectural choice [T1], near-optimal KV cache quantization — a data-free rotation reaching 3.5 bits per channel at parity with a 16-bit cache on LongBench-E [T1] — and eviction [T1]. Two warnings on the third. Eviction is not free: 5.0–5.6 points on MATH500 and 13.3 on AIME at 25% retention, so a compressed 128K window is not a 128K window. And its one efficiency table, on an RTX 4090, shows peak memory 0.2–0.6 GB higher than not compressing, because at a 7B scale the cache is small beside the weights. There is no memory-versus-context-length curve published anywhere for it, and nothing measures any two of the three levers together.

A fourth lever arrived on the supply side rather than the demand side, and it is the first thing here that raises the ceiling without touching the window, the position encoding or the precision: sharding the cache by sequence position across ranks. On 8×B200, vLLM's decode context parallelism reports the baseline running out of memory on 200k+ token requests while the sharded configuration holds a stable frontier, with per-GPU throughput going 1,863 → 6,091 tokens/s at concurrency 512 [T2]. Two scope notes keep it in its lane. The gain is a concurrency gain — it enlarges how many long requests you can serve, not how well any one is read. And it is a multi-GPU lever, so a single-card long-context deployment, which is what the cheap- extension claim is usually about, gets nothing from it.

What is contested

Here is the tension, stated plainly: extension is cheap, and cheap extension of a small model yielding usable context is undemonstrated. That is the exact half this vault's premise needs, and it is the half that has no clean answer.

The instrument changed, and most window claims predate it

RULER (NVIDIA, COLM 2024) scores 17 models on 13 synthetic tasks at 4K–128K, 500 examples per cell, and defines effective context as the longest length still beating an unextended Llama-2-7B at 4K [T1]. Of models claiming 32K or more, about half hold 32K. Yi-34B claims 200K and holds 32K; Mixtral-8×22B claims 64K and holds 32K; DBRX claims 32K and holds 8K.

The tasks are synthetic on purpose — context length becomes a dial, and parametric knowledge cannot substitute for reading. The failure modes RULER exposes are invisible to a needle-in-a-haystack sweep, and they are specific: about 40 points lost to distractor needles, with errors that are near misses (the model matches the range coarsely and fails to locate the key); about 15 points lost by raising the number of queries from one to eight; over 80% of aggregation output at 128K copied verbatim from the single in-context example; and in distractor-padded QA, enough noise drives performance toward the model's no-context baseline — it stops reading altogether.

The practitioner consequence is immediate. A needle test is not an evaluation. If a model card advertises a window and cites a needle sweep, you have learned that the model can copy one string from a haystack, which is passed by models that can do nothing else at length.

The cheap-extension family is at the bottom of that table

The three cheaply-extended checkpoints in RULER are its three worst models. LongChat-7B (linear RoPE interpolation plus a brief fine-tune) and LongAlpaca-13B (LongLoRA) both claim 32K; the 1M-trained LWM-7B claims 1M. All three read effective <4K — they never beat their own unextended base at any length tested.

Two honest qualifications, and they are why the map is contested rather than fading. No YaRN checkpoint appears in the study, so this indicts the family and not the specific method. And post-hoc extension is not refuted as a class: Qwen2-72B is trained at 32K, extended at inference, and reaches an effective 32K near the top of the open rankings, with the authors stating outright that a large training window "is not always necessary."

What predicts effective length across the table is model size and RoPE base frequency, not whether the window was trained in.

The controlled version, and the gate between 4B and 8B

The cross-model regression above has obvious confounds — a 34B and a 7B differ in far more than size. The within-family version was run in July 2026 [T1]: RULER on Qwen3 1.7B / 4B / 8B with YaRN as a baseline, one method, one family, one data blend.

YaRN reads −7.94 / +0.17 / +5.36 against doing nothing. At 1.7B, rescaling the frequencies is nearly eight points worse than leaving the model alone. It comes from authors whose purpose was to beat YaRN, which is the kind of number that survives skepticism.

Read the table by column and the conclusion changes shape, which is the most useful thing on this map. DNTK, DCA and Self-Extend all beat the base model at every size, and a tuning-free position- remapping scheme beats everything. So the problem was never that small models cannot be extended — it is that the one family this vault had sourced is the fragile one. That reinforces RoPE base frequency as the operative variable: rescaling manipulates it directly and breaks, while remapping leaves it alone and moves the indices instead (f(x) = ⌊x/G⌋ with G = max(1, ⌈L/w_pretrained⌉) recomputed from the current length, so remote rotation angles always land inside the trained distribution and nothing is fitted per model).

The critical scope limit, and it is the reason this is not settled: the YaRN in that table is zero-shot, applied at inference with no fine-tuning, where this map's central claim is specifically about ~600 fine-tuning steps. What is established is that the free version of YaRN fails on small models. Whether 600 steps rescues a 1.7B model is unmeasured.

The whole contest may be inside one architectural choice

This article argues rescaling the frequency bank (YaRN) against remapping the indices (Jet-Long, DCA, Self-Extend). In July 2026 Kimi K3, a frontier open-weights model, shipped a production 1M-token window with No Position Encoding — no RoPE on any query or key — carrying position implicitly in the recurrent gating and channel-wise decay of a hybrid linear-attention stack, and states it "extrapolates directly to 1M-token contexts without any positional-encoding modification, such as RoPE rescaling or interpolation" [T1]. That is a model declining to enter the argument.

It resolves nothing here, for three reasons worth stating so nobody over-reads it. It is not the cheap operation this article is about — the window comes from a four-stage pretraining and cooldown curriculum (8K → 64K → 256K → 1M) on a 2.78T-parameter model, which is evidence about how to build long context, not how to buy it. There is no controlled comparison: no RoPE arm, no YaRN arm. And pointedly, no RULER — the one instrument that downgraded this topic in the first place is not run by the report that most challenges its framing.

What does transfer is a data claim, and it is the first one on this topic: "length alone does not confer long-range capability." Genuinely long documents are deduplicated (exact, fuzzy and perceptual hashing), upsampled against short text, and synthetically augmented by permuting and concatenating documents so embedded tasks can only be solved by attending across the full window. If you are building long-context training data, that is the recipe to copy.

Most of a long window is not read at inference, and this is now measured

FlashPrefill V2 (2026-08) publishes the measured attention density of a long prompt at each length — the fraction of key blocks a block-sparse prefill kernel actually needs [T1]:

Running at that final 4.6% costs 1.1 RULER points averaged across the three models and 1.8 at 128K specifically. So at 128K the model is not reading 95% of its window, and taking that 95% away costs about a point. This is the systems-side statement of this article's title, arriving from a CUDA kernel rather than an eval, and it is worth more than the paper's headline speedup — which travels from 47.26× at the operator to 1.2× once the chunked prefill that production engines already run is enabled, and to parity (1.01×) at 4K where density is still 70–76%.

Two riders. Density is measured on needle-in-a-haystack inputs, the single most sparsity-favourable shape; genuine cross-window aggregation should read denser and is not measured. And this is a prefill-only result — decode falls back to dense attention, because one query token per step gives block selection nothing to select over.

If you will evict at inference, fine-tune with eviction on

The sharpest practical result to land on this topic in a month, and it closes an open question this article has carried since it was written: how extension interacts with task fine-tuning. The answer is that they are not two things.

Learning how to Forget (AWS Labs, 2026-08) fine-tunes Qwen3-4B twice from the same base, on the same data, with the same LoRA budget (r = 16) [T1]. One arm uses the state of the art — sequence parallelism, exact attention, every token attending to every earlier one. The other runs with the KV-cache eviction policy already in place, so the model co-adapts to the evictions it will suffer. Both are then served under that policy, on Helmet at 128K:

The third column is what makes this a train/test result rather than a quality result: the exact-attention checkpoints are the better models, and eviction destroys them. The failure mode is not lost retrieval — it is that they stop being able to stop, running to the full 128-token cap in mostly nonsense, with the gold string sometimes buried inside. Which is why the same comparison scored by substring match on four other Helmet datasets reads, in the authors' words, "mixed and inconclusive." A lenient metric flatters the broken arm, because a longer output has more chances to contain the target.

Two honest bounds. The method is not immune to its own finding — its own h2o_orig arm scores 3.0 on json_kv with the same runaway-length signature. And the authors concede sparse-attention inference remains slower than context parallelism, "which may remain the best choice if a large hardware budget can be afforded." What makes it reachable at all is a memory trick worth knowing independently: adjacent cache buffers differ by a scatter at known indices, so autograd can store the delta and rebuild the rest, putting gradients for a 4B model on one A100 40 GB at about 30% more per step than exact attention.

Three different things called "effective context"

Keep them separate; they are measured by different instruments and it is not obvious the ordering between them is the same for any two models.

  • Perplexity-effective — the window over which loss stays flat. What YaRN measures. Cheapest and least informative.
  • Retrieval-effective — the window over which the model can find, track and aggregate. What RULER measures.
  • Supervision-effective — how far into a sequence a training signal remains informative. New, and measured once: in on-policy distillation, a teacher's continuation advantage falls from +0.37 at a 1K-token prefix to +0.02 at 16K, with a usable response-length window of 3K–7K and instability propagating back-to-front [T1]. So a cheaply extended model can hold 128K and still be untrainable past ~7K by at least one standard post-training method, for reasons that have nothing to do with position encoding. One method, mathematics only, 1.5B–1.7B students — an existence proof that the question exists, not an answer.

What would settle it

One run, and everything needed is public: YaRN's own fine-tuning recipe on Qwen3-1.7B, scored on RULER. One small model, one published recipe, one open benchmark, a few hundred GPU-hours. It would move this map in one direction or the other, and no one has done it.

What to actually do, as of 2026-08

  1. If you need a longer window on a model above ~8B, extension is cheap and works. Use it. Budget a few hundred fine-tuning steps, not a pretraining run.
  2. Below ~4B, do not reach for frequency rescaling first. It is the one family measured to hurt at that scale. Tuning-free alternatives — position remapping, dynamic NTK, dual chunk attention, self-extend — beat the base model at every size in the one controlled comparison available.
  3. Score the result on something with distractors and multiple queries. RULER is released. A needle sweep will tell you nothing you want to know, and neither will perplexity.
  4. Price the KV cache before you price the extension. Grouped-query attention, 3.5-bit KV and eviction are the three levers; the first two are close to free and the third is not.
  5. Assume your training signal dies long before your window does. If you are fine-tuning on long sequences, check that the loss past ~7K is doing anything at all.
  6. Fine-tune under the constraint you will serve under. If inference will evict from the KV cache, quantize it, or run any other approximation, tune with that approximation switched on. A model validated under exact attention and served under eviction is a different model, and it fails by generating forever rather than by getting answers wrong.
  7. Report output length alongside any substring-match score. It costs nothing, it is computed from artefacts you already have, and it is the single check that separated "inconclusive" from a sixty-point rout above.
  8. Do not treat a longer pretraining window as a free upgrade. If you control pretraining or long continued-pretraining, the window is a hyperparameter with an interior optimum, not a monotone improvement — and the optimum for knowledge tasks sat near 2048 tokens in the one controlled sweep available.

Caveats on everything above

RULER and the Qwen3 comparison are both synthetic. On the one natural long-context benchmark reported alongside the latter (HELMET-RAG), the methods sit within a point of each other and the winning remapping scheme loses by 0.73 at 1.7B — so the six-point synthetic gap does not reproduce on retrieval work. The Qwen3 study is one model family at a 4× extension ratio (32K → 128K), far short of YaRN's headline, with no fine-tuned baseline of any kind, and its authors note the position-aliasing advantage diminishes at 128K — the method's edge is weakest at the longest length it advertises. Its throughput numbers are H100-only and depend on a bespoke fused kernel that does not exist in llama.cpp.

The other road to long context — partitioning the sequence across devices rather than editing the position encoding, as in Ring Attention — scales trainable context linearly with device count and reaches 16M tokens [T1], but its extension cost 32 A100s against YaRN's ~600 steps. It is a datacenter technique, and it does not speak to the cheap half of this map's claim.
