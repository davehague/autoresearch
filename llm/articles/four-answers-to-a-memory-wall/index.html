Four answers to one memory wall: the state of distributed training

Synthesized 2026-07-22, refreshed 2026-07-26 and again 2026-08-09 from distributed-training (status: consolidating since 2026-07-20). Nine dated entries landed on the map since the last refresh, and three of them were forks: the map shed its activation-recomputation strand on 2026-08-02, its CPU/NVMe demotion strand on 2026-08-03, and its pipeline strand on 2026-08-06. The slug says "four" and, by coincidence and after a detour through six, four is the right count again — for an entirely different reason than in July. Everything below is about full-parameter training on a cluster. If you fine-tune with adapters on one or two cards, skip to "The gap that matters most": the sections that apply to you have moved to other maps, and this article now tells you where.

The claim

Training stops where one device's memory stops, and it stops early. Plain distributed data parallel replicates parameters, gradients, and optimizer states on every GPU, which puts the ceiling near 1B parameters on a 40GB card — the FSDP paper demonstrates DDP failing to wrap T5 above 2.28B. [T1]

The arithmetic underneath is worth memorizing, because most of what follows is a rearrangement of it. Mixed-precision Adam costs 16Ψ bytes per parameter: 2Ψ fp16 params + 2Ψ fp16 grads + 4Ψ fp32 params + 4Ψ momentum + 4Ψ variance. Twelve of those sixteen bytes are fp32 optimizer state that the forward pass never touches, replicated on every GPU. [T1] That much has been settled since ZeRO and is not in dispute anywhere in this literature.

Where the axes live now

July's version of this article listed six answers on one map. Three of them have since been carved onto their own maps, because they turned out to answer different questions. What is left here is the irreducible content of distributed training: partition a tensor across devices inside one synchronous step, trading communication volume for memory. Those compose multiplicatively into N-D parallelism precisely because none of them changes when anything runs.

The bottom three rows are the ones a single-card reader wants, and they left this map for exactly that reason. Recomputation trades compute for activation memory on one device. Demotion buys capacity from slower hardware you already own. Pipelining partitions the schedule, which is why it alone has a bubble, alone sells numerical exactness for throughput, and alone reappears intact in inference serving. None of those is a way of spreading state across a cluster.

What to do

  • Turn on ZeRO stages 1 and 2 unconditionally. ZeRO decomposes sharding into three cumulative switches with asymmetric prices: optimizer states (4× memory reduction), + gradients (8×), + parameters (N_d×, linear in data-parallel degree). Stages 1 and 2 move the same communication volume as plain data parallelism — close to strictly dominant. Stage 3 costs 1.5×. A 7.5B model at 64-way data parallelism goes 120 GB → 31.4 → 16.6 → 1.88 GB per device. So: 1 and 2 always; 3 when you must fit, knowing you are paying bandwidth for it. [T1]
  • Treat sharding as a dial, not a switch. FSDP's sharding factor runs continuously — F=1 is plain DDP (cheapest communication, no savings), F=W is full sharding (lowest memory, most communication), hybrid values a graduating trade. FSDP-unit granularity is a second knob in the same currency. [T1]
  • Expect wrong settings in both directions, and measure. T5-11B loses 7% per-GPU TFLOPS from 8 to 512 GPUs as communication outruns the available overlap. FSDP's CPU rate limiter is worth up to 5× on T5-11B, costs 5% on DeepViT-8B, and does nothing on RegNet-9B — throttling only helps when a CPU running ahead is actually causing allocator defragmentation. The paper's own advice: check num_alloc_retries first. There is no transferable configuration here. [T1]
  • Reach for tensor parallelism only when a single layer won't fit — and keep it inside one node. Megatron partitions the block so a whole layer costs two all-reduces forward and two backward (MLP: column-split the first weight so the nonlinearity stays local, row-split the second; attention: split across heads so each softmax is local). That bought 8.3B parameters on 512 GPUs at 76% scaling efficiency. Those all-reduces are latency-bound, so it stays efficient only inside a high-bandwidth island. [T1]
  • If you run expert parallelism at scale, stop tuning load balance and prove it instead. This is the newest practice on the map and the most decisive. Expert-parallel imbalance has been treated as a hazard to be smoothed with auxiliary losses; Kimi K3 reserves E/R redundant expert slots per rank and shows a balanced plan then always exists. The consequences are systems consequences rather than quality ones: training is never interrupted for rebalancing, all computation shapes become static, per-layer host synchronization disappears, and communication becomes zero-copy with a fixed S×K buffer where the standard path needs S×K×R. Measured at 2.78T parameters with 104.2B active. [T1, first-party]
  • Budget for two memory pools, not one. Model states are handled by partitioning; residual states (activations, temporaries, fragmentation) need different tools — partitioned activation checkpointing, constant-size fusion buffers, live defragmentation. The operational lesson: OOM-from-fragmentation is a distinct failure from OOM-from-size, and they have different fixes. [T1]
  • Expect "it fits now" and "it's faster now" to arrive together. ZeRO reports super-linear speedup from 64 to 400 GPUs — mechanistic, not anomalous: a smaller per-device footprint permits a larger batch, which raises arithmetic intensity. [T1]
  • Profile before you choose a combination; the right answer is a property of your deployment, not your model. The evidence for this now spans three literatures — FSDP's dial, checkpointing's budgeted search, and PipeDream's O(N²M²) dynamic program, which returned a mixture of parallelism types in all but one of its experiments and flipped its answer on the same model between a 25 Gbps and a 10 Gbps cluster. The pipeline evidence moved to pipeline-parallelism; the pattern stayed here, because it is about searching rather than about any one axis. [T1, three sources]

What moved since 2026-07-26

Sequence parallelism was ruled out for consumer hardware, and then partly un-ruled. Ring Attention's precondition is crisp and is also the bad news: c ≥ F/B, block size above the host's FLOPS-to-bandwidth ratio. The same A100 needs a 6.2K per-host sequence over NVLink and 149.5K over InfiniBand — a 24× swing from the wire alone, and a hard floor rather than graceful degradation. That made it a datacenter technique, full stop. As of 2026-08-03 there is a caveat with a real mechanism behind it: Kimi K3's KDA context parallelism observes that a fixed-size recurrent state needs only a fixed-size all-gather regardless of sequence length, so the c ≥ F/B floor may be a property of softmax attention rather than of the axis. That is one first-party report on a hybrid-attention stack, not a replication — but it reopens a question this vault had closed, and it closes in the direction that would help a small cluster. [T1, one source, unreplicated]

Expert parallelism stopped being a footnote. In July it was one row in a table sourced from Switch. It now has a production account at 2.78T parameters and a redundancy argument that converts a tuning problem into a scheduling guarantee. [T1]

The exactness ladder survived the forks and is still the most useful ranking here. Rank these levers by how honestly they compute a gradient, because only one end of the range is safe to ignore. Exact: activation recomputation — "gives equivalent weight gradient for training and can always be safely applied" — which is why it is spent first, and why it now has activation-memory to itself. Silently inexact: sharded training, which the FSDP paper says is "not guaranteed mathematically equivalent to local training," because the optimizer steps over flattened FlatParameters whose layout ignores parameter boundaries, so norm-based clipping and approximate second-order methods compute over the wrong object — no quality delta is measured anywhere, and the failure is silent. Deliberately inexact: asynchronous pipelining, where PipeDream states that without weight stashing "the weight update is not a valid gradient of the loss function f for any weight vector," and where the mechanism that would restore data-parallel equivalence ships off by default. That is a defensible engineering call, documented better than FSDP's. It is also a model trained by an optimizer nobody would write down, whose weights get compared on a leaderboard against models that were. The training system is part of the experimental configuration, and almost no result reports it. [T1, three sources]

What the numbers don't say

  • Everything here is a vendor grading its own homework. Microsoft's "10× faster than SOTA" is measured against baselines Microsoft configured; Meta's FSDP numbers are Meta's; FSDP says outright that it is ZeRO-3, so what replicates across the two is the engineering, not two independent derivations. Megatron's 76% efficiency is NVIDIA-on-NVLink. Kimi K3 is a first-party technical report on a model whose weights are released under CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0, i.e. not a licence under which a reader can straightforwardly reproduce or fine-tune it. No third party has audited any of it. [T1, first-party]
  • The one non-DeepSpeed/FSDP account is also the one nobody can run. Kimi K3 is valuable here precisely because it is the sixth first-party parallelism account and the first outside that lineage since DeepSeek-V3. It is also 2.78T parameters with MXFP4 expert weights — roughly 1.4 TB before any cache — with no single-node deployment anywhere in the report. It corroborates the engineering at a scale that makes the corroboration hard to check.
  • Absolute throughput figures expire with the GPU generation, and the direction is instructive. ZeRO's 38 TFlops/GPU is V100-era; the ratios survive because they follow from byte accounting. PipeDream measures the other side of the same effect: across five models, going K80 → Titan X → V100 raises communication's share of training time, and its advantage over data-parallel grows 2.99× → 5.12×. Compute got cheaper; the interconnect did not. Optimizations about movement get more valuable as silicon improves — the same conclusion FlashAttention reaches about HBM and ZeRO-Infinity about PCIe. [T1, three literatures]
  • Nobody has priced the practitioner-scale question: what actually fits on N cards. The in-vault numbers are 175B on 128–512 A100s, and now 2.78T on a cluster whose size the report does not put in the abstract. This vault's premise runs on far less.

The gap that matters most here

None of these sources is about fine-tuning. Every experiment is generic full-parameter training — no frozen layers, no adapters, no fine-tuning memory profile. The refreshes since July turned that from a shrug into a specific prediction, and the forks have now turned the prediction into a map boundary.

Four of the axes on the table attack terms proportional to the number of trainable parameters. A 4-bit LoRA fine-tune has almost none of those terms: 99.99% of parameters are frozen, and there is barely any fp32 optimizer state left to shard, split, or demote. So PEFT plausibly deletes most of this list — and the axis with the clearest claim to being deleted, optimizer demotion, is now a map of its own with the question stated on it. [unsourced, and a genuinely useful negative result if true]

Activations do not scale that way. The forward activations of a 70B model are the same size whether you update every weight or 0.01% of them. That is why the recomputation strand left first, and it is where the standing experiment went with it. As of 2026-08-09 the cheapest useful unrun experiment in this vault lives on activation-memory and reads:

Peak memory and step time for a 4-bit LoRA fine-tune at a realistic sequence length, across the three reachable checkpointing/offload cells and more than one granularity, plus a layer_offloading arm.

Two details from the tooling ingest of 2026-07-29 sharpen it. Activation offload is a rider on checkpointing, not an alternative — stated independently by two maintainer teams, which is why the grid has three cells rather than four. And PEFT does not only delete memory axes: layer_offloading streams frozen decoder layers, a lever on the model term that a full fine-tune cannot have, with no published number anywhere. [T2, two tool docs]

The honest summary as of 2026-08: the memory arithmetic is settled and replicated; the axes are individually well-measured; no source measures any combination of them; and the combination this vault's premise actually needs — adapters, on a quantized base, at long context, across whichever of these levers still applies — remains undocumented in every one of them. What changed since July is not the answer but the filing: the two levers that matter on one card now have maps where someone might actually measure them.
