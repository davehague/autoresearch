Select, don't collect: the state of data curation

Synthesized 2026-07-25, refreshed 2026-07-30 from data-curation — the map went contested this run, which is the trigger, and five days of drift came with it. Read the change carefully, because it is easy to get backwards: the rule in the title survives and its justification does not. Filtering still wins at every budget a reader of this vault will ever have. It wins because compute is scarce, not because bad data is bad — and one of the crossovers where that stops being true sits at 330M parameters, which is a few hundred dollars. Every number here has a date; this field's defaults rot in months.

The claim

Data curation is the half of the craft where a practitioner has the most leverage and the least published evidence. You cannot outspend a frontier lab on compute. You can out-select them on a narrow target, and as of 2026-07 there are three controlled studies at three different pipeline stages that say what selection is worth and, more usefully, what it is not worth.

The through-line is a single sentence that now holds at 15 trillion pretraining tokens, at one thousand instruction examples, and at the prompt pool a reinforcement-learning run consumes: quality and diversity are the levers, and volume saturates.

Three groups, no shared authors, no shared scale, no shared pipeline stage, and no shared methodology beyond the one thing that matters — all three trained the variants and measured, and all three released the ablations. FineWeb (Hugging Face, NeurIPS 2024 D&B), LIMA (Meta AI et al., NeurIPS 2023) and CoT-Self-Instruct (FAIR at Meta / NYU, 2025). [T1, three groups]

New on 2026-07-30, and it is why this article needed rewriting rather than extending. A Stanford scaling study — A Bitter Lesson for Data Filtering, over 20,000 H100-hours — refutes none of those three measurements and reinterprets all of them. Every filter's advantage turns out to be a crossover, not a property. Unfiltered Common Crawl starts behind aggressively filtered variants and ends ahead of them as training compute grows, across model sizes 15M / 80M / 330M / 1B / 7B, with the crossing point moving right as models get bigger. An ablation is by definition a measurement at one fixed compute budget. So every number in the paragraph above sits at one point on a curve that is now known to have a crossing on it. [T1]

Hold both of these at once, because the useful reading is the narrow one:

  • The crossover for a realistically-sized pool extrapolates to ≈1e30 FLOPs, against ≈5e26 for the largest training runs happening now. That is roughly 2,000× the frontier. Nobody reading this will operate there, and at every scale you can reach, filtering wins — this paper's own curves are the best evidence for it that exists.
  • What changes is the mechanism. A filter is not repairing defective data. It is buying sample efficiency against a compute budget too small to extract the signal directly. That is a better belief to hold, because it tells you when to expect the practice to stop paying instead of asserting that it never will.

What to do

Ablate your data choices instead of arguing about them — and record the compute budget you ablated at. The method claim underneath everything else, and the reason these sources are gradable at all. FineWeb's contribution is not a corpus, it is a corpus plus datatrove plus the proxy models that justify each filtering decision. Train identical small models on each variant and read the downstream benchmark; that is affordable at practitioner scale in a way most of this vault is not. New 2026-07-30: report the step count. Since the ranking of filters depends on how long you train, an ablation without its budget is an experimental condition passed off as a fact about the data — and no filtering paper in this vault reports where on the crossover curve its ablations sit. [T1]

Expect the intuitive move to invert — it has three times now. FineWeb found that aggressive global cross-dump deduplication hurts: it strips so much that quality drops, and per-dump dedup wins because surviving across dumps is itself a weak quality signal. LIMA found that scaling quality-filtered examples 2K → 4K → 8K → 16K → 32K leaves generation quality flat at ~3.8 on a 1–6 scale — a 16-fold increase in data buying nothing measurable. And CoT-Self-Instruct found that filtering a generated prompt pool is worth +4.2 points on a four-benchmark reasoning average (53.0 → 57.2) while shrinking the set. Three defaults inverted by controlled evidence at three stages. [T1]

Spend a fixed curation budget on filtering and prompt diversity, not on volume. LIMA separates the three variables on a 7B model with matched hyperparameters. Quality is worth +0.50: 2,000 filtered Stack Exchange examples score 3.83 against 2,000 unfiltered at 3.33. Diversity is worth +0.34: filtered Stack Exchange (heterogeneous prompts) scores 3.83 against wikiHow at 3.49 — where wikiHow's responses are equally excellent and only the prompts are homogeneous, every one a "how to" question. Holding response quality fixed, prompt heterogeneity is independently valuable. [T1]

Steal the filters; they are unglamorous and they earn half a point. LIMA's Stack Exchange rules, in full: sample at temperature τ=3 across 75 STEM and 99 other exchanges for uniform domain coverage; take top-scoring self-contained questions; require an answer score ≥10; drop answers under 1,200 or over 4,096 characters, written in the first person, or referencing other answers; strip links, images and HTML while retaining code blocks and lists. Nothing there requires a research team. [T1]

Filter synthetic data too, and generate the label with the item. The synthetic-data ablation this article called the most obvious missing study on 2026-07-25 was run two days later, and the answer agrees with everything above. Filtering a generated prompt pool beats generating more of it, and it lifts the weaker generator as well (plain Self-Instruct with reasoning-generated targets goes 49.5 → 53.6), so this is a property of the curation step and not of one pipeline. The non-obvious part is which filter wins: when a model constructs a problem and solves it in the same reasoning pass, checking a later majority vote against that construction-time answer beats the majority vote alone (57.2 vs 55.1), because the construction-time label came out of a trace that knew the intended solution. Design rule: generate the label alongside the item and keep it as an independent reference — a pipeline that labels afterwards has thrown away its cheapest verification signal. [T1]

On the pretraining side, a learned quality filter beats scale. A small classifier trained on LLM-rated "educational value" yields FineWeb-Edu at 1.3T tokens, whose models show large gains on MMLU and ARC against models trained on the unfiltered 15T. Selection over an order of magnitude more data. Caveat the filter honestly: "educational value" is an LLM-labeled proxy with its own biases, and what it systematically excludes — and whether that narrows the model — is unmeasured. [T1]

Assume alignment needs less data than you think, and structure it accordingly. LIMA's headline is the existence proof: LLaMa 65B, 1,000 curated examples (~750,000 tokens), plain supervised loss, no RLHF and no reward model. Against 300 test prompts in a human preference study it is equal-or-preferred to GPT-4 in 43% of cases, Bard in 58%, and RLHF-trained DaVinci003 in 65% — and it beats Alpaca-65B, the same base model trained on 52× more data. Its own absolute assessment is more sober than the win rates: 50% Excellent, 38% Pass, 12% outright Fail. [T1]

If you need multi-turn, 30 examples is the whole budget. Zero-shot, LIMA breaks within three turns in 6 of 10 conversations. Adding 30 hand-written dialogue chains takes excellent responses from 45.2% to 76.1% and failures from 1-per-42-turns to 1-per-46. The appendix reports that removing six examples of a task can break structured generation entirely. Treat capability coverage as a checklist with single-digit counts per item, not as a volume problem. [T1]

Before generating synthetic data, look for a channel where the ground truth already exists. This is Molmo's move and it generalizes past vision: annotators speak for 60–90 seconds per image and a language-only model that never sees the image transcribes and cleans the result (712k images, ~1.3M captions); PixMo-Docs generates charts from code and derives the Q&A from the generating code rather than the rendered image. A different modality, a reversed direction, or a human act that is cheap in the right format will often beat a teacher model. [T1]

Decontaminate on the supply side if you can, and screen on the demand side if you cannot — and know that both tools are weaker than they look. FineWeb removes benchmark n-grams before release, which is available to you for your own fine-tuning mix. For the base model you did not pretrain, Min-K% Prob recovers membership from the model alone at AUC 0.72 — a prior, not a verdict — and works markedly better on your own data (AUC 0.86 for deliberately inserted examples), which is exactly where you would use it. The asymmetry is structural: almost every model this vault's readers fine-tune is one they did not build, so the applicable tool is the weaker one. See the section below for how much weaker. [T1]

And a fourth screen, new 2026-07-30, which costs two eval runs. Memorized answers are brittle to sampling temperature and honestly-learned ones are not: in a controlled contamination sweep, scores hold flat from greedy decoding through τ ≤ 0.56 and then fall sharply, and at heavy contamination raising temperature from 0 to 1.0 costs a factor of about 40× — collapsing to roughly the performance of an uncontaminated model — while clean models barely move. Score the same benchmark at τ=0 and τ=1.0 and read the gap. No corpus, no log-probs, no reference model. Measured at 34M–344M parameters on verbatim insertions, so treat it as a prior like the others. [T1]

Why it works, as far as anyone can say

LIMA's proposed mechanism is the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis: knowledge and capabilities are learned almost entirely during pretraining, and alignment teaches only which subdistribution of response formats to use. The multi-turn result is the cleanest evidence for it — a model trained on zero dialogue examples can converse at all, which is only explicable if the ability was already present and merely needed invoking.

That hypothesis is doing real work elsewhere in the vault, and it is the most useful thing this map exports. peft-methods has been contested since 2026-07-18 over a conditional it could state but not explain: low-rank adapters match full fine-tuning on task adaptation and underperform on genuine domain acquisition, so 4-bit LoRA is the right opening move and an explicit bet that your target is a task, not a body of knowledge. The Superficial Alignment Hypothesis is that bet restated as a claim about where knowledge lives. Two findings — one about parameters, one about data — arrive from opposite directions at the same boundary, and neither paper cites the other. [T1, two groups]

It also yields a prediction nobody has tested: the targets where LoRA underperforms full fine-tuning should be the targets where curated instruction data underperforms continued pretraining. If that correlation holds, it is the a-priori test for "does this target need a high-rank update" that the vault has called its sharpest open item.

As of 2026-07-30 there is a second candidate mechanism, and it is less flattering. If filtering is sample efficiency under a compute constraint, then curation works for the same reason a good curriculum works — it front-loads the signal a limited budget can actually absorb. The two accounts are not incompatible (one is about where knowledge lives, the other about what a budget can reach) and nothing in-vault distinguishes them. The distinguishing evidence would be a fine-tuning crossover, described below.

What is contested or unresolved

The reason filtering works may be a compute constraint, and the crossover is reachable at 330M parameters. This is why the map moved down on 2026-07-30 rather than merely noting a scope limit. The headline extrapolation (≈1e30 FLOPs) is irrelevant to any reader here. The injection experiments are not. Add documents whose words have been randomly shuffled — unigram distribution preserved, syntax destroyed — to a clean Common Crawl pool, and at 330M parameters past roughly 11B training tokens the augmented model beats the clean one. At 1B, even an +800% dose of shuffled text roughly matches clean data. Randomly generated strings hurt a 15M model and stop hurting at 330M and above. That is not tolerance of junk; a gain is not something any account of filtering-as-defect-removal predicts, and the natural reading — that shuffled text still carries usable distributional information a model large enough to represent word order separately can bank — is the vault's inference and not the authors' claim. The one clean loss is first-token prediction, which is exactly what shuffling should break. Discount it properly: the primary metric is loss on three corpora, with the question-answering benchmarks in the appendix agreeing directionally but "much noisier," and this vault's own most-repeated rule is that perplexity is a training-health signal rather than a model-selection criterion — a rule that here cuts against a source the article is otherwise persuaded by. [T1]

The decisive experiment is cheap and unrun: does the crossover exist for fine-tuning data at 7B? Everything above about crossovers is pretraining, which is the stage two of this map's three sources are not in. LIMA's quantity ablation scaled 2K → 32K examples within one source under one filter and found nothing; it never ran the many-epoch, deliberately-low-quality arm — the arm that crosses over in pretraining. Fix a 7B base, fix the example count, sweep filter aggressiveness against epochs, and look for the crossing. That single grid decides whether the downgrade reaches practitioner scale or whether "filter what you have" is merely undefeated there.

Curating by generation carries a contamination mode that curating by filtering does not, and it is worse than it sounds. Asking a model for problems "of similar complexity" to public seeds is a paraphrase generator aimed at the benchmark distribution, whether or not that is anyone's intent. Measured: CodeAlpaca, a Self-Instruct-generated instruction set, contains 12.8% of HumanEval as paraphrases — alongside The Stack at 18.9%, StarCoder-Data at 15.9% and RedPajama-Data-1T at 8.53% — and the field's standard defense, n-gram overlap, scores an F1 of exactly 0.000 against paraphrase while scoring a perfect 1.000 against verbatim copies. It is working as designed and has no signal on a reworded sentence. The demonstration is not hypothetical: Llama-2 13B fine-tuned on rephrased test items takes MMLU from 54.8 to 89.9 and GSM-8K from 28.7 to 95.3, all passing decontamination clean. Two further facts, both new since this article was written. The dose saturates at one copy — in a controlled sweep, a single replica of a test set in the pretraining mix is enough to put a model below the loss that infinite clean compute would asymptote to — so a low percentage is not a safe percentage. And the reliable detector needs the training corpus, which a corpus builder has and a model consumer does not. Every synthetic-data result in this vault, including the one recommended above, reports no decontamination step at all. Operating rule: a decontamination claim without a stated method is uninformative. [T1]

Two sources disagree about where fine-tuning data should come from, and nobody has priced the choice. LLaVA manufactures a corpus with a blind teacher — language-only GPT-4 given captions and bounding boxes produced 158K multimodal instruction samples sufficient to train a working assistant. Molmo argues the whole teacher-distillation lineage costs the community its foundational knowledge and collects from humans instead, reaching the top of an 11-benchmark suite. CoT-Self-Instruct supplies the first head-to-head on one axis and it inverts the default: unfiltered synthetic prompts (53.9) beat filtered human WildChat prompts (50.7) on the same student, judges and recipe. That is about prompts rather than annotations, so Molmo's argument survives intact — and it is not at matched cost, which is the only form the question takes for a practitioner. Which brings up the number nobody reports: each of these filters costs K extra generations per candidate, or K responses scored by a 70B reward model, with no compute accounting anywhere. That missing figure is the same one the crossover framing now demands, and it makes "synthesis versus collection at equal cost" unanswerable twice over.

"More data doesn't help" is still not established in the form you need it. LIMA scaled within one source under one filter, so what it shows is that more of the same distribution saturates. The move a practitioner actually makes is adding a different distribution — which this map's own diversity result predicts would help. Note that CoT-Self-Instruct found 5k → 10k generated prompts worth 1.5 points, which looks like a contradiction and is not: LIMA scaled within one source, CoT-Self-Instruct scaled generation and then filtered, which broadens before selecting. That reconciliation is the vault's reading, not either paper's. The experiment that would settle it is cheap and unrun: hold example count fixed and vary the number of distinct sources.

Distillation and curation win on different targets, and the distinction is easy to lose. LIMA beats Alpaca-65B, a distilled set 52× its size. DeepSeek-R1 shows distilled reasoning traces beating direct RL decisively — 72.6% vs 47.0% AIME at 32B. Both are T1 and they are not in conflict: reasoning traces have a verifiable checker, so the teacher supplies something the student cannot generate and the ground truth is cheap. Alignment style has no checker, and the teacher's advantage is much thinner. Working rule, stated as a conditional because that is all the evidence supports: distill when the target has a checker; curate when it does not.

Watch the instruments, including this article's. LIMA's most load-bearing numbers — the +0.50 and +0.34 deltas — are graded by GPT-3.5-turbo on a 1–6 Likert scale, and evaluation-contamination's judge discount applies in full. Its main results run against 2023-vintage baselines (DaVinci003, Bard, Claude-v1, GPT-4-April), all since retired, so read the win rates as historical. 230 of its 300 test prompts were written by the same authors who wrote 200 training examples, and the paper concedes "significant contact between the groups" produced shared priors it does not quantify. CoT-Self-Instruct is a venue-less preprint from the lab that also authored the filter it adopts, runs one model family at one size, reports AMC23 and AIME24 on 40 and 30 problems with no confidence intervals, and judges its instruction-following results entirely with GPT-4-family models. FineWeb's ablations are validated at small proxy scale, and whether the ranking of curation choices holds at large-model training is assumed rather than shown — a caveat that reads differently now that a scaling study says the ranking depends on scale. The three studies agree, the agreement across stages is the strongest thing on this map, and none of them has been independently reproduced.

The shortest version

As of 2026-07-30: ablate, don't argue. Filter hard, diversify your prompts, filter your synthetic data too, and stop collecting. That advice is unchanged and better-supported than it was five days ago — three stages, three groups. What changed is why it works: filtering buys sample efficiency against a compute budget, so it is a technique with an expiry date rather than a fact about data quality, and one of the crossovers has been measured at 330M parameters. Two things nobody has run, and they are the two you will want next: the same crossover check on fine-tuning data at 7B, and teacher synthesis against human collection at equal cost — which stays unanswerable while nobody prices what filtering costs.
