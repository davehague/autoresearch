Not a stored matrix: the state of adapter objects & serving

Synthesized 2026-09-27 from adapter-objects-and-serving (status: consolidating), on the night it crossed ten dated through-line findings and the entire [article-due] queue. This map forked out of peft-methods on 2026-08-10: the parent argues about whether a low-rank update matches full fine-tuning; this map stopped asking that question and started asking a different one — what kind of object is "the adapter," and what does that choice cost to serve? As of 2026-09 the field has produced at least five different answers to "where does task knowledge live," a hard structural rule for which of them can be merged away for free, and a first, honest, inconclusive look at whether adapters can even share a KV cache with each other.

The founding split: mergeability is not a courtesy, it's a property of linearity

LoRA's cleanest practical trick is that W + BA can be pre-computed once and shipped as an ordinary weight matrix — the adapter disappears at inference, for zero added latency (LoRA, T1). This map exists because that trick turns out to be conditional twice over, and both conditions are structural rather than incidental.

The base format has to cooperate. Across nine quantization backends, merging is the capability that keeps breaking: AQLM cannot merge at all, torchao merges correctly only with LoRA and int8_weight_only and otherwise produces silently incorrect results, and both Intel Neural Compressor and Transformer Engine list merge/unmerge as unsupported. The reason is structural: merging means adding a full-precision low-rank product back into the base and re-encoding it losslessly, which is roughly true of int8 and NF4 and simply false of a codebook scheme where the "weight" is an index into learned vector codes. [T2]

The adapter has to be linear. An adapter path computing B·φ(Ax) has no weight-matrix equivalent, for any φ that is not the identity, at any precision — a property of the function, not of the base format, and one no quantization scheme can route around. AuroOFT (a gated nonlinear low-rank residual over a quantized orthogonal rotation) gains 1.30–2.70 Macro-6 points over matched QOFT and pays for it by becoming permanently unmergeable, on every base format, forever. [T1]

Put together: decide whether you will merge before you choose an adapter architecture. Shipping a quantized, fine-tuned checkpoint disqualifies both a nonlinear adapter and an aggressive (codebook) base format. Serving hot-swappable per-task artifacts off one frozen base makes the trade free, and turns the question into a per-tenant economics problem instead — which is most of what the rest of this map is about. The standing defect, unfixed across every source that crosses this line: not one measures the unmerged path's actual latency or memory on a consumer card. The one number this vault does have, from a different map, is a 76% higher inference latency for an unmerged adapter in one measured setting (four-bit-fine-tuning-buys-vram-with-time-and-energy) — which is the number every nonlinear-adapter paper here implicitly accepts and none of them states.

Where else can task knowledge live? Four more places, none of them a stored matrix

This map's most consequential finding is that "a small trained matrix" is one point in a much larger design space, not the only shape task knowledge can take.

An external factorized operator, read from the residual stream at each injection layer over a frozen backbone, occupies zero prompt positions and never enters the attention cache (KV-Skill, T1 — cited here via its concept note task-knowledge-has-a-third-place-to-live). Under matched ~1.3M-parameter budgets it takes 7 of 8 head-to-heads against soft prefixes, prefix tuning and LoRA, with the margin widening on a larger backbone (35B LiveMath 72.6 vs. LoRA's 52.4) while the artifact shrinks to 20 KiB against LoRA's 10 MiB. The rank analysis underneath it is sharper than the headline: a magnitude-matched random direction collapses to 26.61 (against 79.84 at full rank), so what the method extracts is one genuinely task-aligned direction per layer — not "any low-rank object helps."

A supervised residual carried into logit space, with no weight update and no prompt positions at all (Weightless Fine-Tuning, T1). A cross-prefix transport operator, estimated from dropout-induced cross-covariance on a frozen model, adds a correction directly to the logits at decode. It beats an untuned LoRA-SFT baseline on average on two 8B bases at a claimed <7% of its effective compute, and the mechanism evidence outlasts the benchmark: the induced logit shift matches SFT's own at cosine 0.875 over the top-10 tokens carrying >95% of the mass. The honest price, which the paper never states in its own numbers: the method requires dropout at inference on a frozen checkpoint, and its serving cost is an O(V)-scale operation per token, per personalized request — against a merged LoRA's exact zero. Weightless Fine-Tuning buys back training budget and spends it at decode.

A prompt, converted into a positionless operator rather than re-read every query. Prefill an authored text skill once, take the normalized residual states as the operator's factors, then train only a small interface against a KL/CE objective where teacher and student are the same weights — one with the skill in the prompt, one without. On Qwen3.5-4B this takes LiveMath from 23.4 to 77.2 using the identical words and author; on a larger model (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) the authored prompt actually scores below the unskilled base (26.6 vs. 31.2) while eating 671 prompt positions every query, and conversion recovers 46 points. Read together with the KV-cache-management map, the cost side is stark: a prompt-resident skill inflates prompts 39–65× at fixed content, re-encoded on every request, where the converted operator's overhead is flat from 128 to 8,192 tokens because it never enters the attention cache.

A hypernetwork that emits the update per input instead of storing it. Represent each expert of an MoE-style adapter as a small conditioning vector and have one shared hypernetwork generate the low-rank update row by row, per request — adapter storage becomes independent of expert count, and the adaptation becomes instance-specific rather than task-specific. Paired with a second structural move, a provider basis plus a per-user residual (a high-capacity domain basis trained once and pinned in VRAM, with each tenant training only an r ≤ 2 residual on top), the per-tenant memory term drops 16× (13.63 MB resident against 0.85 MB in flight) and vLLM throughput rises up to +51% under PCIe pressure, with gains that grow with tenant count (+9.5% → +25.4% from 128 to 512 adapters) exactly as amortizing a fixed cost predicts. Both are genuinely new points in the design space, and both share the same two holes: neither tests a quantized base, and neither measures latency or memory for the generated-weights path — which, for a vault premised on one or two consumer cards, is the number that decides whether either is usable at all. The basis-plus-residual design is also a two-party protocol: it needs someone else already serving a basis for your domain, so on one card with one user, alone, it buys nothing.

What ties all five substrates together: this map has never found a case where a non-matrix substrate wins on quality and comes with a real cost story. Every one of them is ahead on some accuracy comparison and silent on latency, memory, or serving throughput — the map's single most consistent methodological gap.

What it costs to serve more than one adapter

The map's second half asks a different question from the first: given a chosen substrate, what does it cost to serve many tenants, many skills, or many requests against it?

Adapters make a bad tool for combining skills, and a good tool for switching between them. Training one LoRA on two disjoint synthetic skills (Fauxjson, Fauxthon) maximizes exactly what it is designed to maximize — isolated per-skill knowledge, where the fused adapter beats both specialists outside an agentic harness (44/50 and 360/500) — and scores 0 of 200 on tasks that compose both skills in one trajectory, because composed work also needs the ordinary language capability specialization erased. Keeping the adapters separate and letting the agent swap between them mid-trajectory solves 116/200, against 101 for a hand-written file-extension heuristic, 64 for the untouched base model, and 59 for delegating to a subagent instead. The swap-vs-subagent comparison is the widest-reaching number in this section and the least emphasized in the source: the same task suite uses 1.3× fewer output tokens on the median success and 46.1× fewer on the median failure (797 vs. 36,731), because a subagent that cannot do the task retries inside a context the host cannot see and returns only a failure, while a swap keeps one trajectory and one visible budget. Model-driven switching has a real ceiling, though: it collapses on the hardest binding (4/50 against the 22/50 its own single-binding rate predicts), where the hand-written rule composes closer to multiplicatively — good enough to beat a heuristic, not good enough to hold a two-skill trajectory together end to end.

An adapter can be a repair mechanism instead of a task adaptation, and the return per GPU-hour is not close. Eight learned "restore" embeddings, run through one LoRA-adapted forward pass over a full KV cache before it gets compressed, then switched off for every subsequent query — a rank-8 LoRA at 0.4% of a 4B backbone's parameters, trained in about two hours on one card — recovers +35 points on RULER-4K at a 5% cache budget (38.2 → 73.2), against nine tuned LoRA task-adapter variants that land within 1–2% of each other on ordinary task quality across sixteen learning rates. The same repair trick reappears against a different lossy operator, a quantizer instead of a cache evictor: rank-2 adapters on the linear layers, 0.12% of parameters per language, trained on 256 samples in 15–20 minutes on a single L4, recover 70–83% of a non-Latin perplexity gap where a published low-rank corrector recovers 5.5%. Both repairs share the same scope limit: neither transfers to an operator it wasn't trained against (the cache repair measures 42.0 against 73.2 when trained on one evictor and run on another), so this reads as a per-operator artifact rather than a general adapter-as-repair recipe, and the quantization repair's own author reports its effect shrinking sharply with scale (28% recovery at 3B falls to 8.5% at 7B).

Whether adapters can share a KV cache with each other is now tested, and the answer is an honest "not yet." The newest arrival in this strand asks the most direct serving-economics question the map has faced: if two LoRA adapters on the same backbone serve the same shared context (a system prompt, a retrieved document), can you prefill that context's KV cache once and reuse it across adapters instead of recomputing per adapter? On a Qwen3-1.7B backbone with an extractive-QA and an arithmetic-reasoning adapter, the quality cost is small but inconsistent — GSM8K exact-match deltas of -4.6 to -0.8 points depending on token budget and training seed — and the memory saving the whole idea exists to buy mostly does not appear: peak memory drops only ~12% with two adapters, because the tested implementation copies the cached values into each adapter's branch rather than physically sharing the underlying storage. Where reuse does clearly pay off is warm-cache time-to-first-token, which improves roughly 16× at an 8K-token shared context, since the expensive prefill compute is skipped even though the storage duplication persists. The paper's own conclusion is the right one to carry forward: "neither quality equivalence nor a general boundary-selection rule is established." [T1, single author, one backbone, two adapters — a first look, not a verdict]

Open questions

  • What does an unmerged adapter actually cost on one consumer card? Every source that crosses the mergeability line — a nonlinear branch, a generated adapter, a swapped residual, a shared-prefix reuse scheme — declines to measure tokens/second or peak memory on a single GPU. One benchmark would price the entire trade this map keeps re-deriving from theory.
  • Is the external-operator substrate a real third place for task knowledge, or one paper? Its seven-of-eight matched-budget win rests on a single study whose training cost per backbone is never stated and whose cross-backbone transfer is untested.
  • Does adapter-as-repair generalize past the specific operator it was trained against? Both repair results (cache eviction, quantization) fail to transfer to a different operator instance of the same class — the strongest evidence yet that the target, not the adapter method, is doing the work.
  • Would a genuinely storage-shared implementation change the KV-prefix-reuse verdict? The tested system copies rather than shares cache storage across adapter branches; whether a system built for real sharing recovers the memory win this run's result found missing is untested.
  • Is a two-party serving protocol reachable for a single practitioner? The basis-plus-residual factorization's gains grow with tenant count and need someone else already serving a domain basis — on one card with one user it is unclear the idea applies at all.

If you are choosing today

Decide whether you will ever merge before you pick an adapter architecture — a nonlinear branch or a codebook-quantized base forecloses it permanently, not just expensively. If you will merge, LoRA on an int8/NF4 base remains the free option this map has found no exception to. If you are serving many tenants or many skills instead, the economics — not the accuracy — is where this map's actual arguments live: a shared basis plus thin per-tenant residuals beats storing full adapters per tenant, keeping adapters separate and switching beats fusing them when a trajectory needs more than one skill, and sharing a KV-cache prefix across adapters is a promising idea with no working implementation yet. Measure the unmerged path's latency on your own card before trusting any of the substrate comparisons above — none of their authors did.
