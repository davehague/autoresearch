The same editorial describes regulatory practice as ranking evidence *types* — human, then animal, then in-vitro, then in-chemico/omics, then in-silico — *"deeply embedded in legislation and guidance (e.g. REACH)"*, with animal data *"generally outweigh[ing] in vitro results."* Their word for it: "NAMs are expected to replace animal testing, yet they are positioned in a way that prevents them from doing so." (c) The same blocker at the FRONT of the pipeline, and the vault's first blind spot caught forwards. stevens-2026-disease-aop-mapping-bioassay-panel (*Environment International*, Helmholtz-funded, ⚠ abstract-only — CC BY licensed and the publisher's host refused every rung of the fetch ladder) curated 487 adverse outcome pathways to 266, mapped them onto six disease clusters, and found the three most-recurring key events are cytotoxicity (6/6 clusters), oxidative stress (5/6) and mitochondrial dysfunction (4/6) — proposing a 15-assay panel on that basis. It is the first machine-executable answer to *"why these assays and not others?"*, and frequency and independence are opposite selection criteria: the events that recur most are the ones that discriminate least. (d) The one place a loop's output met a real outside adjudicator — and it needed three rounds of repair. yu-2026-ai-engineer-certifiable-design-aip (arXiv 2608.21976, preprint, `funding_flag: [industry]`) reports a closed loop designing a 20-megawatt floating wind foundation that received Approval in Principle from the China Classification Society, at 8.1% less steel and 8.1% lower unit cost than the human benchmark.
